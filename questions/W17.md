## W17 · D1 – Acute spinal cord injury: assessment, neurogenic shock, ventilation, surgery

**1.** A 24-year-old man is admitted after a diving injury with a C6 burst fracture. He cannot move his legs or hands, but light touch and pin-prick are preserved in the perianal area; there is no voluntary anal contraction. His ASIA Impairment Scale grade is:

- A. AIS A
- B. AIS E
- C. AIS B
- D. AIS C
- E. AIS D

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Preserved S4–S5 sensation (perianal light touch and pin-prick) with no motor function below the neurological level defines a sensory-incomplete injury – AIS B. Sacral sparing is what separates an incomplete from a complete injury, and AIS B carries a much better prognosis for recovery than AIS A.

**Why not the others:**
- A – AIS A requires absent sensory and motor function in S4–S5; his perianal sensation is preserved.
- B – AIS E means normal motor and sensory function in a patient with a prior deficit.
- D – AIS C requires preserved motor function below the level (more than half of key muscles below grade 3).
- E – AIS D requires at least half of the key muscles below the level to have grade ≥ 3.

**EDIC trap:** completeness should be confirmed after spinal shock resolves (return of the bulbocavernosus reflex, usually within 24–72 h).

**Ref:** ISNCSCI (ASIA) classification; Fehlings MG, Global Spine J 2024.

</details>

**2.** A 30-year-old woman with a complete C5 injury after a road traffic collision has BP 78/40 mmHg, HR 48/min and warm peripheries 2 h after admission. Regarding her haemodynamic management:

- A. The 2024 AO Spine/Praxis guideline suggests a lower MAP limit of about 75–80 mmHg for 3–7 days. — T / F
- B. Phenylephrine is the preferred vasopressor because it does not affect heart rate. — T / F
- C. Haemorrhagic shock must be excluded before attributing hypotension to the cord injury. — T / F
- D. The 2024 guideline recommends noradrenaline as the vasopressor of choice. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – augment MAP to a lower limit of 75–80 mmHg, not actively above 90–95 mmHg, for 3–7 days (weak recommendation, very low-quality evidence).
- B (False) – pure α-agonism causes reflex bradycardia, which is dangerous when vagal tone is unopposed after cervical SCI; noradrenaline (α + β1) is a pragmatic choice.
- C (True) – in trauma, hypotension is haemorrhage until proven otherwise; bradycardia and warm peripheries do not exclude bleeding.
- D (False) – the guideline made no recommendation on the choice of vasopressor because evidence was insufficient.

**Ref:** Kwon BK, Global Spine J 2024.

</details>

**3.** A 68-year-old man trips on a stair and hits his forehead. He has transient bilateral hand burning. MRI shows no fracture but cord signal change at C4–C5 with spondylotic stenosis. Examination shows weakness of both hands (grade 2/5) with legs grade 4/5. The most likely syndrome is:

- A. Anterior cord syndrome
- B. Brown-Séquard syndrome
- C. Conus medullaris syndrome
- D. Posterior cord syndrome
- E. Central cord syndrome

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** A low-energy hyperextension injury of a spondylotic neck in an older patient, without fracture, with hands much weaker than legs, is the classic central cord syndrome. The central grey matter and the medially placed cervical fibres are most affected; prognosis is relatively good and the legs recover first.

**Why not the others:**
- A – anterior cord syndrome causes paralysis and loss of pain/temperature with preserved dorsal columns, affecting the legs at least as much as the arms.
- B – Brown-Séquard syndrome is a hemisection (usually penetrating): ipsilateral motor and proprioceptive loss, contralateral pain/temperature loss.
- C – conus medullaris lesions (T12–L2) cause saddle anaesthesia and sphincter dysfunction, not hand weakness.
- D – posterior cord syndrome causes isolated loss of proprioception and vibration with preserved power.

**EDIC trap:** the 2024 guideline includes central cord syndrome in the recommendation for decompression within 24 h.

**Ref:** Fehlings MG, Global Spine J 2024.

</details>

**4.** A 45-year-old man with a complete C4 injury is breathing spontaneously on day 1 with SpO₂ 95 % on 2 L/min oxygen, VC 1.9 L (body weight 80 kg).

- A. His vital capacity can be expected to deteriorate over the next 3–5 days. — T / F
- B. Vital capacity is expected to improve when he is nursed sitting upright. — T / F
- C. A vital capacity below about 15 mL/kg would support elective intubation. — T / F
- D. Succinylcholine on day 10 carries a risk of severe hyperkalaemia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – ascending cord oedema, fatigue and secretions make VC fall over the first 3–5 days; a patient who looks stable on day 1 may fail on day 3.
- B (False) – in tetraplegia VC is better supine; upright, the abdominal contents pull the diaphragm down and flatten it (an abdominal binder helps).
- C (True) – VC < 15 mL/kg (here < 1.2 L) or a falling trend, rising PaCO₂ and poor secretion clearance support elective intubation; a complete C4 lesion is at high risk.
- D (True) – after ~48–72 h extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors proliferate and succinylcholine can cause life-threatening hyperkalaemia; use rocuronium.

**Ref:** Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine, J Spinal Cord Med 2008.

</details>

**5.** A 40-year-old man with acute traumatic cervical SCI arrives at the trauma centre 3 h after injury. Each of the following is supported by current guidelines EXCEPT:

- A. A 48-hour infusion of high-dose methylprednisolone
- B. Surgical decompression within 24 h of injury
- C. A MAP lower limit of 75–80 mmHg for several days
- D. LMWH thromboprophylaxis started within 72 h when bleeding risk allows
- E. Serial vital capacity measurements

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** The AO Spine 2017 guideline suggests against a 48-h methylprednisolone infusion; a 24-h infusion started within 8 h is only an option (weak recommendation), because the NASCIS II benefit came from a post-hoc subgroup. Steroids increase pneumonia, sepsis, GI bleeding and hyperglycaemia.

**Why not the others:**
- B – decompression within 24 h is recommended regardless of level (Fehlings 2024; Badhiwala 2021).
- C – the 2024 guideline suggests a MAP lower limit of 75–80 mmHg for 3–7 days.
- D – SCI carries one of the highest VTE risks in the ICU; LMWH is started within 72 h once bleeding risk allows.
- E – VC falls over the first days after cervical SCI; serial measurements guide the timing of intubation.

**Ref:** Fehlings MG, Global Spine J 2017; Bracken MB, N Engl J Med 1990.

</details>

**6.** Regarding the timing of surgical decompression in acute SCI:

- A. The 2024 guideline recommends ultra-early surgery within 8 h as the standard of care. — T / F
- B. The individual-patient pooled analysis by Badhiwala et al. found that decompression within 24 h improved motor recovery at one year. — T / F
- C. The 2024 guideline excludes central cord syndrome from the recommendation for early surgery. — T / F
- D. In Badhiwala et al., the effect of delay on motor recovery was linear and constant over the first week. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – the 2024 guideline made no recommendation on "ultra-early" surgery (< 12 h) because the evidence was inconsistent; the recommendation is decompression within 24 h.
- B (True) – 1,548 patients: total motor score recovery at 1 year 23.7 vs 19.7 points with decompression < 24 h (difference 4.0, p = 0.0006).
- C (False) – early surgery is recommended regardless of level, including central cord syndrome.
- D (False) – recovery declined steeply with delay over the first 24–36 h after injury and then plateaued.

**Ref:** Badhiwala JH, Lancet Neurol 2021; Fehlings MG, Global Spine J 2024.

</details>

**7.** A man with a T4 complete SCI from 3 months ago is admitted to the ICU with pneumonia. He suddenly develops headache, facial flushing and sweating, BP 220/120 mmHg and HR 48/min. The first step is:

- A. Intravenous labetalol bolus
- B. CT head to exclude haemorrhage
- C. Intravenous atropine
- D. Sit him upright and check the urinary catheter for obstruction
- E. Start a magnesium infusion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Headache, flushing and sweating above the lesion, severe hypertension and reflex bradycardia in a patient with a lesion at or above T6 is autonomic dysreflexia. The first steps are to sit the patient upright (orthostatic fall in BP), loosen clothing and remove the trigger – a distended bladder or blocked catheter is the commonest.

**Why not the others:**
- A – drugs (short-acting nifedipine or GTN) are used only if SBP stays ≥ 150 mmHg after positioning and removal of the trigger; β-blockade can worsen the bradycardia.
- B – imaging is considered if symptoms persist or neurological signs appear, but must not delay immediate measures.
- C – the bradycardia is a baroreflex response to hypertension; atropine does not treat the cause.
- E – magnesium is not a treatment for autonomic dysreflexia.

**Ref:** Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine, J Spinal Cord Med 2008.

</details>

**9.** A 22-year-old man with a complete C5 injury, ventilated for 5 days, develops profound bradycardia (HR 28/min) during tracheal suctioning. The most appropriate response is:

- A. Insert a permanent pacemaker
- B. Atropine IV and pre-oxygenation before subsequent suctioning
- C. Start an esmolol infusion
- D. Stop all endotracheal suctioning
- E. Give adenosine

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Suction-induced bradycardia after cervical SCI results from unopposed vagal tone (loss of the T1–T4 cardiac sympathetic fibres) aggravated by hypoxia. It responds to atropine or glycopyrronium, and pre-oxygenation before each suction prevents recurrence.

**Why not the others:**
- A – most bradyarrhythmias resolve within 2–6 weeks; a permanent pacemaker is reserved for persistent, refractory cases.
- C – esmolol is a β-blocker and would worsen bradycardia.
- D – secretion clearance is vital in tetraplegia; suctioning should be made safer, not stopped.
- E – adenosine blocks AV conduction and is used for re-entrant SVT.

**EDIC trap:** aminophylline/theophylline or temporary pacing are options for refractory bradycardia.

**Ref:** Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine, J Spinal Cord Med 2008.

</details>

**11.** A 19-year-old man has a stab wound to the left side of his thoracic spine. Which finding is LEAST consistent with a Brown-Séquard (hemisection) syndrome?

- A. Left leg weakness
- B. Loss of vibration sense in the left leg
- C. Loss of pain and temperature sensation in the right leg
- D. Bilateral loss of pain sensation with preserved proprioception in both legs
- E. Preserved light touch in the right leg

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Bilateral loss of pain and temperature with preserved proprioception and vibration is an anterior cord syndrome (anterior spinal artery territory, dorsal columns spared). It does not fit a hemisection.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the corticospinal tract has already crossed, so motor weakness is ipsilateral (left).
- B – the dorsal columns cross in the medulla, so loss of vibration and proprioception is ipsilateral (left).
- C – spinothalamic fibres cross within 1–2 segments, so pain/temperature loss is contralateral (right).
- E – light touch is carried in both dorsal columns and spinothalamic tracts and is usually relatively preserved.

**Ref:** ISNCSCI (ASIA) classification; Fehlings MG, Global Spine J 2024.

</details>

**12.** In the NASCIS II trial (NEJM 1990), high-dose methylprednisolone:

- A. Did not improve outcome in the primary analysis; benefit was seen only in a post-hoc subgroup treated within 8 h
- B. Significantly reduced mortality at 1 year
- C. Was given as a 48-hour infusion
- D. Was superior to naloxone in all patients regardless of timing
- E. Reduced the rate of pneumonia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** NASCIS II (n = 487) compared methylprednisolone (30 mg/kg bolus + 5.4 mg/kg/h for 23 h), naloxone and placebo. The primary analysis was negative; motor improvement appeared only in a post-hoc subgroup treated within 8 h.

**Why not the others:**
- B – no mortality benefit was shown.
- C – the 48-h regimen was tested later, in NASCIS III.
- D – there was no overall benefit over naloxone or placebo.
- E – high-dose steroids increase infections (pneumonia, sepsis), not reduce them.

**Ref:** Bracken MB, N Engl J Med 1990; Fehlings MG, Global Spine J 2017.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 28-year-old motorcyclist has a complete C5 SCI and is intubated in the ED. On ICU admission: BP 80/45 mmHg, HR 50/min, Hb 11.5 g/dL, FAST negative. Outline your haemodynamic and respiratory management for the first week.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Exclude haemorrhage first (CT trauma series, pelvis, long bones, retroperitoneum); bradycardia does not exclude bleeding.
- Neurogenic shock: loss of sympathetic tone (lesion above T6) and of cardiac accelerator fibres → hypotension, bradycardia, warm skin.
- Fluids to euvolaemia, avoid overload (pulmonary oedema).
- Vasopressor: noradrenaline (α + β1); avoid pure α-agonists that worsen bradycardia. 2024 guideline gives no vasopressor recommendation.
- MAP lower limit 75–80 mmHg, not actively above 90–95 mmHg, for 3–7 days (weak, very low evidence; Kwon 2024).
- Bradycardia with suction/turning: pre-oxygenate, atropine/glycopyrronium; aminophylline or pacing if refractory.
- Urgent decompression within 24 h (Fehlings 2024, Badhiwala 2021).
- Ventilation: C5 complete → diaphragm partially affected, intercostals lost; secretion clearance, cough assist; plan early tracheostomy; weaning with progressive ventilator-free trials; VC better supine.
- Rocuronium instead of succinylcholine after 48–72 h.
- VTE prophylaxis (LMWH within 72 h), pressure care, ileus, bladder catheter; no routine methylprednisolone.

</details>


## W17 · D2 – ICU-acquired weakness: CIP, CIM, diaphragm dysfunction, prevention

**1.** A 63-year-old woman is awake on day 14 after septic shock and ARDS. She follows commands but cannot lift her arms against gravity; facial muscles are normal. On the MRC scale she scores 3/5 in all six muscle groups bilaterally. Which statement is correct?

- A. Her MRC sum score is 18, which defines ICU-acquired weakness
- B. Her MRC sum score is 36; she meets criteria for ICU-acquired weakness (< 48)
- C. Her MRC sum score is 36; ICU-acquired weakness requires < 24
- D. Her MRC sum score is 48, the upper limit of normal
- E. The MRC sum score cannot be used while she is still in the ICU

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Six muscle groups are tested on each side (12 in total), so 3/5 everywhere gives 36 out of 60. ICU-acquired weakness is defined as an MRC sum score < 48, and < 36 defines severe weakness, so she sits at the border of the severe range.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the score includes both sides (12 groups), so it is 36, not 18.
- C – the ICUAW threshold is < 48, not < 24.
- D – 48 is the diagnostic threshold; the maximum (normal) score is 60.
- E – the score is designed for use in the ICU once the patient is awake and cooperative (e.g. ≥ 3/5 on the S5Q).

**Ref:** Vanhorebeek I, Intensive Care Med 2020; Fan E, Am J Respir Crit Care Med 2014.

</details>

**2.** A 55-year-old man is unable to be weaned on day 18 after abdominal sepsis. Nerve conduction studies show reduced compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes with normal sensory nerve action potentials (SNAP), and direct muscle stimulation shows reduced muscle excitability.

- A. The findings are most consistent with critical illness myopathy. — T / F
- B. Distal sensory loss is expected on examination. — T / F
- C. Recovery is generally better than with critical illness polyneuropathy. — T / F
- D. A muscle biopsy is required to confirm the diagnosis before rehabilitation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – low CMAP with normal SNAP and reduced excitability on direct muscle stimulation indicate a primary myopathy.
- B (False) – sensory nerves are spared in CIM; distal sensory loss points to critical illness polyneuropathy.
- C (True) – CIM usually recovers over weeks to months; CIP/CINM recovery is slower and often incomplete.
- D (False) – muscle biopsy is not routine, and rehabilitation should not wait for it.

**Ref:** Vanhorebeek I, Intensive Care Med 2020.

</details>

**3.** A 48-year-old man, ventilated in the ICU for 10 days for severe pneumonia, is found to be weak when sedation is stopped. Which finding most strongly suggests a diagnosis OTHER than ICU-acquired weakness?

- A. Symmetrical proximal limb weakness
- B. Reduced tendon reflexes
- C. Prolonged weaning
- D. Bilateral ptosis and ophthalmoplegia
- E. Normal creatine kinase

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** ICU-acquired weakness spares facial and ocular muscles. Ptosis and ophthalmoplegia point to another diagnosis, such as myasthenia gravis, botulism or the Miller Fisher variant of Guillain–Barré syndrome.

**Why not the others:**
- A – symmetrical, proximal, flaccid weakness is the typical pattern of ICUAW.
- B – tendon reflexes are often reduced in ICUAW (especially CIP), although they may be normal.
- C – prolonged weaning is a typical consequence of ICUAW and diaphragm dysfunction.
- E – CK is usually normal or only mildly raised in ICUAW.

**Ref:** Vanhorebeek I, Intensive Care Med 2020; Fan E, Am J Respir Crit Care Med 2014.

</details>

**4.** Regarding risk factors for ICU-acquired weakness:

- A. Early supplemental parenteral nutrition in the first ICU week increased weakness in the EPaNIC subanalysis. — T / F
- B. Premorbid obesity is a strong independent risk factor. — T / F
- C. A 48-h infusion of cisatracurium in early ARDS has clearly been shown to increase ICUAW. — T / F
- D. Male sex is a recognised risk factor. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – late PN reduced weakness (34 % vs 43 %) and accelerated recovery in the EPaNIC subanalysis.
- B (False) – obesity appeared protective in cohort data.
- C (False) – a short course of cisatracurium in early ARDS (ACURASYS, ROSE) did not increase ICUAW; risk relates to prolonged infusions and the combination with corticosteroids.
- D (False) – female sex, not male sex, is the reported risk factor.

**Ref:** Hermans G, Lancet Respir Med 2013; Vanhorebeek I, Intensive Care Med 2020.

</details>

**5.** In the TEAM trial (NEJM 2022) of early active mobilisation in 750 mechanically ventilated adults, the intervention:

- A. Increased days alive and out of hospital at day 180
- B. Reduced 180-day mortality
- C. Reduced delirium duration
- D. Improved return to independent function at hospital discharge
- E. Did not change days alive and out of hospital, and increased mobilisation-related adverse events

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** In TEAM (n = 750), days alive and out of hospital at day 180 were 143 vs 145 – no difference. Mobilisation-related adverse events were more than twice as frequent with the intervention (9.2 % vs 4.1 %).

**Why not the others:**
- A – the primary outcome was not improved.
- B – 180-day mortality was 22.5 vs 19.5 % – not significant, and numerically higher with the intervention.
- C – shorter delirium duration was a finding of Schweickert 2009, not TEAM.
- D – better return to independent function at discharge (59 vs 35 %) was also the Schweickert 2009 result.

**EDIC trap:** "more and earlier" mobilisation is not automatically better – intensity must be individualised.

**Ref:** Hodgson CL (TEAM), N Engl J Med 2022; Schweickert WD, Lancet 2009.

</details>

**7.** A 58-year-old woman is in the ICU on day 5 with septic shock and is expected to need ventilation for over a week. Which intervention has the best evidence for reducing ICU-acquired weakness?

- A. High-dose protein (2.0 g/kg/day) from day 1

- C. Withholding supplemental parenteral nutrition during the first ICU week

**Why not the others:**

- B – neuromuscular electrical stimulation has inconsistent evidence and is not recommended routinely.
- D – growth hormone increased mortality in critically ill adults and is not used.
- E – deep sedation promotes immobility, delirium and weakness.

**Ref:** Hermans G, Lancet Respir Med 2013; Bels JLM (PRECISe), Lancet 2024.

**9.** In the randomised trial by Schweickert et al. (Lancet 2009), early physical and occupational therapy combined with daily sedation interruption in ventilated adults resulted in:

**12.** A 52-year-old woman, 3 days after admission with pneumonia, reports tingling in the feet followed 1 week earlier by ascending leg weakness; now she has bilateral facial weakness and areflexia. CSF protein is 1.8 g/L with 2 cells/µL. The most likely diagnosis is:

- A. Critical illness polyneuropathy
- B. Critical illness myopathy
- C. Guillain–Barré syndrome
- D. Myasthenic crisis
- E. Disuse atrophy

**7.** A 46-year-old woman on thiazide diuretics is brought in after a generalised seizure; she is now drowsy (GCS 11) and vomiting. Serum Na⁺ is 112 mmol/L. What is the most appropriate immediate treatment?

- A. Fluid restriction to 800 mL/day
- B. Tolvaptan 15 mg orally
- C. 1 L of 0.9 % saline over 1 hour
- D. Slow 3 % saline infusion aiming for a rise of 20 mmol/L in 24 hours
- E. 150 mL of 3 % NaCl over 20 minutes, repeated (up to twice) until Na⁺ rises by about 5 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Hyponatraemia with severe symptoms (seizure, reduced consciousness, vomiting) is treated with 150 mL boluses of 3 % saline over 20 minutes, checking Na⁺ between boluses, until it rises by ~5 mmol/L. The total rise should then be limited to ≤ 10 mmol/L in the first 24 h.

**Why not the others:**
- A – fluid restriction is too slow for severe symptoms.
- B – vaptans are not used for severe symptomatic hyponatraemia (unpredictable, overcorrection risk).
- C – isotonic saline raises Na⁺ slowly and unpredictably.
- D – a rise of 20 mmol/L in 24 h exceeds the safe limit and risks osmotic demyelination.

**8.** Regarding sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE):

- D. Serum S100β confirms the diagnosis. — T / F

**10.** A 50-year-old man is admitted comatose after an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. His core temperature on arrival in the ICU is 35.2 °C. According to the 2025 ERC–ESICM guidelines, what is the most appropriate temperature management?

- B. Cool him to 33 °C for 24 hours

- E. Do not actively rewarm, and actively prevent fever (> 37.5 °C) for at least 36–72 hours
