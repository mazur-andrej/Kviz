## W28 · D1 – PK changes in critical illness, augmented renal clearance, TDM (DALI, BLING III, TARGET, DOLPHIN)

**1.** A 24-year-old man with severe traumatic brain injury is on day 3 in the ICU with ventilator-associated pneumonia. Plasma creatinine is 48 µmol/L and an 8-hour urine collection shows creatinine clearance of 210 mL/min/1.73 m². He receives meropenem 1 g every 8 h as 30-min infusions. What is the most likely pharmacokinetic consequence?

- A. Meropenem accumulation with a risk of seizures
- B. Sub-therapeutic meropenem concentrations for much of the dosing interval
- C. Increased free fraction of meropenem due to hypoalbuminaemia
- D. Reduced volume of distribution of meropenem
- E. No change, as meropenem is mainly metabolised by the liver

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** He has augmented renal clearance (CrCl ≥ 130 mL/min/1.73 m²), typical of young trauma/TBI patients. Renally cleared β-lactams are eliminated faster, so concentrations fall below the MIC for part of the interval. Options are higher doses, extended or continuous infusion and TDM.

**Why not the others:**
- A – accumulation happens with reduced, not augmented, clearance.
- C – meropenem has low protein binding (~2 %); hypoalbuminaemia matters for highly bound drugs.
- D – the Vd of hydrophilic drugs usually increases in critical illness.
- E – meropenem is mainly excreted unchanged by the kidney.

**Ref:** Udy AA, Clin Pharmacokinet 2010; Roberts JA (DALI), Clin Infect Dis 2014.

</details>

**2.** Regarding pharmacokinetic principles in critically ill patients:

- A. The loading dose of a drug is determined mainly by its volume of distribution. — T / F
- B. The loading dose of vancomycin should be halved in a patient with acute kidney injury. — T / F
- C. Half-life is proportional to volume of distribution and inversely proportional to clearance. — T / F
- D. Steady state is reached after approximately 4–5 half-lives. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – loading dose = target concentration × Vd.
- B (False) – the loading dose fills the volume of distribution and is independent of clearance; the maintenance dose or interval is adjusted for AKI.
- C (True) – t½ = 0.693 × Vd / CL.
- D (True) – about 94–97 % of steady state after 4–5 half-lives; a loading dose is used to avoid this delay.

**Ref:** Roberts JA, Lancet Infect Dis 2014.

</details>

**3.** A 58-year-old woman with septic shock from a perforated colon starts piperacillin-tazobactam. Which dosing strategy is most consistent with the 2026 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines?

- A. 4.5 g as a 3-min bolus every 8 h
- B. Continuous infusion from the start, without a loading dose
- C. A loading dose given as a bolus, then prolonged (extended or continuous) infusion for maintenance
- D. A reduced first dose because her creatinine is rising
- E. Dosing guided only by trough level after the fourth dose

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** SSC 2026 makes a strong recommendation for prolonged infusion of β-lactams for maintenance after an initial bolus loading dose. The bolus reaches target concentrations immediately; the infusion maintains fT > MIC.

**Why not the others:**
- A – intermittent short infusions give peaks and long troughs below the MIC.
- B – without a loading dose it takes several half-lives to reach target.
- D – the first dose should not be reduced for AKI.
- E – waiting until the fourth dose leaves the patient under-treated early; TDM is suggested only case by case.

**Ref:** Prescott HC (SSC 2026), Crit Care Med 2026; Dulhunty JM (BLING III), JAMA 2024.

</details>

**4.** Regarding the BLING III trial of continuous versus intermittent β-lactam infusion in sepsis:

- A. Continuous infusion significantly reduced 90-day all-cause mortality in the primary analysis. — T / F
- B. Clinical cure at day 14 was higher with continuous infusion. — T / F
- C. The study drugs were piperacillin-tazobactam and meropenem. — T / F
- D. More than 7,000 critically ill patients were analysed. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – 24.9 vs 26.8 % (OR 0.91, 95 % CI 0.81–1.01; p = 0.08); not statistically significant.
- B (True) – 55.7 vs 50.0 % (OR 1.26).
- C (True) – both arms received the same daily dose.
- D (True) – 7,031 patients in the primary analysis; the accompanying meta-analysis showed RR 0.86 for 90-day mortality.

**Ref:** Dulhunty JM (BLING III), JAMA 2024.

</details>

**5.** A 66-year-old woman on phenytoin for post-craniotomy seizures has albumin 18 g/L and normal renal function. Her total phenytoin level is 7 mg/L (reference 10–20 mg/L) and she has new nystagmus and ataxia. What is the best next step?

- A. Increase the phenytoin dose by 50 %
- B. Give a reloading dose of 20 mg/kg
- C. Switch to valproate
- D. Measure the free phenytoin concentration
- E. Infuse albumin to raise the total level

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Phenytoin is ~90 % albumin-bound. With severe hypoalbuminaemia the free (active) fraction rises, so a "low" total level may hide a toxic free level, as her nystagmus and ataxia suggest. The free level (therapeutic ~1–2 mg/L) should guide dosing.

**Why not the others:**
- A – increasing the dose could worsen toxicity.
- B – a reload may cause severe toxicity.
- C – valproate is also highly protein-bound and interacts with phenytoin; it does not address the question.
- E – albumin infusion does not treat toxicity and is not a dosing strategy.

**Ref:** Roberts JA, Lancet Infect Dis 2014.

</details>

**6.** A 45-year-old man with MRSA bacteraemia receives vancomycin. Which monitoring target is recommended by the 2020 ASHP/IDSA consensus guideline?

- A. Trough 15–20 mg/L in all patients
- B. Peak concentration ≥ 40 mg/L
- C. Time above MIC of 100 %
- D. Trough below 5 mg/L
- E. AUC₂₄ of 400–600 mg·h/L, preferably estimated with Bayesian software

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Vancomycin efficacy correlates with AUC/MIC. The 2020 revised consensus recommends an AUC₂₄ of 400–600 mg·h/L (MIC 1 mg/L), ideally Bayesian-estimated from one or two levels. Trough-only targets of 15–20 mg/L increase nephrotoxicity without better efficacy.

**Why not the others:**
- A – trough-only monitoring is no longer recommended.
- B – peak levels are not the PD index for vancomycin.
- C – fT > MIC is the target for β-lactams.
- D – such a low trough would usually mean under-exposure.

**Ref:** Rybak MJ, Am J Health Syst Pharm 2020; Abdul-Aziz MH, Intensive Care Med 2020.

</details>

**7.** Regarding antimicrobial pharmacodynamic targets:

- A. Aminoglycoside efficacy correlates with the ratio of peak concentration to MIC. — T / F
- B. Once-daily aminoglycoside dosing increases nephrotoxicity compared with multiple daily dosing. — T / F
- C. β-lactam efficacy correlates mainly with peak concentration. — T / F
- D. Linezolid troughs above about 7 mg/L are associated with thrombocytopenia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – target Cmax/MIC ≥ 8–10.
- B (False) – once-daily dosing uses high peaks and the post-antibiotic effect; the drug-free trough period reduces, rather than increases, renal tubular uptake and nephrotoxicity.
- C (False) – β-lactams are time-dependent: fT > MIC.
- D (True) – target trough 2–7 mg/L; higher levels cause myelosuppression.

**Ref:** Abdul-Aziz MH, Intensive Care Med 2020.

</details>

**8.** A 52-year-old man on veno-arterial ECMO with a new circuit requires escalating doses of sedation. Which drug is most likely to be sequestered by the ECMO circuit?

- A. Fentanyl
- B. Meropenem
- C. Gentamicin
- D. Morphine
- E. Vancomycin

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Lipophilic and highly protein-bound drugs (fentanyl, midazolam, propofol, voriconazole) bind to oxygenator membranes and tubing, especially in new circuits. Higher doses may be needed and levels fall unpredictably.

**Why not the others:**
- B – meropenem is hydrophilic with low protein binding; sequestration is minimal.
- C – gentamicin is hydrophilic and poorly bound.
- D – morphine is relatively hydrophilic among opioids; sequestration is much less than with fentanyl.
- E – vancomycin is hydrophilic; ECMO changes its Vd rather than sequestering it.

**Ref:** Roberts JA, Lancet Infect Dis 2014.

</details>

**9.** All of the following are recognised risk factors for augmented renal clearance in ICU patients EXCEPT:

- A. Age under 50 years
- B. Traumatic brain injury
- C. Sepsis with a hyperdynamic circulation
- D. Male sex
- E. High SOFA score with multiple organ failure

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** ARC occurs in patients with preserved renal reserve and a hyperdynamic circulation; it is associated with lower illness severity (SOFA ≤ 4). Multi-organ failure tends to reduce renal clearance.

**Why not the others:**
- A – younger age is the strongest predictor.
- B – TBI and subarachnoid haemorrhage are classic settings.
- C – sepsis-induced high cardiac output increases renal blood flow.
- D – male sex is a risk factor in most cohorts.

**Ref:** Udy AA, Clin Pharmacokinet 2010.

</details>

**11.** A 70-year-old woman with septic shock receives midazolam by infusion. On day 4 she remains unresponsive 48 h after the infusion was stopped; her creatinine clearance is 20 mL/min. Which mechanism best explains this?

- A. Increased CYP3A4 activity caused by inflammation
- B. Accumulation of the active metabolite α-hydroxymidazolam glucuronide and reduced hepatic metabolism
- C. Redistribution of midazolam from muscle into plasma after fluid removal
- D. Conversion of midazolam to morphine-6-glucuronide
- E. Increased protein binding of midazolam

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Inflammation down-regulates CYP3A4, slowing midazolam clearance, and the active glucuronide metabolite accumulates in renal failure. Midazolam is lipophilic, so prolonged infusion also creates a large peripheral reservoir (long context-sensitive half-time).

**Why not the others:**
- A – inflammation (IL-6) reduces, not increases, CYP3A4 activity.
- C – redistribution from a peripheral reservoir prolongs effect, but the main issue here is reduced elimination of drug and metabolite.
- D – morphine-6-glucuronide is a morphine metabolite.
- E – hypoalbuminaemia lowers binding; higher binding would reduce effect.

**Ref:** Roberts JA, Lancet Infect Dis 2014.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 30-year-old man with 40 % TBSA burns develops Pseudomonas bacteraemia on day 6. Plasma creatinine is 45 µmol/L. Outline how you would dose and monitor his antibiotics.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- **PK changes:** hypermetabolic phase → ↑ cardiac output and renal blood flow → **augmented renal clearance**; ↑ Vd (oedema, fluid resuscitation, hypoalbuminaemia, losses through wounds).
- **Measure CrCl** (8–24 h urinary collection) – a low creatinine and eGFR are misleading.
- **β-lactam** (e.g. piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime or meropenem): full **loading dose as a bolus**, then **extended or continuous infusion** at the upper dose range (SSC 2026 strong recommendation); TDM if available, targeting 100 % fT > MIC (consider fT > 4× MIC) and avoiding toxic troughs.
- **Aminoglycoside** if combination is used: once-daily high dose (gentamicin 7 mg/kg or amikacin 25–30 mg/kg) by adjusted weight; peak for Cmax/MIC ≥ 8–10 and trough or nomogram level for toxicity.
- **Vancomycin** if MRSA is a concern: loading 20–35 mg/kg, AUC-guided (400–600 mg·h/L) or continuous infusion.
- **Re-evaluate daily:** clinical response, cultures and MIC, renal function (ARC can turn into AKI), source control (wound excision, line removal), de-escalation.

</details>


## W28 · D2 – Sedatives and opioids (PADIS 2025, SPICE III, MENDS2, A2B, SESAR, RSI trial)

**1.** A 19-year-old man with severe TBI has received propofol at 6 mg/kg/h for 4 days with noradrenaline. He develops lactate 8 mmol/L, CK 25,000 U/L, triglycerides 6 mmol/L and coved ST elevation in V1–V3. What is the most important immediate step?

- A. Start a dexmedetomidine bolus and continue propofol at a lower dose
- B. Give sodium bicarbonate and continue propofol
- C. Increase noradrenaline to a MAP of 90 mmHg
- D. Stop propofol and provide supportive care, including early kidney replacement therapy if needed
- E. Give intravenous dantrolene

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** This is propofol-related infusion syndrome: high dose for > 48 h, catecholamines and TBI, with lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hypertriglyceridaemia and a Brugada-like ECG. The cornerstone is stopping propofol immediately, with supportive care (haemodialysis for acidosis and hyperkalaemia, ECMO for refractory cardiac failure).

**Why not the others:**
- A – any continued propofol exposure maintains the mitochondrial toxicity.
- B – bicarbonate does not treat the cause.
- C – higher catecholamine doses are a risk factor and do not treat PRIS.
- E – dantrolene treats malignant hyperthermia, not PRIS.

**Ref:** Devlin JW (PADIS), Crit Care Med 2018.

</details>

**2.** Regarding dexmedetomidine:

- A. It is a selective α2-adrenoceptor agonist acting in the locus coeruleus. — T / F
- B. It causes clinically important respiratory depression at sedative doses. — T / F
- C. In SPICE III, early dexmedetomidine as the primary sedative reduced 90-day mortality. — T / F
- D. A rapid loading dose may cause transient hypertension. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – α2:α1 selectivity ≈ 1,600:1 (clonidine ≈ 200:1).
- B (False) – respiratory drive is preserved; it can be used in non-intubated patients.
- C (False) – 90-day mortality 29.1 % in both groups (n = 4,000), with more bradycardia and hypotension.
- D (True) – peripheral α2B-mediated vasoconstriction before central sympatholysis; loading doses are usually omitted in the ICU.

**Ref:** Shehabi Y (SPICE III), NEJM 2019.

</details>

**3.** A 63-year-old woman is ventilated for pneumococcal pneumonia. She is comfortable on fentanyl, with RASS 0 and CPOT 1. Which sedation strategy is most appropriate?

- A. Continuous midazolam infusion targeting RASS −4
- B. Continue analgesia-based sedation targeting light sedation, without adding a routine sedative infusion
- C. Add propofol to target RASS −3 to protect against self-extubation
- D. Add haloperidol to prevent delirium
- E. Start neuromuscular blockade to improve ventilator synchrony

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** She is pain-free (CPOT ≤ 2) and calm (RASS 0), which is the PADIS target. Analgesia-first sedation with light targets shortens ventilation and ICU stay; a sedative is added only for persistent agitation.

**Why not the others:**
- A – deep benzodiazepine sedation increases delirium and ventilation time.
- C – deeper sedation has no indication here.
- D – prophylactic haloperidol does not reduce delirium or mortality.
- E – NMBA is not indicated in a synchronous, calm patient.

**Ref:** Devlin JW (PADIS), Crit Care Med 2018; Lewis K, Crit Care Med 2025.

</details>

**4.** Regarding recent randomised trials of ICU sedation:

- A. In A2B, dexmedetomidine shortened time to successful extubation compared with propofol. — T / F
- B. In A2B, agitation was more frequent with α2-agonist-based sedation than with propofol. — T / F
- C. In SESAR, inhaled sevoflurane in moderate-to-severe ARDS was associated with higher 90-day mortality than propofol. — T / F
- D. In MENDS2, dexmedetomidine increased days alive without delirium or coma in sepsis compared with propofol. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – neither dexmedetomidine nor clonidine shortened time to extubation.
- B (True) – more agitation and more rescue sedation; more bradycardia with dexmedetomidine.
- C (True) – 52.9 vs 44.3 % (HR 1.31), with fewer ventilator-free days.
- D (False) – no difference in the primary outcome or in 90-day mortality.

**Ref:** Walsh TS (A2B), JAMA 2025; Jabaudon M (SESAR), JAMA 2025; Hughes CG (MENDS2), NEJM 2021.

</details>

**5.** A 72-year-old man with AKI (creatinine clearance 15 mL/min) has been on a morphine infusion for 3 days after laparotomy. He is now drowsy with pinpoint pupils and a respiratory rate of 7/min. What is the most likely explanation?

- A. Accumulation of morphine-3-glucuronide causing analgesia
- B. Accumulation of the active metabolite morphine-6-glucuronide
- C. Increased CYP3A4 metabolism of morphine
- D. Esterase deficiency
- E. Serotonin syndrome

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Morphine is glucuronidated to morphine-6-glucuronide, a potent µ-agonist that is renally excreted and accumulates in AKI, causing prolonged sedation and respiratory depression. Fentanyl or remifentanil are preferred in renal failure.

**Why not the others:**
- A – M3G has no analgesic activity; it is neuroexcitatory (myoclonus, seizures).
- C – morphine is metabolised by glucuronidation (UGT2B7), not CYP3A4.
- D – esterase metabolism applies to remifentanil.
- E – serotonin syndrome causes agitation, hyperthermia and clonus, not miosis and bradypnoea.

**Ref:** Devlin JW (PADIS), Crit Care Med 2018.

</details>

**6.** A 55-year-old man with hepatic and renal failure needs analgesia during ventilation. Which opioid has a context-sensitive half-time that remains short regardless of infusion duration and is independent of liver and kidney function?

- A. Fentanyl
- B. Morphine
- C. Remifentanil
- D. Methadone
- E. Pethidine

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Remifentanil is hydrolysed by non-specific plasma and tissue esterases; its context-sensitive half-time is ~ 3–4 min after any infusion duration, and it does not depend on hepatic or renal function. Analgesia stops quickly when the infusion stops, so plan follow-on analgesia.

**Why not the others:**
- A – fentanyl's context-sensitive half-time increases markedly after long infusions; CYP3A4-dependent.
- B – morphine's active metabolite accumulates in renal failure.
- D – methadone has a long, variable half-life.
- E – norpethidine accumulates and causes seizures.

**Ref:** Devlin JW (PADIS), Crit Care Med 2018.

</details>

**7.** A 61-year-old woman with septic shock on noradrenaline 0.2 µg/kg/min requires emergency intubation. Regarding the choice of induction agent:

- A. In the RSI trial, ketamine reduced 28-day in-hospital mortality compared with etomidate. — T / F

**Ref:** Casey JD (RSI), NEJM 2025.

</details>

**8.** A 48-year-old woman has been on fentanyl and midazolam infusions for 12 days for severe ARDS. Within 24 h of abrupt cessation she develops agitation, tachycardia, sweating, mydriasis, yawning and diarrhoea. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A. Serotonin syndrome
- B. Propofol-related infusion syndrome
- C. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
- D. Hyperactive delirium due to sepsis
- E. Iatrogenic opioid and benzodiazepine withdrawal syndrome

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Abrupt cessation after more than 5–7 days of infusion produces withdrawal: sympathetic overactivity with yawning, mydriasis, lacrimation, diarrhoea and agitation. It is prevented by tapering (10–20 % per day) or converting to enteral methadone/clonazepam, with α2-agonists as adjuncts.

**Why not the others:**
- A – serotonin syndrome requires serotonergic drugs and causes clonus and hyperreflexia.
- B – PRIS requires propofol and causes acidosis and rhabdomyolysis.
- C – NMS follows dopamine antagonists, with rigidity and hyperthermia.
- D – sepsis-associated delirium does not explain yawning, mydriasis and diarrhoea after cessation.

**Ref:** Devlin JW (PADIS), Crit Care Med 2018.

</details>

## W28 · D3 – Neuromuscular blockers and reversal (succinylcholine, rocuronium, sugammadex, ACURASYS, ROSE)

**1.** A 34-year-old man with 45 % TBSA burns sustained 10 days ago needs re-intubation for respiratory failure. Which neuromuscular blocker should be avoided?

- A. Rocuronium
- B. Cisatracurium
- C. Succinylcholine
- D. Vecuronium
- E. Atracurium

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** From ~ 24–48 h after a major burn, extrajunctional and α7 acetylcholine receptors proliferate across the muscle membrane. Succinylcholine depolarises all of them, causing a massive K⁺ efflux and possible hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest. The risk persists for months, until wounds heal.

**Why not the others:**
- A – rocuronium is the usual choice; higher doses may be needed because of resistance.
- B – cisatracurium is safe, though resistance also occurs.
- D – vecuronium is not contraindicated.
- E – atracurium is not contraindicated, though it releases histamine.

**Ref:** Fuchs-Buder T (ESAIC), Eur J Anaesthesiol 2023.

</details>

**2.** Regarding succinylcholine:

- A. It is metabolised by acetylcholinesterase in the synaptic cleft. — T / F
- B. Its effect is prolonged in patients homozygous for atypical plasma cholinesterase. — T / F
- C. It is a trigger for malignant hyperthermia. — T / F
