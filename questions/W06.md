## W06 · D1 – Hypo- and hypernatraemia: algorithm, rate of correction, ODS

**1.** A 45-year-old has a generalised seizure; Na⁺ 116 mmol/L, duration unknown. The most appropriate first treatment according to the European guideline is:

- A. 150 mL of 3 % saline IV over 20 minutes, repeated as needed with sodium checks
- B. Fluid restriction to 800 mL/day
- C. 0.9 % saline 1 L over 1 hour
- D. Tolvaptan 15 mg orally
- E. 3 % saline infusion at 0.5 mL/kg/h, aiming for +8 mmol/L in 24 h

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** A seizure is a severe symptom, and the guideline recommends 150 mL of 3 % NaCl IV over 20 min immediately, checking Na⁺ after 20 min and repeating up to twice (3 boluses in total). The aim is a rise of about 5 mmol/L in the first hour, whatever the duration.

**Why not the others:**
- B – fluid restriction raises Na⁺ by only a few mmol/L per day, which is far too slow for a patient who is seizing.
- C – 0.9 % saline raises Na⁺ by < 1 mmol/L per litre and can even lower it in SIAD.
- D – vaptans act slowly and unpredictably and are not recommended for severe symptoms.
- E – a slow infusion delays the urgent 5 mmol/L rise.

**EDIC trap:** treat a hyponatraemia of unknown duration as chronic for the correction limits, but severe symptoms still get boluses.

**Ref:** Spasovski G et al., ESICM/ESE/ERBP hyponatraemia guideline, Intensive Care Med 2014;40:320–331 (full version Eur J Endocrinol 2014;170:G1–G47); Baek SH et al. (SALSA), JAMA Intern Med 2021;181:81–92.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the diagnostic approach to hypotonic hyponatraemia (European guideline):

- A. SIAD can be diagnosed while the patient is hypovolaemic. — T / F
- B. Urine osmolality ≤ 100 mOsm/kg is typical of SIAD. — T / F
- C. Urine sodium ≤ 30 mmol/L suggests a low effective arterial blood volume. — T / F
- D. Adrenal insufficiency must be excluded before diagnosing SIAD. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – SIAD requires clinical euvolaemia. Hypovolaemia is itself a non-osmotic stimulus for ADH, so it must be excluded or corrected first.
- B (False) – urine osmolality ≤ 100 mOsm/kg means ADH is suppressed (primary polydipsia, beer potomania, low solute intake); in SIAD it is > 100 mOsm/kg.
- C (True) – urine Na⁺ ≤ 30 mmol/L reflects avid renal Na⁺ retention caused by a low effective arterial volume (extrarenal losses, heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome).
- D (True) – adrenal insufficiency (cortisol deficiency stimulates ADH), hypothyroidism, pituitary and renal insufficiency and recent diuretic use must all be excluded before SIAD is diagnosed.

**EDIC trap:** diuretics raise urine Na⁺ even when the patient is hypovolaemic, so U-Na⁺ > 30 in a patient on a thiazide does not prove SIAD.

**Ref:** Spasovski G et al., ESICM/ESE/ERBP hyponatraemia guideline, Intensive Care Med 2014;40:320–331 (full version Eur J Endocrinol 2014;170:G1–G47).

</details>

**3.** A 60-kg woman (TBW 0.5 × body weight) has Na⁺ 112 mmol/L. Using the Adrogué–Madias formula, 1 L of 3 % saline (Na⁺ 513 mmol/L) would be expected to raise serum Na⁺ by approximately:

- A. 18 mmol/L
- B. 13 mmol/L
- C. 4 mmol/L
- D. 25 mmol/L
- E. 8 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** TBW = 0.5 × 60 = 30 L. By Adrogué–Madias, ΔNa⁺ = (infusate Na⁺ + K⁺ − serum Na⁺) / (TBW + 1) = (513 + 0 − 112) / (30 + 1) = 401 / 31 ≈ **12.9 mmol/L**. One litre would exceed the 24 h limit even before any aquaresis.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 18 mmol/L would mean dividing 401 by ~22 L instead of TBW + 1 = 31 L, so it overestimates.
- C – 4 mmol/L is roughly the effect of only ~300 mL of 3 % saline (0.3 × 12.9), so it underestimates threefold.
- D – 25 mmol/L would need a TBW of only ~15 L, which nearly doubles the true effect.
- E – 8 mmol/L would need a TBW of ~49 L, i.e. a much heavier patient.

**EDIC trap:** the formula ignores urine output, so with aquaresis the real rise can be much larger. Check Na⁺ every 2–4 h.

**Ref:** Adrogué HJ, Madias NE, N Engl J Med 2000;342:1581–1589; Spasovski G et al., ESICM/ESE/ERBP hyponatraemia guideline, Intensive Care Med 2014;40:320–331 (full version Eur J Endocrinol 2014;170:G1–G47).

</details>

**4.** Regarding correction of chronic hyponatraemia:

- A. Hypokalaemia, alcoholism and malnutrition decrease the risk of osmotic demyelination. — T / F
- B. Overcorrection should be managed by continuing hypertonic saline at a lower rate. — T / F
- C. The rise in Na⁺ should not exceed 20 mmol/L in the first 24 hours. — T / F
- D. After the first day, the limit is 8 mmol/L per 24 hours until Na⁺ reaches 130 mmol/L. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – hypokalaemia, alcoholism, malnutrition, advanced liver disease and Na⁺ ≤ 105 mmol/L **increase** the risk of osmotic demyelination; in these patients aim for ≤ 8 mmol/L/24 h.
- B (False) – stop the hypertonic saline and actively re-lower Na⁺ with 10 mL/kg of electrolyte-free water (e.g. 5 % glucose) over 1 h ± desmopressin 2 µg IV (no more often than every 8 h), with close Na⁺ monitoring.
- C (False) – the limit is 10 mmol/L in the first 24 h; a 20 mmol/L rise would be dangerous overcorrection.
- D (True) – after the first day, Na⁺ may rise by ≤ 8 mmol/L in every further 24 h until it reaches 130 mmol/L.

**EDIC trap:** ODS appears 2–6 days after the overcorrection, so a well patient on day 1 does not exclude it.

**Ref:** Spasovski G et al., ESICM/ESE/ERBP hyponatraemia guideline, Intensive Care Med 2014;40:320–331 (full version Eur J Endocrinol 2014;170:G1–G47); Sterns RH, N Engl J Med 2015;372:55–65.

</details>

**5.** A 70-kg man (TBW 0.6 × body weight) has Na⁺ 155 mmol/L. His free water deficit is approximately:

- A. 3.0 L
- B. 6.0 L
- C. 1.5 L
- D. 4.5 L
- E. 7.5 L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** TBW = 0.6 × 70 = 42 L. Free water deficit = TBW × (Na⁺/140 − 1) = 42 × (155/140 − 1) = 42 × 0.107 ≈ **4.5 L**. This figure leaves out ongoing losses (insensible, urinary, GI), which must be added. Chronic hypernatraemia is traditionally lowered by ≤ 10–12 mmol/L/24 h.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 3.0 L would need a TBW of ~28 L (0.4 × weight), so it underestimates the deficit.
- B – 6.0 L would need a TBW of ~56 L (0.8 × weight), which is physiologically implausible.
- C – 1.5 L is the result of using ECF volume (0.2 × weight = 14 L) instead of total body water.
- E – 7.5 L would match a Na⁺ of ~165 mmol/L in this patient.

**Ref:** Adrogué HJ, Madias NE, Hypernatremia, N Engl J Med 2000;342:1493–1499.

</details>

**6.** Regarding the SALSA trial:

- A. It compared rapid intermittent boluses with slow continuous infusion of hypertonic saline. — T / F
- B. Re-lowering therapy was needed more often with rapid intermittent boluses. — T / F
- C. Several cases of osmotic demyelination occurred in the bolus group. — T / F
- D. Rapid intermittent bolus significantly reduced overcorrection (the primary outcome). — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – SALSA randomised 178 Korean patients with moderately severe or severe symptomatic hyponatraemia to rapid intermittent boluses (RIB) or a slow continuous infusion (SCI) of 3 % NaCl.
- B (False) – re-lowering therapy was needed **less** often with RIB (41.4 % vs 57.1 %).
- C (False) – no case of osmotic demyelination occurred in either group.
- D (False) – overcorrection, the primary outcome, did not differ significantly (17.2 % vs 24.2 %); RIB reached the early Na⁺ target more often.

**EDIC trap:** "not superior" does not mean "unsafe". SALSA supports the guideline's bolus strategy.

**Ref:** Baek SH et al., JAMA Intern Med 2021;181:81–92.

</details>

**7.** A 50-year-old man drinking large volumes of beer with poor food intake has Na⁺ 118 mmol/L, serum osmolality 250 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 60 mOsm/kg. The most likely mechanism is:

- A. Low solute intake with excess water (beer potomania)
- B. Cerebral salt wasting
- C. SIAD
- D. Primary adrenal insufficiency
- E. Pseudohyponatraemia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Serum osmolality is 250 mOsm/kg, so the hyponatraemia is hypotonic, and urine osmolality ≤ 100 mOsm/kg means ADH is suppressed. Beer contains little solute, and the kidney can excrete water only with solute: maximal water excretion ≈ daily solute load / minimal U-osm (e.g. 250 mOsm / 50 mOsm/kg ≈ 5 L/day). Beer drunk beyond that volume is retained as free water.

**Why not the others:**
- B – cerebral salt wasting causes hypovolaemia with high urine Na⁺ and concentrated urine (ADH appropriately on).
- C – SIAD requires urine osmolality > 100 mOsm/kg.
- D – primary adrenal insufficiency gives concentrated urine with high urine Na⁺, hypovolaemia and often hyperkalaemia.
- E – pseudohyponatraemia has a normal measured serum osmolality, not 250 mOsm/kg.

**EDIC trap:** once he eats again, solute intake rises and **aquaresis** follows, causing rapid overcorrection. His ODS risk is high (alcohol, malnutrition).

**Ref:** Spasovski G et al., ESICM/ESE/ERBP hyponatraemia guideline, Intensive Care Med 2014;40:320–331 (full version Eur J Endocrinol 2014;170:G1–G47); Sterns RH, N Engl J Med 2015;372:55–65.

</details>

**8.** Treatment of SIAD with moderate or profound hyponatraemia (no severe symptoms) according to the European guideline:

- A. Vasopressin receptor antagonists are recommended in profound hyponatraemia. — T / F
- B. Demeclocycline and lithium are recommended against. — T / F
- C. Fluid restriction is first-line. — T / F
- D. Urea is recommended against. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – the guideline does not recommend vasopressin receptor antagonists in moderate hyponatraemia and recommends **against** them in profound hyponatraemia (risk of overcorrection; tolvaptan is also hepatotoxic).
- B (True) – demeclocycline (nephrotoxic, unpredictable) and lithium are recommended against.
- C (True) – fluid restriction is first-line. It tends to fail when U-osm is > 500 mOsm/kg or (U-Na⁺ + U-K⁺) exceeds serum Na⁺.
- D (False) – urea 0.25–0.50 g/kg/day is a **second-line** option, as is a low-dose loop diuretic with oral NaCl.

**Ref:** Spasovski G et al., ESICM/ESE/ERBP hyponatraemia guideline, Intensive Care Med 2014;40:320–331 (full version Eur J Endocrinol 2014;170:G1–G47).

</details>

**9.** A patient with DKA has glucose 39.6 mmol/L and measured Na⁺ 125 mmol/L. Using a correction of 1.6 mmol/L per 5.6 mmol/L glucose above 5.6 mmol/L, the corrected Na⁺ is closest to:

- A. 145 mmol/L
- B. 140 mmol/L
- C. 135 mmol/L
- D. 130 mmol/L
- E. 127 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** (39.6 − 5.6) / 5.6 = 6.07 steps of 5.6 mmol/L; 6.07 × 1.6 = 9.7; 125 + 9.7 ≈ **135 mmol/L**. Glucose is an effective osmole that pulls water out of cells (translocational, hypertonic hyponatraemia). As insulin lowers the glucose, water re-enters cells and Na⁺ should rise towards the corrected value.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 145 mmol/L would need a factor of ~3.3 per 5.6 mmol/L, which overestimates.
- B – 140 mmol/L is the result with Hillier's factor of 2.4 per 5.6 mmol/L, not the 1.6 specified in the stem.
- D – 130 mmol/L adds only about half of the correction.
- E – 127 mmol/L corrects for barely one 5.6 mmol/L step.

**EDIC trap:** a corrected Na⁺ that is high-normal or rising during DKA treatment signals a free water deficit. It is not hypotonic hyponatraemia, so do not restrict water or give hypertonic saline.

**Ref:** Katz MA, N Engl J Med 1973;289:843–844; Hillier TA et al., Am J Med 1999;106:399–403.

</details>

**10.** Regarding hypernatraemia in the ICU:

- A. A rise in urine osmolality after desmopressin suggests central diabetes insipidus. — T / F
- B. Polyuria with urine osmolality < 300 mOsm/kg suggests diabetes insipidus. — T / F
- C. In a retrospective ICU cohort (Chauhan 2019), rapid correction (> 0.5 mmol/L/h) was associated with higher mortality. — T / F
- D. Acute hypernatraemia (< 48 h) can be corrected faster than chronic hypernatraemia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – in central DI the kidney still responds to ADH, so urine osmolality rises markedly after desmopressin; in nephrogenic DI the response is minimal.
- B (True) – in hypernatraemia the urine should be small in volume and maximally concentrated (> 600–800 mOsm/kg). Polyuria with U-osm < 300 mOsm/kg therefore points to DI.
- C (False) – in 449 critically ill adults, correction faster than 0.5 mmol/L/h was **not** associated with higher mortality or with cerebral oedema attributable to the correction.
- D (True) – in acute hypernatraemia (< 48 h) the brain has not yet accumulated organic osmolytes, so faster correction is safe. Chronic hypernatraemia is traditionally lowered by ≤ 10–12 mmol/L/24 h.

**Ref:** Chauhan K et al., Clin J Am Soc Nephrol 2019;14:656–663; Adrogué HJ, Madias NE, N Engl J Med 2000;342:1493–1499.

</details>

**11.** A ventilated trauma patient on day 6 receives high-protein enteral nutrition. Na⁺ has risen from 141 to 154 mmol/L over 5 days despite 2.5–3 L/day of urine. Glucose is normal and no mannitol or diuretics are given. Serum urea has risen to 22 mmol/L; urine osmolality is 720 mOsm/kg, urine Na⁺ + K⁺ = 70 mmol/L. The most likely mechanism is:

- A. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
- B. Urea-induced osmotic diuresis
- C. Central diabetes insipidus
- D. Primary sodium gain from hypertonic fluids
- E. Insensible water loss with appropriate renal water conservation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** U-osm is high and urine volume large, so total solute excretion is ~720 × 2.75 ≈ 2000 mOsm/day (usual load ~600–900). Na⁺ + K⁺ explain only 2 × 70 = 140 of the 720 mOsm/kg, so most of the osmoles are urea from protein feeding and catabolism (serum urea 22 mmol/L). Electrolyte-free water clearance = 2.75 × [1 − 70/154] ≈ **1.5 L/day** of free water lost, which drives the Na⁺ up.

**Why not the others:**
- A – nephrogenic DI gives dilute or isosthenuric urine, not 720 mOsm/kg.
- C – central DI gives hypotonic urine (often < 150–300 mOsm/kg) that concentrates after desmopressin.
- D – no hypertonic fluids were given, and the low urine electrolyte content argues against a sodium load.
- E – appropriate renal water conservation means a small volume of concentrated urine, not 2.5–3 L/day.

**EDIC trap:** use the **electrolyte-free** water clearance (urine Na⁺ + K⁺), not total free water clearance. Treat with free water and review the protein load.

**Ref:** Lindner G et al., Nephrol Dial Transplant 2012;27:962–967.

</details>

**12.** Hypernatraemia with a normal or high urine output:

- A. In central diabetes insipidus urine osmolality is inappropriately low and increases after desmopressin. — T / F
- B. In the polyuric phase of AKI urine osmolality is typically close to plasma osmolality. — T / F
- C. After high-dose furosemide, a urine osmolality close to plasma osmolality proves nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. — T / F
- D. Osmotic diuresis from mannitol or glucose produces urine with an osmolality above plasma. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – without ADH the urine stays hypotonic despite a high plasma osmolality (e.g. after brain death, TBI or pituitary surgery), and it concentrates after desmopressin.
- B (True) – recovering tubules cannot rebuild the medullary gradient, so the urine is isosthenuric (~280–320 mOsm/kg) and copious.
- C (False) – loop diuretics wash out the medullary concentration gradient, so the kidney cannot concentrate urine whatever the ADH level. Reassess after the drug effect has worn off.
- D (True) – the unreabsorbed solute drags water with it. The urine is iso- to hypertonic, but the large volume and low electrolyte content still cause a net loss of free water.

**EDIC trap:** in a hypernatraemic patient, a "normal" urine output is never appropriate. The expected response is a small volume of maximally concentrated urine.

**Ref:** Lindner G et al., Nephrol Dial Transplant 2012;27:962–967; Adrogué HJ, Madias NE, N Engl J Med 2000;342:1493–1499.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 65-year-old malnourished alcoholic woman is admitted with vomiting and a seizure; Na⁺ 108 mmol/L, duration unknown. Outline your management over the first 48 hours.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Severe symptoms (seizure, vomiting) → 150 mL 3 % NaCl IV over 20 min, check Na⁺ after 20 min, repeat up to 3 boluses; target +5 mmol/L in the first hour (regardless of acuity).
- Unknown duration → treat as chronic; she has several ODS risk factors (Na⁺ near 105, alcoholism, malnutrition, possible hypokalaemia/liver disease) → cautious limit ~8 mmol/L/24 h.
- Absolute limits: ≤ 10 mmol/L in the first 24 h, ≤ 8 mmol/L per further 24 h until Na⁺ 130.
- Frequent Na⁺ checks (every 2–4 h initially) and urine output monitoring; the Adrogué–Madias formula ignores aquaresis.
- Anticipate aquaresis once the ADH stimulus resolves (volume repletion after vomiting, nausea settling, restored solute intake).
- If overcorrected: 10 mL/kg free water (5 % glucose) over 1 h ± desmopressin 2 µg IV (not more often than every 8 h).
- Diagnostic work-up in parallel: serum/urine osmolality, urine Na⁺, volume status, exclude adrenal/thyroid insufficiency; correct K⁺.

</details>

**O2. (open)** An 80-kg elderly man after neurosurgery has Na⁺ 160 mmol/L, polyuria and urine osmolality 150 mOsm/kg. Calculate his free water deficit and outline diagnosis and treatment.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Polyuria + urine osmolality < 300 → diabetes insipidus; after neurosurgery most likely central.
- Desmopressin test: a rise in urine osmolality confirms central DI (nephrogenic causes: lithium, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia).
- TBW = 0.5 × 80 = 40 L; deficit = 40 × (160/140 − 1) ≈ 5.7 L.
- 1 L of 5 % glucose lowers Na⁺ by (0 − 160) / 41 ≈ 3.9 mmol/L; ~2.6 L is needed to lower Na⁺ by 10 mmol/L/24 h, plus ongoing (urinary) losses.
- Acute hypernatraemia (< 48 h, DI after neurosurgery) can be corrected faster; for chronic, traditionally ≤ 10–12 mmol/L/24 h.
- Chauhan (CJASN 2019): faster correction (> 0.5 mmol/L/h) was not associated with higher mortality or cerebral oedema; evidence for the limit in adults is weak.
- Frequent Na⁺ monitoring; treat with desmopressin plus free water.

</details>


## W06 · D2 – Potassium and magnesium: hyperkalaemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia

**1.** A haemodialysis patient has K⁺ 7.3 mmol/L with broad QRS complexes. The first drug to give is:

- A. Calcium chloride 10 % 10 mL IV
- B. Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate 10 g orally
- C. Furosemide 80 mg IV
- D. Nebulised salbutamol 10 mg
- E. Insulin 10 units with 25 g glucose

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** K⁺ 7.3 mmol/L with broad QRS complexes is severe hyperkalaemia (≥ 6.5 mmol/L) with ECG changes. ERC 2025 starts by protecting the heart with 10 mL 10 % CaCl₂ (≈ 6.8 mmol Ca²⁺) IV over 5 min, or 30 mL 10 % Ca gluconate. Calcium stabilises the myocardial membrane within minutes and lasts ~30–60 min, but it does not lower K⁺. Shifting therapies follow, then urgent dialysis, which is the definitive removal in this patient.

**Why not the others:**
- B – SZC removes K⁺ through the gut over hours, so it gives no immediate protection to the myocardium.
- C – furosemide needs residual renal function, which a haemodialysis patient usually lacks.
- D – salbutamol is an adjunct that shifts K⁺; it does not stabilise the membrane, and many dialysis patients respond poorly.
- E – insulin–glucose is the fastest K⁺-lowering drug (onset ~15–30 min), but with ECG changes it comes after calcium.

**Ref:** ERC Guidelines 2025: Special Circumstances in Resuscitation, Resuscitation 2025;215(Suppl 1):110753.

</details>

**2.** Regarding insulin–glucose for hyperkalaemia (ERC 2025):

- A. If pre-treatment glucose is < 7 mmol/L, a 10 % glucose infusion at 50 mL/h for 5 hours is suggested. — T / F
- B. Nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg can be added. — T / F
- C. Insulin removes potassium from the body. — T / F
- D. The dose is 25 units of soluble insulin with 10 g glucose IV. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – ERC 2025: if pre-treatment glucose is < 7 mmol/L, follow the bolus with 10 % glucose at 50 mL/h for 5 h (25 g) to prevent delayed hypoglycaemia.
- B (True) – nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg adds to the effect of insulin but is not sufficient on its own.
- C (False) – insulin stimulates Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and only **shifts** K⁺ into cells. Total body K⁺ is unchanged, so K⁺ rebounds unless it is removed (SZC, diuretic, dialysis).
- D (False) – the dose is 10 units of soluble insulin with **25 g** glucose IV (= 50 mL 50 %, 125 mL 20 % or 250 mL 10 %).

**EDIC trap:** hypoglycaemia is the most common complication, especially with low baseline glucose and renal failure (slower insulin clearance). Monitor glucose for several hours.

**Ref:** ERC Guidelines 2025: Special Circumstances in Resuscitation, Resuscitation 2025;215(Suppl 1):110753.

</details>

**3.** You need to give 25 g of glucose and only 20 % glucose is available. The required volume is:

- A. 250 mL
- B. 50 mL
- C. 100 mL
- D. 200 mL
- E. 125 mL

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** 20 % glucose = 20 g per 100 mL = 0.2 g/mL, so 25 g / 0.2 g/mL = **125 mL**. The same 25 g dose is 50 mL of 50 % or 250 mL of 10 % glucose. This is the glucose given with 10 units of insulin for hyperkalaemia.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 250 mL is the volume needed with 10 % glucose.
- B – 50 mL is the volume needed with 50 % glucose.
- C – 100 mL of 20 % glucose provides only 20 g.
- D – 200 mL of 20 % glucose provides 40 g.

**EDIC trap:** 50 % glucose is very irritant to veins. Diluted solutions are kinder to peripheral veins but add volume.

**Ref:** ERC Guidelines 2025: Special Circumstances in Resuscitation, Resuscitation 2025;215(Suppl 1):110753.

</details>

**4.** Regarding calcium in hyperkalaemia:

- A. In hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest, calcium and sodium bicarbonate should not be given together through the same line without flushing. — T / F
- B. 10 mL of 10 % calcium gluconate contains roughly three times more calcium than 10 mL of 10 % calcium chloride. — T / F
- C. Calcium lowers the serum potassium concentration. — T / F
- D. Its membrane-stabilising effect lasts about 24 hours, so a single dose suffices. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – calcium and bicarbonate precipitate as CaCO₃, so give them through separate lines or flush in between. In hyperkalaemic arrest ERC 2025 recommends 10 mL 10 % CaCl₂ plus 50 mmol NaHCO₃.
- B (False) – the reverse is true: 10 mL 10 % CaCl₂ contains ≈ 6.8 mmol Ca²⁺ and 10 mL 10 % gluconate ≈ 2.2 mmol, so ~30 mL of gluconate is needed for an equivalent dose.
- C (False) – calcium restores the gap between resting and threshold membrane potential and leaves the serum K⁺ unchanged.
- D (False) – the effect lasts only ~30–60 min. Repeat the dose if ECG changes persist, and always add shifting and removal therapy.

**EDIC trap:** CaCl₂ is more irritant on extravasation, so give it through a large or central vein.

**Ref:** ERC Guidelines 2025: Special Circumstances in Resuscitation, Resuscitation 2025;215(Suppl 1):110753.

</details>

**5.** A patient on furosemide and long-term omeprazole has K⁺ 2.9 mmol/L that does not improve despite 3 days of IV KCl. The most likely contributing factor is:

- A. Metabolic acidosis
- B. Hypercalcaemia
- C. Hypophosphataemia
- D. Hyponatraemia
- E. Hypomagnesaemia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Loop diuretics (renal Mg²⁺ loss) and long-term PPIs (impaired intestinal absorption via TRPM6/7) both cause magnesium deficiency. Low intracellular Mg²⁺ releases its normal block of the ROMK channels in the collecting duct, so the kidney keeps wasting K⁺. KCl stays ineffective until Mg²⁺ is replaced (e.g. MgSO₄ 2 g ≈ 8 mmol IV, then an infusion).

**Why not the others:**
- A – metabolic acidosis shifts K⁺ out of cells and raises serum K⁺; furosemide usually causes alkalosis.
- B – hypercalcaemia is not a recognised cause of KCl-refractory hypokalaemia in this setting, and loop diuretics tend to lower calcium.
- C – hypophosphataemia often coexists (refeeding, alkalosis) but does not block K⁺ repletion.
- D – hyponatraemia does not cause renal K⁺ wasting.

**EDIC trap:** a normal serum Mg²⁺ does not exclude depletion (< 1 % is in serum), so replace empirically if hypokalaemia is refractory.

**Ref:** Gennari FJ, N Engl J Med 1998;339:451–458; Huang CL, Kuo E, J Am Soc Nephrol 2007;18:2649–2652.

</details>

**6.** ECG features of hypokalaemia include:

- A. ST-segment depression — T / F
- B. U waves — T / F
- C. T-wave flattening or inversion — T / F
- D. Prolonged QT(U) interval with risk of torsades de pointes — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – ST-segment depression is a typical repolarisation change of hypokalaemia.
- B (True) – U waves (most prominent in V2–V3) reflect delayed repolarisation and grow as K⁺ falls.
- C (True) – the T wave flattens or inverts, and it may merge with the U wave.
- D (True) – the QT(U) interval lengthens and predisposes to torsades de pointes, especially with hypomagnesaemia, digoxin or QT-prolonging drugs.

**EDIC trap:** peaked T waves, a prolonged PR and a broad QRS point to **hyperkalaemia**. Correlation of the ECG with the K⁺ level is poor in both directions.

**Ref:** Gennari FJ, N Engl J Med 1998;339:451–458; ERC Guidelines 2025: Special Circumstances in Resuscitation, Resuscitation 2025;215(Suppl 1):110753.

**7.** In cardiac arrest attributed to severe hypokalaemia, ERC 2025 suggests:

- A. Potassium is contraindicated during CPR
- B. KCl 100 mmol as a bolus
- C. KCl 40 mmol over 1 hour
- D. Oral potassium after ROSC only
- E. KCl 20 mmol IV over 2–3 minutes, followed by 10 mmol over 2 minutes

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** ERC 2025 recommends KCl **20 mmol IV/IO over 2–3 min, then 10 mmol over 2 min** for hypokalaemic cardiac arrest, followed by an infusion adjusted to repeated K⁺ measurements, with any magnesium deficit corrected at the same time. A rapid potassium bolus is justified only during arrest, where the rhythm is already lethal. Outside arrest, peripheral rates are kept to ~10 mmol/h.

**Why not the others:**
- A – severe hypokalaemia is a reversible cause of arrest (one of the "4 Hs"), and correcting it is part of ALS.
- B – a 100 mmol bolus would cause fatal hyperkalaemia.
- C – 40 mmol over 1 h is too slow during arrest; it is a monitored central-line replacement rate.

**8.** Recognised causes of hyperkalaemia include:

**9.** A patient with torsades de pointes receives 2 g of magnesium sulphate (MgSO₄·7H₂O). This corresponds to approximately:

- B. 8 mmol Mg²⁺

- D. 20 mmol Mg²⁺

- D – 20 mmol is about 5 g of MgSO₄·7H₂O.

**10.** Regarding magnesium:

- A. A normal serum magnesium reliably excludes total body magnesium deficiency. — T / F
- B. Severe hypermagnesaemia can cause loss of deep tendon reflexes and respiratory depression. — T / F
- C. Hypomagnesaemia may cause hypocalcaemia by impairing PTH secretion and action. — T / F
- D. Long-term proton pump inhibitor use can cause hypomagnesaemia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

**O1. (open)** A patient with AKI on an ACE inhibitor and trimethoprim has K⁺ 7.0 mmol/L with peaked T waves and a widened QRS. Outline your management step by step, including doses, and explain the role of each drug.

**O2. (open)** Explain why hypokalaemia may be refractory to potassium replacement in an ICU patient and how you would correct it safely.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- ~98 % of K⁺ is intracellular, so a small fall in serum K⁺ reflects a large total deficit (hundreds of mmol).

**1.** Total calcium 1.84 mmol/L, albumin 22 g/L. Using the formula Ca + 0.02 × (40 − albumin), the corrected calcium is:

- E. 2.48 mmol/L

**A.**

**Why A:** Corrected Ca = 1.84 + 0.02 × (40 − 22) = 1.84 + 0.02 × 18 = 1.84 + 0.36 = **2.20 mmol/L**. The value looks normal, but in critically ill patients the formula agrees poorly with ionised calcium because of pH changes, complexing anions and hypoalbuminaemia. Measure iCa²⁺ directly.

- B – 1.98 adds only 0.14, as if the albumin deficit were 7 g/L instead of 18 g/L.
- C – 2.10 adds only 0.26 (an arithmetic slip).
- D – 2.36 comes from adding 0.36 to 2.00 instead of to the measured 1.84.
- E – 2.48 would need an albumin of only 8 g/L.

**2.** Regarding ionised calcium:

- A. Alkalosis decreases ionised calcium. — T / F
- B. Albumin-corrected calcium accurately predicts ionised calcium in critically ill patients. — T / F
- C. A total/ionised calcium ratio > 2.5 during citrate CRRT suggests citrate accumulation. — T / F
- D. About 90 % of plasma calcium is ionised. — T / F

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – alkalosis increases the negative charge on albumin, so more Ca²⁺ is bound and iCa²⁺ falls (hyperventilation tetany). Acidosis has the opposite effect.
- B (False) – albumin-corrected calcium agrees poorly with iCa²⁺ in the ICU (altered pH, citrate/lactate/phosphate complexes, hypoalbuminaemia), so measure iCa²⁺.

**3.** In the refeeding syndrome trial by Doig et al. (2015), protocolised caloric restriction in critically ill adults with refeeding hypophosphataemia:

- A. Increased mortality
- B. Consisted of stopping all nutrition for 5 days
- C. Was tested only in patients with anorexia nervosa
- D. Significantly increased the primary outcome of days alive after ICU discharge
- E. Did not significantly change the primary outcome but improved 60-day survival

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** The trial enrolled 339 critically ill adults (13 ICUs, Australia/New Zealand) with refeeding hypophosphataemia. Restriction meant 20 kcal/h for ≥ 2 days, then a gradual return to target over 2–3 days. The primary outcome (days alive after ICU discharge) improved by 4.9 days, which was not significant (p = 0.19). Survival at day 60 was higher (~91 % vs ~79 %, p = 0.002).

- B – energy was reduced to 20 kcal/h, not stopped, and only for ≥ 48 h.

- D – the primary outcome did not reach significance.

**4.** Consequences of severe hypophosphataemia include:

- A. Hypercalcaemia — T / F
- B. Respiratory muscle weakness and difficult weaning — T / F
- C. Haemolysis and rhabdomyolysis — T / F
- D. Right shift of the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve due to increased 2,3-DPG — T / F

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – hypophosphataemia does not raise calcium. Rapid IV phosphate can instead **lower** iCa²⁺ through Ca–P precipitation.

**5.** A patient with metastatic lung cancer is confused and dehydrated; total calcium 3.9 mmol/L, creatinine mildly raised. The most appropriate initial management is:

- A. Hydrocortisone as first-line therapy
- B. Furosemide alone
- C. Oral calcium restriction only
- D. Calcium gluconate
- E. IV isotonic fluid plus an IV bisphosphonate

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Total Ca 3.9 mmol/L (≥ 3.5 = severe) with confusion in a patient with lung cancer is most likely PTHrP-mediated hypercalcaemia of malignancy. Hypercalcaemia causes nephrogenic DI, and the resulting volume depletion lowers GFR and calcium excretion, so the first step is IV isotonic fluid. An IV bisphosphonate (e.g. zoledronic acid 4 mg) is given at the same time and acts in 2–4 days; calcitonin can bridge the first 48 h.

**Why not the others:**
- A – glucocorticoids work in calcitriol-mediated disease (lymphoma, granulomas, vitamin D intoxication), not in PTHrP-mediated hypercalcaemia from solid tumours.
- B – furosemide alone worsens the dehydration; use it only after volume repletion if the patient is fluid overloaded.
- C – restricting oral calcium does little when bone resorption drives the hypercalcaemia.
- D – calcium gluconate would make the hypercalcaemia worse.

**EDIC trap:** if renal failure limits bisphosphonate use, denosumab is the alternative (watch for hypocalcaemia).

**Ref:** Walker MD, Shane E, JAMA 2022;328:1624–1636; El-Hajj Fuleihan G et al., J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2023;108:507–528.

**6.** Regarding refeeding syndrome:

- A. Thiamine should be withheld until feeding has reached the energy target. — T / F
- B. Insulin release drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium out of cells. — T / F
- C. ESPEN 2019 recommends continuing full energy targets despite refeeding hypophosphataemia. — T / F

**1.** A 62-year-old woman with chronic thiazide-induced hyponatraemia (Na⁺ 112 mmol/L) received 3 % saline boluses for confusion. After stopping the thiazide, urine output is 400 mL/h and Na⁺ is 124 mmol/L at 12 hours. The best next step is:

- C. 5 mmol/L
- D. 0.8 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** ΔNa⁺ = (154 + 0 − 120) / (42 + 1) = 34 / 43 ≈ **0.8 mmol/L**. Isotonic saline hardly raises Na⁺ at all. In SIAD, if urine osmolality exceeds that of saline (~308 mOsm/kg), the infused Na⁺ is excreted in a smaller volume and the water is retained, so serum Na⁺ can even fall ("desalination").

**Why not the others:**
- A – 12 mmol/L is more than even 1 L of 3 % saline would achieve here: (513 − 120) / 43 ≈ 9.1.
- B – 3.4 is a decimal slip (34 / 10).
- C – 5 mmol/L is the first-hour target for 3 % boluses in severe symptoms, not the effect of 0.9 % saline.
- E – 8 mmol/L is the daily correction ceiling for high-risk patients, not the effect per litre.

**Ref:** Adrogué HJ, Madias NE, N Engl J Med 2000;342:1581–1589; Spasovski G et al., Intensive Care Med 2014;40:320–331.

**4.** Regarding emergency treatment of hyperkalaemia:

- A. Hypoglycaemia is a common complication of insulin–glucose therapy. — T / F
- B. Dialysis has no role in refractory severe hyperkalaemia. — T / F
- C. Calcium is given to lower the serum potassium when ECG changes are present. — T / F
- D. Salbutamol alone is sufficient treatment for severe hyperkalaemia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – hypoglycaemia is the most common complication, especially with low baseline glucose or renal failure. ERC 2025 adds a 10 % glucose infusion if pre-treatment glucose is < 7 mmol/L.
- B (False) – dialysis is the definitive treatment to remove K⁺ in refractory severe hyperkalaemia, particularly with renal failure.
- C (False) – calcium stabilises the myocardial membrane but does not change the serum K⁺.
- D (False) – salbutamol 10–20 mg nebulised is an adjunct to insulin–glucose. It is insufficient alone, and some patients do not respond.

**Ref:** ERC Guidelines 2025: Special Circumstances in Resuscitation, Resuscitation 2025;215(Suppl 1):110753.

**6.** Regarding hypokalaemia:

- A. Hypomagnesaemia causes renal potassium wasting. — T / F
- B. A small fall in serum potassium reflects only a small total body deficit. — T / F
- C. IV potassium should preferably be diluted in 5 % glucose. — T / F
- D. Metabolic alkalosis shifts potassium into cells. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – Mg²⁺ deficiency releases the inhibition of ROMK channels, so renal K⁺ wasting continues until magnesium is replaced.
- B (False) – ~98 % of K⁺ is intracellular, so even a 1 mmol/L fall in serum K⁺ reflects a deficit of the order of 200–400 mmol.
- C (False) – glucose stimulates insulin, which shifts K⁺ into cells and can deepen the hypokalaemia; dilute KCl in 0.9 % saline.
- D (True) – in alkalosis H⁺ leaves cells in exchange for K⁺, and bicarbonaturia increases renal K⁺ loss.

**7.** An 85-year-old woman, 50 kg (TBW 0.45 × weight), has Na⁺ 168 mmol/L. The free water deficit is approximately:

- A. 7.0 L
- B. 4.5 L
- C. 2.5 L
- D. 5.5 L
- E. 3.5 L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** TBW = 0.45 × 50 = 22.5 L. Deficit = 22.5 × (168/140 − 1) = 22.5 × 0.2 = **4.5 L**, to which ongoing losses must be added. At ≤ 10–12 mmol/L/24 h, correcting chronic hypernatraemia from 168 to ~145 mmol/L takes about 2–3 days.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 7.0 L would need a TBW of 35 L (0.7 × weight), far too high for an elderly woman.
- C – 2.5 L would match a Na⁺ of only ~155 mmol/L.
- D – 5.5 L assumes a TBW of 0.55 × weight.
- E – 3.5 L would need a TBW of ~17.5 L (0.35 × weight), which underestimates the deficit.

**EDIC trap:** use the TBW fraction for age and sex: 0.6 young men, 0.5 young women and elderly men, 0.45 elderly women.

**Ref:** Adrogué HJ, Madias NE, Hypernatremia, N Engl J Med 2000;342:1493–1499.

**9.** A malnourished patient with alcohol use disorder starts parenteral nutrition. On day 3 he fails a spontaneous breathing trial; phosphate is 0.25 mmol/L. The most likely explanation is:

- A. Refeeding hypophosphataemia causing respiratory muscle weakness
- B. Critical illness polyneuropathy
- C. Hypermagnesaemia
- D. Hypercalcaemia
- E. Metabolic alkalosis from parenteral nutrition

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Malnutrition and alcohol misuse put him at high risk of refeeding. The glucose in parenteral nutrition triggers insulin release, which shifts phosphate into cells; a Pi of 0.25 mmol/L (< 0.32) is severe. ATP and 2,3-DPG depletion weakens the diaphragm, which explains the failed SBT. Treat with IV phosphate, restrict energy for 48 h (ESPEN), give thiamine and replace K⁺ and Mg²⁺.

**Why not the others:**
- B – critical illness polyneuropathy follows prolonged sepsis or multiorgan failure and does not explain a Pi of 0.25 mmol/L on day 3.
- C – refeeding causes **hypo**magnesaemia; hypermagnesaemia needs renal failure or iatrogenic loading.
- D – hypercalcaemia is not a feature of refeeding, and IV phosphate can even lower calcium.
- E – metabolic alkalosis from PN is uncommon and does not explain severe hypophosphataemia.

**Ref:** Singer P et al., ESPEN ICU guideline, Clin Nutr 2019;38:48–79; da Silva JSV et al., Nutr Clin Pract 2020;35:178–195.

**11.** A 76-year-old man, 80 kg (TBW 40 L), has Na⁺ 160 mmol/L. The expected effect of 1 L of 5 % glucose on serum Na⁺ (Adrogué–Madias) is:

- A. −2.0 mmol/L
- B. −6.0 mmol/L
- C. −10 mmol/L
- D. −3.9 mmol/L
- E. −1.0 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** ΔNa⁺ = (0 − 160) / (40 + 1) = −160 / 41 ≈ **−3.9 mmol/L** per litre of 5 % glucose. To lower Na⁺ by 10 mmol/L in 24 h, about 10 / 3.9 ≈ 2.6 L is needed, plus replacement of ongoing (insensible, urinary) losses.

**Why not the others:**
- A – −2.0 mmol/L is the predicted effect of 1 L of 0.45 % saline: (77 − 160) / 41 ≈ −2.0.
- B – −6.0 mmol/L would need a TBW of only ~26 L.
- C – −10 mmol/L is the daily target, not the effect of one litre.
- E – −1.0 mmol/L underestimates the effect about fourfold; such small changes come from near-isotonic fluids, e.g. Hartmann's (130 − 160) / 41 ≈ −0.7.

**EDIC trap:** the formula ignores urine output, so check Na⁺ frequently, especially with DI or osmotic diuresis.

**Ref:** Adrogué HJ, Madias NE, N Engl J Med 2000;342:1493–1499.

**13.** A patient with essential thrombocythaemia (platelets 1100 × 10⁹/L) has serum K⁺ 6.4 mmol/L, a normal ECG and normal renal function. The best next step is:

- A. Arrange urgent dialysis
- B. Start insulin–glucose
- C. Measure potassium in a heparinised whole-blood sample (blood gas analyser)
- D. Give sodium bicarbonate
- E. Give calcium chloride immediately

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Platelets release K⁺ while blood clots in a serum tube, so extreme thrombocytosis produces **pseudohyperkalaemia**. A normal ECG and normal renal function support this. Measure K⁺ in heparinised whole blood (blood gas analyser) or plasma; a serum–plasma difference of > ~0.4 mmol/L confirms it.

**Why not the others:**
- A – dialysis is for confirmed, refractory hyperkalaemia, not an unverified result.
- B – insulin–glucose for an artefactual value risks hypoglycaemia and true hypokalaemia.
- D – bicarbonate is not first-line even for true hyperkalaemia outside acidosis or arrest.
- E – calcium is indicated for severe hyperkalaemia with ECG changes; the ECG here is normal and 6.4 mmol/L is moderate.

**EDIC trap:** extreme leucocytosis (e.g. CLL), haemolysis and fist clenching cause the same artefact.

**15.** Total calcium 2.00 mmol/L, albumin 25 g/L, ionised calcium 0.92 mmol/L in a septic patient. Which statement is correct?

- A. Calcium must be given routinely to all septic patients with this result
- B. Ionised calcium is not affected by pH
- C. Corrected calcium is 2.30 mmol/L, so calcium status is normal
- D. Corrected calcium is 1.70 mmol/L
- E. Corrected calcium is 2.30 mmol/L, but the ionised value shows true hypocalcaemia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Corrected Ca = 2.00 + 0.02 × (40 − 25) = 2.00 + 0.30 = 2.30 mmol/L, which looks normal. The measured iCa²⁺ of 0.92 mmol/L (normal ~1.1–1.3) shows true hypocalcaemia. The albumin formula performs poorly in critically ill patients, so the ionised value decides.

**Why not the others:**
- A – routine supplementation of asymptomatic mild hypocalcaemia in sepsis has no proven benefit; treat symptomatic or severe cases or specific settings (massive transfusion, hyperkalaemia).
- B – pH changes iCa²⁺: alkalosis lowers it and acidosis raises it.
- C – the arithmetic is right but the interpretation is wrong, because iCa²⁺ is low.
- D – 1.70 subtracts the correction instead of adding it.

**Ref:** Aberegg SK, Ionized calcium in the ICU, Chest 2016;149:846–855.
