## W06 · D1 – Hypo- and hypernatraemia: algorithm, rate of correction, ODS

**1.** A 45-year-old has a generalised seizure; Na⁺ 116 mmol/L, duration unknown. The most appropriate first treatment according to the European guideline is:

- A. 150 mL of 3 % saline IV over 20 minutes, repeated as needed with sodium checks
- B. Fluid restriction to 800 mL/day
- C. 0.9 % saline 1 L over 1 hour
- D. Tolvaptan 15 mg orally
- E. 3 % saline infusion at 0.5 mL/kg/h, aiming for +8 mmol/L in 24 h

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Severe symptoms → bolus of 3 % NaCl, target +5 mmol/L in the first hour, regardless of acuity.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the diagnostic approach to hypotonic hyponatraemia (European guideline):

- A. SIAD can be diagnosed while the patient is hypovolaemic. — T / F
- B. Urine osmolality ≤ 100 mOsm/kg is typical of SIAD. — T / F
- C. Urine sodium ≤ 30 mmol/L suggests a low effective arterial blood volume. — T / F
- D. Adrenal insufficiency must be excluded before diagnosing SIAD. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – T.** SIAD = euvolaemia (A). Urine osmolality ≤ 100 mOsm/kg suggests excess water or low solute intake; in SIAD it is > 100 (B).

</details>

**3.** A 60-kg woman (TBW 0.5 × body weight) has Na⁺ 112 mmol/L. Using the Adrogué–Madias formula, 1 L of 3 % saline (Na⁺ 513 mmol/L) would be expected to raise serum Na⁺ by approximately:

- A. 18 mmol/L
- B. 13 mmol/L
- C. 4 mmol/L
- D. 25 mmol/L
- E. 8 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** TBW = 30 L; (513 − 112) / (30 + 1) = 401 / 31 ≈ **12.9** → 1 L would exceed the 24-h limit, even without aquaresis.

</details>

**4.** Regarding correction of chronic hyponatraemia:

- A. Hypokalaemia, alcoholism and malnutrition decrease the risk of osmotic demyelination. — T / F
- B. Overcorrection should be managed by continuing hypertonic saline at a lower rate. — T / F
- C. The rise in Na⁺ should not exceed 20 mmol/L in the first 24 hours. — T / F
- D. After the first day, the limit is 8 mmol/L per 24 hours until Na⁺ reaches 130 mmol/L. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.** These factors increase the risk (A). Stop; free water 10 mL/kg over 1 h ± desmopressin 2 µg (B). The limit is 10 mmol/L in the first 24 h (C).

</details>

**5.** A 70-kg man (TBW 0.6 × body weight) has Na⁺ 155 mmol/L. His free water deficit is approximately:

- A. 3.0 L
- B. 6.0 L
- C. 1.5 L
- D. 4.5 L
- E. 7.5 L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.** TBW 42 L × (155/140 − 1) = 42 × 0.107 = **4.5 L**.

</details>

**6.** Regarding the SALSA trial:

- A. It compared rapid intermittent boluses with slow continuous infusion of hypertonic saline. — T / F
- B. Re-lowering therapy was needed more often with rapid intermittent boluses. — T / F
- C. Several cases of osmotic demyelination occurred in the bolus group. — T / F
- D. Rapid intermittent bolus significantly reduced overcorrection (the primary outcome). — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.** D: 17.2 vs 24.2 %, NS; B: less often, 41.4 vs 57.1 %; C: no ODS.

</details>

**7.** A 50-year-old man drinking large volumes of beer with poor food intake has Na⁺ 118 mmol/L, serum osmolality 250 mOsm/kg, urine osmolality 60 mOsm/kg. The most likely mechanism is:

- A. Low solute intake with excess water (beer potomania)
- B. Cerebral salt wasting
- C. SIAD
- D. Primary adrenal insufficiency
- E. Pseudohyponatraemia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Urine osmolality ≤ 100 = ADH suppressed. Caution: once solute intake is restored, **rapid aquaresis and overcorrection** may occur (high ODS risk – alcohol, malnutrition).

</details>

**8.** Treatment of SIAD with moderate or profound hyponatraemia (no severe symptoms) according to the European guideline:

- A. Vasopressin receptor antagonists are recommended in profound hyponatraemia. — T / F
- B. Demeclocycline and lithium are recommended against. — T / F
- C. Fluid restriction is first-line. — T / F
- D. Urea is recommended against. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.** The guideline recommends against vasopressin receptor antagonists (A). Urea 0.25–0.50 g/kg/day is a second-line option (D).

</details>

**9.** A patient with DKA has glucose 39.6 mmol/L and measured Na⁺ 125 mmol/L. Using a correction of 1.6 mmol/L per 5.6 mmol/L glucose above 5.6 mmol/L, the corrected Na⁺ is closest to:

- A. 145 mmol/L
- B. 140 mmol/L
- C. 135 mmol/L
- D. 130 mmol/L
- E. 127 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** (39.6 − 5.6) / 5.6 = 6.07; × 1.6 = 9.7; 125 + 9.7 ≈ **135** → hypertonic (translocational) hyponatraemia, not hypotonic.

</details>

**10.** Regarding hypernatraemia in the ICU:

- A. A rise in urine osmolality after desmopressin suggests central diabetes insipidus. — T / F
- B. Polyuria with urine osmolality < 300 mOsm/kg suggests diabetes insipidus. — T / F
- C. In a retrospective ICU cohort (Chauhan 2019), rapid correction (> 0.5 mmol/L/h) was associated with higher mortality. — T / F
- D. Acute hypernatraemia (< 48 h) can be corrected faster than chronic hypernatraemia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.** No difference in mortality (C).

</details>

**11.** A ventilated trauma patient on day 6 receives high-protein enteral nutrition. Na⁺ has risen from 141 to 154 mmol/L over 5 days despite 2.5–3 L/day of urine. Glucose is normal and no mannitol or diuretics are given. Serum urea has risen to 22 mmol/L; urine osmolality is 720 mOsm/kg, urine Na⁺ + K⁺ = 70 mmol/L. The most likely mechanism is:

- A. Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus
- B. Urea-induced osmotic diuresis
- C. Central diabetes insipidus
- D. Primary sodium gain from hypertonic fluids
- E. Insensible water loss with appropriate renal water conservation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** High U-osm (well above plasma) with a large volume → total solute excretion ~720 × 2.75 ≈ 2000 mOsm/day; Na⁺ + K⁺ explain only a small part of the urine osmoles (2 × 70 = 140 of 720) → the rest is mostly urea from protein feeding/catabolism. The electrolyte-free water clearance is positive (2.75 × [1 − 70/154] ≈ 1.5 L/day) → hypernatraemia. Central or nephrogenic DI would give dilute or isosmolar urine (C, A); with E, urine output would be low. Treat with enteral/IV free water and review the protein load (Lindner, NDT 2012).

</details>

**12.** Hypernatraemia with a normal or high urine output:

- A. In central diabetes insipidus urine osmolality is inappropriately low and increases after desmopressin. — T / F
- B. In the polyuric phase of AKI urine osmolality is typically close to plasma osmolality. — T / F
- C. After high-dose furosemide, a urine osmolality close to plasma osmolality proves nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. — T / F
- D. Osmotic diuresis from mannitol or glucose produces urine with an osmolality above plasma. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.** C: loop diuretics wash out the medullary concentration gradient, so the kidney cannot concentrate urine whatever the ADH level – reassess once the drug effect has worn off. D: in osmotic diuresis the urine is iso- to hypertonic, but the solute load forces a large volume, so free water is still lost.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 65-year-old malnourished alcoholic woman is admitted with vomiting and a seizure; Na⁺ 108 mmol/L, duration unknown. Outline your management over the first 48 hours.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Severe symptoms (seizure, vomiting) → 150 mL 3 % NaCl IV over 20 min, check Na⁺ after 20 min, repeat up to 3 boluses; target +5 mmol/L in the first hour (regardless of acuity).
- Unknown duration → treat as chronic; she has several ODS risk factors (Na⁺ near 105, alcoholism, malnutrition, possible hypokalaemia/liver disease) → cautious limit ~8 mmol/L/24 h.
- Absolute limits: ≤ 10 mmol/L in the first 24 h, ≤ 8 mmol/L per further 24 h until Na⁺ 130.
- Frequent Na⁺ checks (every 2–4 h initially) and urine output monitoring; the Adrogué–Madias formula ignores aquaresis.
- Anticipate aquaresis once the ADH stimulus resolves (volume repletion after vomiting, nausea settling, restored solute intake).
- If overcorrected: 10 mL/kg free water (5 % glucose) over 1 h ± desmopressin 2 µg IV (not more often than every 8 h).
- Diagnostic work-up in parallel: serum/urine osmolality, urine Na⁺, volume status, exclude adrenal/thyroid insufficiency; correct K⁺.

</details>

**O2. (open)** An 80-kg elderly man after neurosurgery has Na⁺ 160 mmol/L, polyuria and urine osmolality 150 mOsm/kg. Calculate his free water deficit and outline diagnosis and treatment.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Polyuria + urine osmolality < 300 → diabetes insipidus; after neurosurgery most likely central.
- Desmopressin test: a rise in urine osmolality confirms central DI (nephrogenic causes: lithium, hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia).
- TBW = 0.5 × 80 = 40 L; deficit = 40 × (160/140 − 1) ≈ 5.7 L.
- 1 L of 5 % glucose lowers Na⁺ by (0 − 160) / 41 ≈ 3.9 mmol/L; ~2.6 L is needed to lower Na⁺ by 10 mmol/L/24 h, plus ongoing (urinary) losses.
- Acute hypernatraemia (< 48 h, DI after neurosurgery) can be corrected faster; for chronic, traditionally ≤ 10–12 mmol/L/24 h.
- Chauhan (CJASN 2019): faster correction (> 0.5 mmol/L/h) was not associated with higher mortality or cerebral oedema; evidence for the limit in adults is weak.
- Frequent Na⁺ monitoring; treat with desmopressin plus free water.

</details>


## W06 · D2 – Potassium and magnesium: hyperkalaemia, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia

**1.** A haemodialysis patient has K⁺ 7.3 mmol/L with broad QRS complexes. The first drug to give is:

- A. Calcium chloride 10 % 10 mL IV
- B. Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate 10 g orally
- C. Furosemide 80 mg IV
- D. Nebulised salbutamol 10 mg
- E. Insulin 10 units with 25 g glucose

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** With ECG changes, first membrane stabilisation with calcium; then shift and removal (in a dialysis patient → urgent dialysis).

</details>

**2.** Regarding insulin–glucose for hyperkalaemia (ERC 2025):

- A. If pre-treatment glucose is < 7 mmol/L, a 10 % glucose infusion at 50 mL/h for 5 hours is suggested. — T / F
- B. Nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg can be added. — T / F
- C. Insulin removes potassium from the body. — T / F
- D. The dose is 25 units of soluble insulin with 10 g glucose IV. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.** Insulin only shifts K⁺ into cells (C). The dose is 10 units of soluble insulin with 25 g glucose IV (D).

</details>

**3.** You need to give 25 g of glucose and only 20 % glucose is available. The required volume is:

- A. 250 mL
- B. 50 mL
- C. 100 mL
- D. 200 mL
- E. 125 mL

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** 20 % = 20 g / 100 mL → 25 g = **125 mL**.

</details>

**4.** Regarding calcium in hyperkalaemia:

- A. In hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest, calcium and sodium bicarbonate should not be given together through the same line without flushing. — T / F
- B. 10 mL of 10 % calcium gluconate contains roughly three times more calcium than 10 mL of 10 % calcium chloride. — T / F
- C. Calcium lowers the serum potassium concentration. — T / F
- D. Its membrane-stabilising effect lasts about 24 hours, so a single dose suffices. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.** B: the reverse – calcium chloride 6.8 vs gluconate ~2.2 mmol; A: precipitation; D: the effect is short-lived, so doses may need repeating.

</details>

**5.** A patient on furosemide and long-term omeprazole has K⁺ 2.9 mmol/L that does not improve despite 3 days of IV KCl. The most likely contributing factor is:

- A. Metabolic acidosis
- B. Hypercalcaemia
- C. Hypophosphataemia
- D. Hyponatraemia
- E. Hypomagnesaemia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** Diuretics + PPI → Mg²⁺ deficiency → renal K⁺ wasting (ROMK); without Mg correction hypokalaemia is refractory.

</details>

**6.** ECG features of hypokalaemia include:

- B. U waves — T / F

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.** Peaked T waves would suggest hyperkalaemia.

</details>

**7.** In cardiac arrest attributed to severe hypokalaemia, ERC 2025 suggests:

- B. KCl 100 mmol as a bolus
- C. KCl 40 mmol over 1 hour
- D. Oral potassium after ROSC only

**E.** 

</details>

**8.** Recognised causes of hyperkalaemia include:

**10.** Regarding magnesium:

- A. A normal serum magnesium reliably excludes total body magnesium deficiency. — T / F
- B. Severe hypermagnesaemia can cause loss of deep tendon reflexes and respiratory depression. — T / F

- D. Long-term proton pump inhibitor use can cause hypomagnesaemia. — T / F

**O1. (open)** A patient with AKI on an ACE inhibitor and trimethoprim has K⁺ 7.0 mmol/L with peaked T waves and a widened QRS. Outline your management step by step, including doses, and explain the role of each drug.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Severe hyperkalaemia (≥ 6.5 mmol/L, ERC 2025) with ECG changes = urgent treatment; exclude pseudohyperkalaemia only if it does not delay therapy (blood gas analyser sample).
- Protect the heart: 10 mL 10 % CaCl₂ IV over 5 min (or 30 mL 10 % Ca gluconate over 10 min); stabilises the membrane, does not lower K⁺; short effect (~30–60 min) → repeat if ECG changes persist.
- Shift K⁺ into cells: insulin 10 U + glucose 25 g IV (onset ~15–30 min); if pre-treatment glucose < 7 mmol/L, follow with 10 % glucose 50 mL/h for 5 h; monitor for hypoglycaemia.
- Add nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg (additive, insufficient alone).
- Remove K⁺: SZC 10 g orally, loop diuretic if passing urine, dialysis if refractory; insulin/salbutamol only shift K⁺ → rebound.
- Monitor K⁺ and glucose.
- Prevent: stop ACE inhibitor and trimethoprim (ENaC blockade), treat the AKI cause.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why hypokalaemia may be refractory to potassium replacement in an ICU patient and how you would correct it safely.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- ~98 % of K⁺ is intracellular, so a small fall in serum K⁺ reflects a large total deficit (hundreds of mmol).
- Hypomagnesaemia (diuretics, long-term PPIs, alcohol, refeeding, CRRT…) releases ROMK inhibition → renal K⁺ wasting; without Mg²⁺ correction hypokalaemia remains refractory.
- Serum Mg poorly reflects stores (< 1 % in serum) – a normal level does not exclude deficiency.
- Ongoing shift into cells (insulin, β₂-agonists/adrenaline, alkalosis, refeeding) or ongoing losses (diuretics, mineralocorticoids, amphotericin B) also contribute.
- Give MgSO₄ (2 g ≈ 8 mmol IV, then infusion) alongside KCl.
- IV KCl peripherally up to ~10 mmol/h, higher rates centrally only with ECG monitoring; dilute in glucose-free solution.
- Monitor ECG (U waves, QT(U)) and stop QT-prolonging drugs.

</details>


## W06 · D3 – Calcium and phosphate, refeeding syndrome

**1.** Total calcium 1.84 mmol/L, albumin 22 g/L. Using the formula Ca + 0.02 × (40 − albumin), the corrected calcium is:

- A. 2.20 mmol/L
- B. 1.98 mmol/L
- C. 2.10 mmol/L
- D. 2.36 mmol/L
- E. 2.48 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** 0.02 × 18 = 0.36; 1.84 + 0.36 = **2.20**. In the ICU, however, iCa²⁺ should be measured.

</details>

**2.** Regarding ionised calcium:

- A. Alkalosis decreases ionised calcium. — T / F
- B. Albumin-corrected calcium accurately predicts ionised calcium in critically ill patients. — T / F
- C. A total/ionised calcium ratio > 2.5 during citrate CRRT suggests citrate accumulation. — T / F
- D. About 90 % of plasma calcium is ionised. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.** About 45–50 % of plasma calcium is ionised; ~40 % is protein-bound (D).

</details>

**3.** In the refeeding syndrome trial by Doig et al. (2015), protocolised caloric restriction in critically ill adults with refeeding hypophosphataemia:

- A. Increased mortality
- B. Consisted of stopping all nutrition for 5 days

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** Primary outcome +4.9 days, NS; 60-day survival higher (p = 0.002). Restriction = 20 kcal/h for ≥ 2 days.

**4.** Consequences of severe hypophosphataemia include:

- B. Respiratory muscle weakness and difficult weaning — T / F

**5.** A patient with metastatic lung cancer is confused and dehydrated; total calcium 3.9 mmol/L, creatinine mildly raised. The most appropriate initial management is:

**6.** Regarding refeeding syndrome:

**11.** A 46 kg woman (BMI 16.5) with alcohol use disorder and 8 days of minimal intake is ventilated for aspiration pneumonia. Enteral feeding was started at 25 kcal/kg/day. On day 3, phosphate has fallen from 1.10 to 0.40 mmol/L, K⁺ is 3.0 and Mg²⁺ 0.55 mmol/L; she fails a spontaneous breathing trial with rapid shallow breathing. The most appropriate management is:

- A. Replace the feed with glucose 10 % infusion to avoid protein overload
- B. Stop all nutrition for 7 days
- C. No change – a fall in phosphate is expected and harmless during refeeding
- D. Reduce energy intake (e.g. 20 kcal/h for ≥ 48 h), give IV phosphate, K⁺, Mg²⁺ and thiamine, then increase feeding gradually
- E. Continue full feeding and give oral phosphate only

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.** Phosphate fell by ~64 % within 5 days of feeding with organ dysfunction (respiratory muscle weakness → weaning failure) = **severe refeeding syndrome** (ASPEN 2020: fall in PO₄/K⁺/Mg²⁺ of 10–20 % mild, 20–30 % moderate, > 30 % or organ dysfunction severe). She also met NICE high-risk criteria (≥ 2: BMI < 18.5, little intake > 5 days, alcohol) and should have started at ≤ 10 kcal/kg/day with thiamine. Doig 2015 / ESPEN: restrict calories for 48 h, replace electrolytes IV, then increase gradually. Glucose alone drives insulin and further intracellular phosphate shift. See the PCP/refeeding case in [W03/D4](#W03-D4) and the [EDIC II-style cases workshop](#extra-clinical-cases-workshop).

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 50-kg woman with BMI 15 has eaten almost nothing for 2 weeks and is admitted to the ICU. Outline how you would start nutrition, and how you would respond if her phosphate falls from 1.0 to 0.50 mmol/L on day 2.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- High risk of refeeding syndrome by NICE CG32 (BMI < 16 and little/no intake > 10 days; one criterion is enough).
- Thiamine and B vitamins before the first feed and during the first 10 days.
- Start cautiously: max. 10 kcal/kg/day (= 500 kcal/day), increase gradually; prophylactic K⁺, PO₄, Mg²⁺ according to levels.
- Monitor phosphate at least daily in the first week (2–3 times daily once refeeding hypophosphataemia develops – ESPEN 2019).
- Day 2: Pi 0.50 (< 0.65) with a fall of 0.50 (> 0.16) within 72 h = refeeding hypophosphataemia (Doig definition).
- Restrict to 20 kcal/h for ≥ 48 h, then return gradually to target over 2–3 days, plus IV phosphate; monitor Ca²⁺ and K⁺ during replacement.
- Evidence: Doig 2015 – primary outcome NS (+4.9 days, p = 0.19) but higher 60-day survival (~91 % vs ~79 %, p = 0.002).

</details>

**O2. (open)** A septic patient on citrate CRRT has total Ca 2.40 mmol/L, iCa²⁺ 0.85 mmol/L and a worsening high anion gap metabolic acidosis. Interpret these results and explain why the albumin-corrected calcium is unhelpful here.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Total/ionised Ca ratio = 2.40 / 0.85 ≈ 2.8 (> ~2.5) → citrate accumulation.
- Citrate accumulation is typical with citrate CRRT in liver failure/shock: ↓ iCa²⁺, ↑ total/iCa ratio, high-AG metabolic acidosis.
- Only iCa²⁺ (~45–50 % of plasma Ca) is biologically active; complexed Ca (citrate) increases total Ca without raising iCa²⁺.
- Corrected Ca (+ 0.02 × (40 − albumin)) is inaccurate in the ICU (altered pH, complexes, hypoalbuminaemia) → measure iCa²⁺.
- Consequences of low iCa²⁺: tetany, seizures, prolonged QT, hypotension, ↓ contractility, impaired coagulation.
- Management: treat symptomatic/severe hypocalcaemia with IV Ca gluconate, correct Mg²⁺, and reassess the citrate anticoagulation.

</details>


## W06 · Saturday – weekly review: Electrolytes

**1.** A 62-year-old woman with chronic thiazide-induced hyponatraemia (Na⁺ 112 mmol/L) received 3 % saline boluses for confusion. After stopping the thiazide, urine output is 400 mL/h and Na⁺ is 124 mmol/L at 12 hours. The best next step is:

- A. Start tolvaptan
- B. Continue observation; the correction is within limits
- C. Give 0.9 % saline to replace urine output
- D. Stop sodium-containing fluids, give electrolyte-free water (about 10 mL/kg over 1 h) and consider desmopressin 2 µg IV
- E. Give furosemide

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.** +12 mmol/L in 12 h > 10 mmol/L/24 h → overcorrection (aquaresis after stopping the thiazide) → actively re-lower Na⁺.

</details>

**2.** According to the European hyponatraemia guideline:

**3.** A 70-kg man (TBW 42 L) with Na⁺ 120 mmol/L receives 1 L of 0.9 % saline (Na⁺ 154 mmol/L). The Adrogué–Madias formula predicts a rise in Na⁺ of approximately:
