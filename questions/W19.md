## W19 · D1 – Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state

**1.** A 24-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes presents with vomiting. Glucose 28 mmol/L, venous pH 7.18, HCO₃⁻ 9 mmol/L, capillary β-hydroxybutyrate 5.2 mmol/L, K⁺ 3.1 mmol/L. What is the most appropriate immediate step after starting fluids?

- A. Start fixed-rate insulin 0.1 U/kg/h and recheck K⁺ in 2 hours
- B. Give an insulin bolus of 0.1 U/kg followed by an infusion
- C. Give sodium bicarbonate 1 mmol/kg
- D. Replace potassium and delay insulin until K⁺ is above 3.5 mmol/L
- E. Start subcutaneous rapid-acting insulin every 2 hours

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Total-body potassium is depleted in DKA, and insulin will push K⁺ into cells. With K⁺ < 3.5 mmol/L at presentation, insulin is delayed while potassium is given (about 10–20 mmol/h with ECG monitoring) until K⁺ > 3.5 mmol/L.

**Why not the others:**
- A – starting insulin with K⁺ 3.1 mmol/L risks fatal hypokalaemia and arrhythmia.
- B – a bolus is not required, and insulin must wait here anyway.
- C – bicarbonate is not indicated at pH 7.18 and would worsen hypokalaemia.
- E – subcutaneous insulin is for mild/uncomplicated moderate DKA; this is moderate–severe with hypokalaemia.

**Ref:** Umpierrez GE, Diabetes Care 2024; Dhatariya KK, Diabet Med 2022.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the diagnostic criteria of hyperglycaemic crises according to the 2024 international consensus:

- A. DKA requires a plasma glucose of at least 13.9 mmol/L (250 mg/dL). — T / F
- B. A β-hydroxybutyrate of 3.0 mmol/L or more fulfils the ketosis criterion for DKA. — T / F
- C. A bicarbonate below 18 mmol/L fulfils the acidosis criterion for DKA, even if the pH is 7.32. — T / F
- D. HHS requires an effective serum osmolality of at least 350 mOsm/kg. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – the glucose criterion is ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) *or* a history of diabetes, so euglycaemic DKA is included.
- B (True) – BHB ≥ 3.0 mmol/L (or urine ketones ≥ 2+).
- C (True) – acidosis is defined as pH < 7.30 *and/or* HCO₃⁻ < 18 mmol/L.
- D (False) – HHS needs effective osmolality > 300 mOsm/kg (or total > 320), with glucose ≥ 33.3 mmol/L.

**Ref:** Umpierrez GE, Diabetes Care 2024.

</details>

**3.** A 58-year-old man on empagliflozin develops vomiting 2 days after a laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Glucose 9.8 mmol/L, pH 7.15, HCO₃⁻ 8 mmol/L, anion gap 24 mmol/L, β-hydroxybutyrate 6.1 mmol/L, lactate 1.4 mmol/L. What is the most appropriate management?

- A. No insulin because the glucose is below 11 mmol/L; give fluids only
- B. Stop empagliflozin and give IV fluids, fixed-rate insulin and early dextrose
- C. Sodium bicarbonate infusion to pH 7.30
- D. Continue empagliflozin and start metformin
- E. Haemodialysis for unexplained high anion gap acidosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** This is euglycaemic DKA associated with an SGLT2 inhibitor (fasting, surgery). Treatment is as for DKA – fluids, fixed-rate insulin 0.1 U/kg/h – with dextrose from the start so that enough insulin can be given to clear the ketones, and the SGLT2 inhibitor is stopped.

**Why not the others:**
- A – ketoacidosis is driven by insulin deficiency; insulin is the key therapy whatever the glucose.
- C – bicarbonate is not routinely indicated (2024: only if pH < 7.0).
- D – the SGLT2 inhibitor caused the problem, and metformin is contraindicated in acidosis.
- E – the cause is known and treatable medically.

**Ref:** Umpierrez GE, Diabetes Care 2024.

</details>

**4.** Regarding potassium, sodium and ketone measurements in DKA:

- A. A normal serum potassium at presentation excludes a total-body potassium deficit. — T / F
- B. The nitroprusside test detects acetoacetate but not β-hydroxybutyrate. — T / F
- C. A measured sodium of 140 mmol/L with glucose 40 mmol/L indicates a free-water deficit. — T / F
- D. Insulin therapy tends to raise serum potassium. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – total K⁺ is depleted by 3–5 mmol/kg; serum K⁺ is normal or high because of insulin deficiency and hyperosmolality.
- B (True) – blood BHB should be measured; nitroprusside reactions can paradoxically increase during recovery.
- C (True) – corrected Na⁺ ≈ 140 + 1.6–2.4 × (34/5.6) ≈ 150–155 mmol/L, i.e. hypernatraemic dehydration.
- D (False) – insulin drives K⁺ into cells and lowers serum K⁺.

**Ref:** Umpierrez GE, Diabetes Care 2024; Dhatariya KK, Diabet Med 2022.

</details>

**5.** A 78-year-old nursing-home resident with type 2 diabetes is confused. Glucose 52 mmol/L, Na⁺ 152 mmol/L, urea 24 mmol/L, β-hydroxybutyrate 0.8 mmol/L, pH 7.36. Which is the best initial management?

- A. Insulin bolus 0.1 U/kg, then 0.1 U/kg/h
- B. 0.45 % saline 1 L/h to correct hypernatraemia rapidly
- C. Isotonic crystalloid, delaying fixed-rate insulin at 0.05 U/kg/h until glucose stops falling with fluids, plus LMWH prophylaxis
- D. Isotonic crystalloid aiming for a sodium fall of 15 mmol/L in 24 h
- E. Sodium bicarbonate and furosemide

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** This is HHS (glucose ≥ 30–33 mmol/L, effective osmolality 2 × 152 + 52 = 356 mOsm/kg, no ketoacidosis). Fluids are the main treatment and lower glucose by themselves; insulin is started at 0.05 U/kg/h once glucose stops falling (JBDS), and LMWH is given because of high VTE risk.

**Why not the others:**
- A – DKA dosing in HHS lowers glucose and osmolality too fast and shifts fluid out of the circulation.
- B – hypotonic fluid is used only if osmolality fails to fall despite adequate isotonic fluid; rapid correction risks cerebral oedema and ODS.
- D – Na⁺ should fall by no more than 10 mmol/L in 24 h.
- E – there is no acidosis; diuretics worsen dehydration.

**Ref:** Mustafa OG, Diabet Med 2023; Umpierrez GE, Diabetes Care 2024.

</details>

**6.** A 30-year-old man with DKA has received 0.9 % saline (6 L) and insulin for 14 hours. β-hydroxybutyrate is 0.4 mmol/L, glucose 9 mmol/L, pH 7.31, Na⁺ 141, Cl⁻ 117, HCO₃⁻ 16 mmol/L. What best explains the persistently low bicarbonate?

- A. Persistent ketoacidosis needing a higher insulin rate
- B. Lactic acidosis from occult sepsis
- C. Hyperchloraemic normal anion gap acidosis from saline
- D. Renal tubular acidosis type 4
- E. Starvation ketosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The anion gap is 141 − (117 + 16) = 8 mmol/L (normal) and ketones have cleared (< 0.6 mmol/L). The residual acidosis is hyperchloraemic, caused by large volumes of 0.9 % saline; it is benign and does not need more insulin. Balanced crystalloids reduce it (SMART/SALT-ED DKA subgroup).

**Why not the others:**
- A – BHB 0.4 mmol/L and pH 7.31 meet resolution criteria.
- B – lactic acidosis would raise the anion gap.
- D – type 4 RTA is a chronic hyperkalaemic state, not explained by this course.
- E – starvation ketosis would raise BHB and the anion gap.

**Ref:** Self WH, JAMA Netw Open 2020; Umpierrez GE, Diabetes Care 2024.

</details>

**7.** Regarding fluid and insulin management of adult DKA:

- A. Long-acting basal insulin should be continued during the intravenous insulin infusion. — T / F
- B. Once glucose falls below about 14 mmol/L, the insulin infusion should be stopped. — T / F
- C. In the SMART/SALT-ED DKA subgroup, balanced crystalloids shortened the time to DKA resolution compared with saline. — T / F
- D. A fall in β-hydroxybutyrate of at least 0.5 mmol/L/h indicates an adequate response to insulin. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – continuing basal insulin prevents rebound ketosis after the infusion stops.
- B (False) – dextrose is added and insulin continued until ketoacidosis resolves.
- C (True) – median 13.0 vs 16.9 h (n = 172).
- D (True) – JBDS targets: BHB ↓ ≥ 0.5 mmol/L/h, HCO₃⁻ ↑ ≥ 3 mmol/L/h, glucose ↓ ≥ 3 mmol/L/h.

**Ref:** Dhatariya KK, Diabet Med 2022; Self WH, JAMA Netw Open 2020.

</details>

**8.** A 16-year-old with severe DKA (pH 6.98) becomes drowsy with headache, bradycardia and hypertension 6 hours into treatment. Glucose is 15 mmol/L, K⁺ 4.2 mmol/L. What is the most appropriate immediate action?

- A. Give mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg (or 3 % saline) and reduce the fluid rate
- B. Give sodium bicarbonate
- C. Increase the insulin infusion
- D. Give a 20 mL/kg bolus of 0.45 % saline
- E. Arrange an urgent EEG before any treatment

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Headache, falling consciousness, bradycardia and hypertension during DKA treatment in a young patient suggest cerebral oedema. Treatment should not wait for imaging: hyperosmolar therapy (mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg or hypertonic saline), head-up positioning, reduced fluid rate and ICU care.

**Why not the others:**
- B – bicarbonate is a risk factor for cerebral oedema in children.
- C – a faster fall in glucose and osmolality could worsen oedema.
- D – hypotonic fluid worsens brain swelling.
- E – this is a clinical diagnosis; treat first, then CT if needed.

**Ref:** Kuppermann N (PECARN FLUID), N Engl J Med 2018; Umpierrez GE, Diabetes Care 2024.

**9.** Regarding the hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state:

- A. The water deficit is usually larger than in DKA. — T / F
- B. A rise in measured sodium during the first hours of treatment is acceptable if osmolality is falling. — T / F
- C. Mortality is lower than that of DKA. — T / F
- D. Prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin is recommended unless contraindicated. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – about 100–220 mL/kg vs about 100 mL/kg in DKA.
- B (True) – as glucose falls, water moves into cells and Na⁺ rises; what matters is a controlled fall in osmolality (3–8 mOsm/kg/h).
- C (False) – HHS mortality (5–20 %) is much higher than DKA (< 1–2 %).
- D (True) – hyperviscosity and immobility give a high VTE risk.

**Ref:** Mustafa OG, Diabet Med 2023.

**10.** A 45-year-old woman is admitted with moderate DKA. All of the following are recommended in the 2024 consensus EXCEPT:

- A. Measuring blood β-hydroxybutyrate to monitor response
- B. Adding potassium to fluids when K⁺ is below 5.0 mmol/L
- C. Starting dextrose when glucose falls below 13.9 mmol/L
- D. Routine phosphate replacement
- E. Treating the precipitating cause

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Phosphate falls during treatment, but routine replacement has not improved outcomes; it is given only for severe hypophosphataemia (< 0.32 mmol/L) or symptoms (muscle weakness, cardiac dysfunction, haemolysis).

**Why not the others:**
- A – blood BHB is the preferred monitoring parameter.
- B – K⁺ replacement starts below 5.0 mmol/L (target 4–5).
- C – dextrose allows insulin to continue until ketones clear.
- E – infection, insulin omission and other triggers must be treated.

**Ref:** Umpierrez GE, Diabetes Care 2024.

</details>

**11.** A 35-year-old man is recovering from DKA. β-hydroxybutyrate 0.5 mmol/L, pH 7.34, and he is eating. He has not been on insulin before. What is the safest way to stop the intravenous insulin infusion?

- A. Stop the infusion and give subcutaneous insulin when glucose exceeds 15 mmol/L
- B. Stop the infusion and start metformin
- C. Give subcutaneous basal insulin, and stop the infusion immediately in the same minute
- D. Continue the infusion for 24 more hours regardless of eating
- E. Give subcutaneous basal (and prandial) insulin and continue the infusion for 1–2 hours afterwards

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** The half-life of IV insulin is only minutes. Subcutaneous basal insulin is given 1–2 h before the infusion is stopped (basal–bolus, about 0.3–0.6 U/kg/day in insulin-naïve patients) to avoid rebound hyperglycaemia and ketosis.

**Why not the others:**
- A – a gap without insulin leads to recurrent DKA.
- B – oral agents are not appropriate for insulin-deficient DKA.
- C – without overlap there is an insulin-free gap while subcutaneous insulin is absorbed.
- D – once resolved and eating, prolonging the infusion has no benefit.

**Ref:** Umpierrez GE, Diabetes Care 2024; Dhatariya KK, Diabet Med 2022.

</details>

**O2. (open)** How does the management of HHS differ from that of DKA?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

## W19 · D2 – Glucose control in the ICU: Leuven, NICE-SUGAR, TGC-fast and current targets

**1.** A 66-year-old man with septic shock on noradrenaline and enteral feeding has repeated arterial glucose values of 12–13 mmol/L. Which insulin strategy is best supported by current guidelines?

- A. No insulin until glucose exceeds 15 mmol/L
- B. IV insulin targeting 4.4–6.1 mmol/L
- C. Subcutaneous sliding-scale insulin every 6 hours
- D. IV insulin infusion by protocol, targeting about 7.8–10 mmol/L
- E. Stop enteral feeding to lower glucose

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** SSC 2021 (unchanged in SSC 2026) recommends starting insulin at glucose ≥ 10 mmol/L (180 mg/dL), with a typical target of 7.8–10 mmol/L; in an unstable patient on vasopressors an IV infusion with a validated protocol is preferred.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the threshold for starting insulin is 10 mmol/L, not 15.
- B – tight control increased mortality in NICE-SUGAR and showed no benefit in TGC-fast.
- C – subcutaneous absorption is unpredictable in shock; sliding scales alone are discouraged.
- E – withholding nutrition to control glucose is not appropriate.

**Ref:** Prescott HC (SSC 2026), Crit Care Med 2026; Honarmand K, Crit Care Med 2024.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the NICE-SUGAR trial:

- A. It randomised more than 6,000 mixed medical and surgical ICU patients. — T / F
- B. Intensive glucose control (4.5–6.0 mmol/L) increased 90-day mortality. — T / F
- C. Severe hypoglycaemia (≤ 2.2 mmol/L) was more common with intensive control. — T / F
- D. The benefit of intensive control was confined to surgical patients. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – 6,104 patients in 42 centres.
- B (True) – 27.5 vs 24.9 % (OR 1.14, P = 0.02).
- C (True) – 6.8 vs 0.5 %.
- D (False) – there was no benefit in the surgical subgroup either; the treatment effect did not differ between surgical and medical patients.

**Ref:** NICE-SUGAR Investigators, N Engl J Med 2009.

</details>

**3.** A 54-year-old woman in septic shock (noradrenaline 0.4 µg/kg/min, generalised oedema, Hb 72 g/L) has a capillary fingerstick glucose of 3.4 mmol/L. What is the best next step?

- A. Check glucose immediately on arterial blood with a blood-gas analyser, while treating as hypoglycaemia if confirmed
- B. Ignore it – capillary values are always falsely low
- C. Increase the insulin infusion
- D. Repeat fingerstick in 4 hours
- E. Give glucagon 1 mg IM

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Capillary point-of-care meters are inaccurate in shock, vasopressor therapy, oedema and anaemia. An arterial sample on a blood-gas analyser should be checked at once; true hypoglycaemia is treated with IV dextrose and a review of insulin and nutrition.

**Why not the others:**
- B – the error can go either way; hypoglycaemia must not be dismissed.
- C – increasing insulin with possible hypoglycaemia is dangerous.
- D – a 4-hour delay could allow severe neuroglycopenia.
- E – with IV access, IV dextrose is faster and more reliable; glucagon is a fallback.

**Ref:** Evans L, Intensive Care Med 2021; Honarmand K, Crit Care Med 2024.

</details>

**4.** Regarding the Leuven I trial (Van den Berghe, 2001) and TGC-fast (2023):

- A. Leuven I enrolled mainly cardiac surgical patients. — T / F
- B. Patients in Leuven I commonly received early parenteral nutrition. — T / F
- C. TGC-fast showed that tight glucose control reduced 90-day mortality when early parenteral nutrition was withheld. — T / F
- D. TGC-fast used a computerised insulin-dosing algorithm in the tight-control arm. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – about 63 % were cardiac surgery patients.
- B (True) – early IV glucose and parenteral nutrition were standard, producing marked hyperglycaemia in the control arm.
- C (False) – there was no difference in ICU stay (HR 1.00) or 90-day mortality (10.5 vs 10.1 %).
- D (True) – the LOGIC-Insulin algorithm.

**Ref:** Van den Berghe G, N Engl J Med 2001; Gunst J (TGC-fast), N Engl J Med 2023.

</details>

**5.** A 70-year-old man on an insulin infusion (4 U/h) has his enteral feed stopped for a planned tracheostomy. Two hours later his glucose is 2.1 mmol/L. Which is the most likely cause?

- A. Relative adrenal insufficiency
- B. Insulinoma
- C. Continuing insulin after interruption of nutrition
- D. Capillary measurement artefact
- E. Sepsis-induced gluconeogenesis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Interrupting nutrition while the insulin infusion continues is a classic, preventable cause of ICU hypoglycaemia. Protocols must couple insulin to nutrition: reduce or stop insulin, or give dextrose, when feeding stops.

**Why not the others:**
- A – adrenal insufficiency can cause hypoglycaemia but does not explain the timing.
- B – insulinoma is rare and would not appear after a feed stop on an infusion.
- D – artefact is possible but must be confirmed, not assumed; the timing points to insulin.
- E – sepsis *increases* gluconeogenesis (though severe sepsis and liver failure can cause hypoglycaemia).

**Ref:** Honarmand K, Crit Care Med 2024; NICE-SUGAR Investigators, N Engl J Med 2012.

</details>

**6.** According to the SCCM 2024 guideline on glycaemic control, which statement for critically ill adults is correct?

- A. Insulin should be titrated to 4.4–7.7 mmol/L to reduce infections
- B. Continuous glucose monitoring is recommended as the sole monitoring method
- C. Glucose targets should be individualised according to HbA1c
- D. Insulin is recommended only when glucose exceeds 15 mmol/L
- E. Titrating insulin to an intensive range of 4.4–7.7 mmol/L rather than 7.8–11.1 mmol/L is not suggested

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** SCCM 2024 makes a conditional recommendation (moderate certainty) against intensive targets (80–139 mg/dL) compared with conventional targets (140–200 mg/dL), mainly because of hypoglycaemia; it also suggests protocols with explicit decision support.

**Why not the others:**
- A – this is exactly what the guideline suggests against.
- B – CGM accuracy in critical illness is not established enough for such a recommendation.
- C – HbA1c-based individualised targets are a research priority (CONTROLING was neutral).
- D – the usual starting threshold is ~10 mmol/L (180 mg/dL).

**Ref:** Honarmand K, Crit Care Med 2024.

</details>

**7.** Regarding hypoglycaemia in ICU patients:

- A. Moderate hypoglycaemia (2.3–3.9 mmol/L) is not associated with mortality. — T / F
- B. Sedation and β-blockers can mask the warning signs of hypoglycaemia. — T / F
- C. Kidney failure lowers the risk of hypoglycaemia by reducing insulin clearance. — T / F
- D. Glucagon is highly effective in patients with acute liver failure. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – in NICE-SUGAR, moderate hypoglycaemia was associated with death (HR ≈ 1.4), severe with HR ≈ 2.1.
- B (True) – adrenergic and neuroglycopenic signs are masked in sedated, ventilated patients.
- C (False) – reduced insulin clearance (and gluconeogenesis) in kidney failure *increases* the risk.
- D (False) – glucagon needs hepatic glycogen; it works poorly in liver failure – use IV dextrose.

**Ref:** NICE-SUGAR Investigators, N Engl J Med 2012.

**8.** A 62-year-old woman with acute ischaemic stroke and diabetes has a glucose of 13 mmol/L on admission. Which statement about glucose management is best supported by the SHINE trial?

- A. Intensive IV insulin to 4.4–7.2 mmol/L improves functional outcome
- B. Intensive treatment did not improve functional outcome and caused more severe hypoglycaemia
- C. Hyperglycaemia should be left untreated in stroke
- D. Glucose should be kept below 5 mmol/L to protect the penumbra
- E. Dextrose infusions should be given to all stroke patients

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** SHINE (n = 1,151) compared IV insulin targeting 4.4–7.2 mmol/L with subcutaneous insulin targeting 4.4–10 mmol/L: favourable outcome 20.5 vs 21.6 % (no difference), with severe hypoglycaemia 2.6 vs 0 %.

**Why not the others:**
- A – no improvement was found.
- C – hyperglycaemia is still treated, to the standard range.
- D – low glucose is harmful to the injured brain (neuroglycopenia).
- E – routine dextrose worsens hyperglycaemia.

**Ref:** Johnston KC (SHINE), JAMA 2019.

</details>

**9.** Regarding mechanisms and causes of hyperglycaemia in critical illness:

- A. Cortisol and catecholamines promote hepatic gluconeogenesis. — T / F
- B. Hydrocortisone for septic shock lowers blood glucose. — T / F
- C. Neurons and endothelial cells take up glucose largely independently of insulin. — T / F
- D. Parenteral nutrition dextrose loads contribute to hyperglycaemia. — T / F

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

**10.** A 59-year-old man is admitted to the ICU after major abdominal surgery. All of the following increase the risk of insulin-induced hypoglycaemia EXCEPT:

- A. Starting continuous kidney replacement therapy
- B. Decompensated cirrhosis
- C. Abrupt cessation of parenteral nutrition while insulin continues
- D. Intensive glucose targets
- E. Starting a hydrocortisone infusion for septic shock

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Hydrocortisone raises glucose and insulin requirements. Hypoglycaemia occurs when steroids are *tapered or stopped* without reducing insulin.

**Why not the others:**
- A – kidney failure reduces insulin clearance; some CRRT fluids are glucose-free and remove glucose.
- B – liver failure impairs gluconeogenesis and glycogen stores.
- C – a classic iatrogenic cause.
- D – tight targets multiplied hypoglycaemia in every trial.

**Ref:** Honarmand K, Crit Care Med 2024.

**11.** A peritoneal dialysis patient using icodextrin has a point-of-care glucose of 18 mmol/L but appears clinically hypoglycaemic and sweaty. What is the most likely explanation?

- A. Stress hyperglycaemia
- B. Falsely high reading on a glucose dehydrogenase–PQQ meter due to maltose from icodextrin
- C. Pseudohypoglycaemia from leucocytosis
- D. Insulin resistance from uraemia
- E. Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Icodextrin is metabolised to maltose, which GDH-PQQ meters read as glucose. Glucose can be dangerously overestimated, insulin given in error, and true hypoglycaemia missed. Glucose should be checked with a laboratory or blood-gas analyser (glucose oxidase/hexokinase).

**Why not the others:**
- A – stress hyperglycaemia does not explain hypoglycaemic symptoms.
- C – leucocytosis causes falsely *low* lab values in stored samples.
- D – uraemia does not cause sweating with apparent high glucose.
- E – HHS causes dehydration and confusion, not adrenergic sweating, and glucose would be far higher.

**Ref:** Honarmand K, Crit Care Med 2024.

</details>

## W19 · D3 – Thyroid emergencies: thyroid storm, myxoedema coma and thyroid function in critical illness

**1.** A 38-year-old woman with untreated Graves' disease is admitted with pneumonia, temperature 40.1 °C, AF at 160/min, agitation and jaundice. Burch–Wartofsky score is 65. Which treatment sequence is most appropriate?

- A. Lugol's iodine first, then propylthiouracil 1 hour later
- B. Radioiodine ablation within 24 hours
- C. Propylthiouracil (or methimazole), then iodide at least 1 hour later, plus β-blockade and hydrocortisone
- D. Aspirin for fever and levothyroxine
- E. Amiodarone loading for AF and observation of thyroid function

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** This is thyroid storm (BWPS ≥ 45). The thionamide is given first to block synthesis; iodide is given at least 1 h later to block release without supplying substrate for new hormone. β-blockade (cautious, ideally short-acting IV) and hydrocortisone 300 mg then 100 mg every 8 h complete the treatment, together with antibiotics.

**Why not the others:**
- A – iodide before a thionamide can fuel further hormone synthesis.
- B – radioiodine is a definitive treatment, not for the acute storm, and may worsen thyroiditis.
- D – salicylates displace T4 from binding proteins; levothyroxine is absurd here.
- E – amiodarone is an iodine load and is not first-line; observation is inadequate.

**Ref:** Ross DS, Thyroid 2016; Taylor P, Eur Thyroid J 2026.

## W19 · D4 – Adrenal insufficiency, adrenal crisis and critical illness–related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI)

- A. Short Synacthen test, then treat according to the result

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Why C:** This is adrenal crisis. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV immediately, then 200 mg/24 h (infusion or 50 mg every 6 h), with isotonic fluid (about 1 L in the first hour) and dextrose for hypoglycaemia. At these doses hydrocortisone also supplies enough mineralocorticoid activity.

- A – the diagnosis is known; testing would only delay life-saving treatment.
- B – dexamethasone has no mineralocorticoid activity, and oral fludrocortisone is unreliable with vomiting.

- C. Fluid restriction is the first-line treatment of hyponatraemia after subarachnoid haemorrhage. — T / F
- D. Vasopressin receptor antagonists are recommended first-line for profound hyponatraemia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – up to two or three boluses, checking Na⁺ between them.
- B (True) – a 5 mmol/L rise usually controls symptoms; then ≤ 10 mmol/L in 24 h and ≤ 8 mmol/L per subsequent day.
- C (False) – fluid restriction after SAH increases delayed cerebral ischaemia; salt and volume are replaced.
- D (False) – the guideline does not recommend vasopressin antagonists for moderate or profound hyponatraemia (overcorrection, hepatotoxicity).

**Ref:** Spasovski G, Eur J Endocrinol 2014. → [D5](D5.md)
