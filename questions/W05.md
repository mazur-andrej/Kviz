## W05 · D1 – Acid–base: Henderson–Hasselbalch, compensation, anion gap, delta ratio

**1.** A septic patient has Na⁺ 136 mmol/L, Cl⁻ 100 mmol/L, HCO₃⁻ 16 mmol/L and albumin 24 g/L. The albumin-corrected anion gap is approximately:

- A. 28 mmol/L
- B. 30 mmol/L
- C. 24 mmol/L
- D. 20 mmol/L
- E. 14 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Uncorrected AG = 136 − (100 + 16) = 20 mmol/L. Albumin is 16 g/L below 40, so the correction is 0.25 × 16 = 4 → corrected AG = 20 + 4 = **24 mmol/L**, a clear HAGMA partly hidden by hypoalbuminaemia (Figge).

**Why not the others:**
- A – 28 would need a correction of 0.5 per g/L, twice the validated factor.
- B – 30 would need a correction of 10 mmol/L, as if albumin were 0 g/L.
- D – 20 is the uncorrected AG; it ignores the albumin deficit that almost every ICU patient has.
- E – 14 lies in the classical normal range; the correction is added to, never subtracted from, the AG.

**EDIC trap:** each 10 g/L fall in albumin lowers the AG by ~2.5 mmol/L.

**Ref:** Figge J et al., Crit Care Med 1998;26:1807–1810; Kraut JA, Madias NE, Clin J Am Soc Nephrol 2007;2:162–174.

</details>

**2.** A patient with metabolic acidosis has HCO₃⁻ 12 mmol/L and PaCO₂ 34 mmHg (4.5 kPa). Which statements are correct?

- A. The expected PaCO₂ by Winter's formula is approximately 26 ± 2 mmHg. — T / F
- B. The calculated pH is approximately 7.30. — T / F
- C. Respiratory compensation is appropriate. — T / F
- D. The measured PaCO₂ indicates a coexisting respiratory acidosis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – Winter's formula: 1.5 × 12 + 8 = 26 ± 2 mmHg (24–28 mmHg, ≈ 3.2–3.7 kPa).
- B (False) – pH = 6.1 + log(12 / (0.03 × 34)) = 6.1 + log 11.8 = 6.1 + 1.07 ≈ **7.17**; Henderson gives the same: [H⁺] = 24 × 34 / 12 = 68 nmol/L.
- C (False) – 34 mmHg is above the upper limit (28 mmHg), so the ventilatory response is inadequate.
- D (True) – a PaCO₂ above the Winter range means a coexisting respiratory acidosis (fatigue, sedation, impending respiratory failure).

**EDIC trap:** a "near-normal" PaCO₂ in severe metabolic acidosis is a warning sign, not reassurance.

**Ref:** Albert MS, Dell RB, Winters RW, Ann Intern Med 1967;66:312–322; Berend K et al., N Engl J Med 2014;371:1434–1445.

</details>

**3.** A patient with normal albumin has an anion gap of 30 mmol/L and HCO₃⁻ of 18 mmol/L. The most likely interpretation is:

- A. Pure hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis
- B. High anion gap metabolic acidosis with a coexisting metabolic alkalosis
- C. Pure high anion gap metabolic acidosis
- D. Laboratory error, because the anion gap cannot exceed 25 mmol/L
- E. Combined high and normal anion gap metabolic acidosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Delta ratio = (AG − 12) / (24 − HCO₃⁻) = (30 − 12) / (24 − 18) = 18 / 6 = **3** (> 2). HCO₃⁻ has fallen much less than the unmeasured anions have risen; equivalently, corrected HCO₃⁻ = 18 + 18 = 36 (> 26). Both point to a coexisting metabolic alkalosis (vomiting, NG suction, diuretics).

**Why not the others:**
- A – an AG of 30 is clearly raised; a pure hyperchloraemic acidosis has a normal AG and a delta ratio < 0.4.
- C – a pure HAGMA gives a ratio of 0.8–2.0, i.e. HCO₃⁻ would be about 6–10 mmol/L here.
- D – the AG can exceed 25 mmol/L, for example in lactic acidosis or toxic alcohol poisoning (often > 30).
- E – combined HAGMA + NAGMA gives a ratio of 0.4–0.8, with HCO₃⁻ falling more than the AG rises.

**Ref:** Rastegar A, J Am Soc Nephrol 2007;18:2429–2431; Berend K et al., N Engl J Med 2014;371:1434–1445.

</details>

**4.** Regarding expected compensation in acid–base disorders:

- A. In chronic respiratory acidosis, HCO₃⁻ rises by about 3.5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg rise in PaCO₂. — T / F
- B. In acute respiratory alkalosis, HCO₃⁻ falls by about 5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg fall in PaCO₂. — T / F
- C. Compensation for a metabolic acidosis may return pH to above 7.40. — T / F
- D. Renal compensation for a respiratory disorder is complete within 6 hours. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – once renal adaptation is complete (3–5 days), HCO₃⁻ rises by ~3.5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg rise in PaCO₂; the acute value is only ~1.
- B (False) – acute respiratory alkalosis lowers HCO₃⁻ by ~**2** mmol/L per 10 mmHg; 4–5 mmol/L is the chronic value.
- C (False) – compensation never fully corrects and never overshoots; a pH > 7.40 in a metabolic acidosis means a second, primary alkalosis.
- D (False) – renal compensation takes **3–5 days**; it is respiratory compensation of a metabolic disorder that starts within minutes to hours.

**Ref:** Berend K, de Vries AP, Gans RO, N Engl J Med 2014;371:1434–1445.

</details>

**5.** An arterial sample shows PaCO₂ 48 mmHg and HCO₃⁻ 36 mmol/L. Using the Henderson equation, which pH is internally consistent with these values?

- A. 7.50
- B. 7.60
- C. 7.40
- D. 7.30
- E. 7.36

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Henderson: [H⁺] = 24 × 48 / 36 = **32 nmol/L**, 8 below 40 → pH ≈ 7.48–7.49. Henderson–Hasselbalch: 36 / (0.03 × 48) = 36 / 1.44 = 25; log 25 = 1.40 → **pH 7.50**. This is a metabolic alkalosis with appropriate compensation (expected PaCO₂ = 40 + 0.7 × 12 ≈ 48 mmHg).

**Why not the others:**
- B – pH 7.60 ([H⁺] 25 nmol/L) would need HCO₃⁻ of ~46 mmol/L at this PaCO₂.
- C – pH 7.40 ([H⁺] 40) would need HCO₃⁻ of ~29 mmol/L at this PaCO₂.
- D – pH 7.30 ([H⁺] 50) would need HCO₃⁻ of ~23 mmol/L, which is not an alkalosis.
- E – pH 7.36 ([H⁺] ~44) would need HCO₃⁻ of ~26 mmol/L.

**Ref:** Berend K, de Vries AP, Gans RO, N Engl J Med 2014;371:1434–1445.

</details>

**6.** Regarding the anion gap:

- A. A fall in albumin of 10 g/L lowers the anion gap by about 2.5 mmol/L. — T / F
- B. Hypoalbuminaemia lowers the anion gap. — T / F
- C. IgG paraproteinaemia and lithium intoxication can lower the anion gap. — T / F
- D. Diarrhoea is a typical cause of high anion gap metabolic acidosis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – Figge's correction is 0.25 mmol/L per g/L, i.e. ~2.5 mmol/L per 10 g/L fall in albumin.
- B (True) – albumin is the largest unmeasured anion, so hypoalbuminaemia lowers the AG and can hide a HAGMA.
- C (True) – cationic IgG paraproteins and lithium are unmeasured cations that lower the AG; bromide/iodide do so by causing spurious hyperchloraemia.
- D (False) – diarrhoea loses HCO₃⁻ while Cl⁻ is retained, so it causes a normal-AG (hyperchloraemic) acidosis with a negative urine anion gap.

**Ref:** Kraut JA, Madias NE, Clin J Am Soc Nephrol 2007;2:162–174; Figge J et al., Crit Care Med 1998;26:1807–1810.

</details>

**7.** A patient with long-standing COPD has PaCO₂ 70 mmHg and HCO₃⁻ 34 mmol/L. The best description is:

- A. Acute respiratory acidosis
- B. Primary metabolic alkalosis with respiratory compensation
- C. Chronic (compensated) respiratory acidosis
- D. Acute respiratory acidosis with metabolic acidosis
- E. Chronic respiratory acidosis with an additional metabolic acidosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** PaCO₂ is 30 mmHg above 40. Chronic compensation predicts HCO₃⁻ = 24 + 3.5 × 3 = **34.5**, which matches the measured 34. pH = 6.1 + log(34 / (0.03 × 70)) = 6.1 + log 16.2 ≈ **7.31**: an acidaemia with appropriate chronic renal compensation.

**Why not the others:**
- A – acute compensation predicts HCO₃⁻ ≈ 24 + 1 × 3 = 27 and pH ≈ 7.21.
- B – the patient is acidaemic; compensation for a metabolic alkalosis would raise PaCO₂ to only ~47 mmHg (40 + 0.7 × 10), not 70.
- D – an added metabolic acidosis would push HCO₃⁻ below the acute prediction (27), not up to 34.
- E – HCO₃⁻ matches the chronic prediction, so there is no extra metabolic acidosis.

**Ref:** Berend K, de Vries AP, Gans RO, N Engl J Med 2014;371:1434–1445.

</details>

**8.** Regarding the delta ratio (ΔAG/ΔHCO₃⁻):

- A. In diabetic ketoacidosis it is often close to 1 because ketoanions are lost in the urine. — T / F
- B. In lactic acidosis it is typically higher, around 1.6. — T / F
- C. A ratio of 0.3 suggests a pure lactic acidosis. — T / F
- D. A ratio above 2 may reflect a pre-existing high bicarbonate from chronic respiratory acidosis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – ketoanions are filtered and excreted in the urine with Na⁺/K⁺, and Cl⁻ is retained instead, so the AG rises roughly as much as HCO₃⁻ falls (ratio ~1).
- B (True) – lactate stays in the plasma, while part of the H⁺ is buffered inside cells and bone, so HCO₃⁻ falls less than the AG rises (~1.6).
- C (False) – a ratio < 0.4 means a pure hyperchloraemic NAGMA; a pure lactic acidosis gives 0.8–2.0.
- D (True) – a high baseline HCO₃⁻ (compensated chronic hypercapnia) makes the ratio > 2 without a true metabolic alkalosis.

**EDIC trap:** always ask about the patient's baseline HCO₃⁻ before diagnosing a hidden metabolic alkalosis.

**Ref:** Rastegar A, J Am Soc Nephrol 2007;18:2429–2431.

</details>

**9.** A patient has a pure metabolic acidosis with HCO₃⁻ 8 mmol/L. The expected PaCO₂ with appropriate compensation is closest to:

- A. 5.3 kPa
- B. 2.7 kPa
- C. 3.6 kPa
- D. 4.5 kPa
- E. 1.9 kPa

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Winter's formula: 1.5 × 8 + 8 = **20 ± 2 mmHg**; 20 / 7.5 = **2.7 kPa** (range ≈ 2.4–2.9 kPa). The kPa shortcut HCO₃⁻/5 + 1 = 2.6 kPa gives the same answer.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 5.3 kPa (40 mmHg) is a normal PaCO₂; in this setting it would mean a severe coexisting respiratory acidosis.
- C – 3.6 kPa (27 mmHg) is the expected value for HCO₃⁻ ~13; here it would mean inadequate compensation.
- D – 4.5 kPa (34 mmHg) is far above the expected range, i.e. a respiratory acidosis.
- E – 1.9 kPa (14 mmHg) is below the expected range and would mean an additional respiratory alkalosis.

**EDIC trap:** questions mix units; 1 kPa = 7.5 mmHg.

**Ref:** Albert MS, Dell RB, Winters RW, Ann Intern Med 1967;66:312–322.

</details>

**10.** A ventilated patient has pH 7.49, PaCO₂ 30 mmHg and HCO₃⁻ 22 mmol/L, measured 2 hours after an increase in minute ventilation. Which statements are correct?

- A. The HCO₃⁻ is consistent with acute compensation. — T / F
- B. The primary disorder is respiratory alkalosis. — T / F
- C. With chronic compensation, HCO₃⁻ would be expected to be about 14 mmol/L. — T / F
- D. The primary disorder is metabolic alkalosis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – acute respiratory alkalosis lowers HCO₃⁻ by ~2 mmol/L per 10 mmHg: 24 − 2 × 1 = 22, which matches. Two hours is too short for renal compensation.
- B (True) – alkalaemia (pH 7.49) with a low PaCO₂ and only a slightly low HCO₃⁻ is a primary respiratory alkalosis (iatrogenic hyperventilation). Check: 22 / (0.03 × 30) = 24.4; log 24.4 = 1.39 → pH 7.49 ✓.
- C (False) – chronic compensation (~4 per 10 mmHg) predicts HCO₃⁻ ≈ 20 mmol/L, not 14.
- D (False) – a metabolic alkalosis would have a raised HCO₃⁻ and a compensatory rise, not a fall, in PaCO₂.

**Ref:** Berend K, de Vries AP, Gans RO, N Engl J Med 2014;371:1434–1445.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A septic patient has pH 7.19, PaCO₂ 38 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 14 mmol/L, Na⁺ 140, Cl⁻ 104 and albumin 20 g/L. Work through the blood gas step by step and interpret it.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Acidaemia with low HCO₃⁻ → primary metabolic acidosis.
- Winter's formula: expected PaCO₂ = 1.5 × 14 + 8 = 29 ± 2 (27–31 mmHg); measured 38 mmHg is too high → coexisting respiratory acidosis (fatigue, sedation, impending respiratory failure).
- AG = 140 − (104 + 14) = 22 mmol/L.
- Albumin-corrected AG = 22 + 0.25 × (40 − 20) = 27 mmol/L → HAGMA (e.g. lactate).
- Delta ratio = (27 − 12) / (24 − 14) = 1.5 → within 0.8–2.0 = pure HAGMA, no additional NAGMA or metabolic alkalosis.
- Conclusion: high anion gap metabolic acidosis with an inadequate respiratory response (mixed metabolic + respiratory acidosis); interpret ranges, not decimals.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why the uncorrected anion gap can be misleading in the critically ill, and how to avoid this pitfall.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- AG represents unmeasured anions, the largest part of which is albumin (negatively charged at physiological pH).
- Hypoalbuminaemia is almost universal in ICU patients (Fencl, AJRCCM 2000) and lowers the AG by ~2.5 mmol/L per 10 g/L fall in albumin.
- Hence a "normal" AG may hide a HAGMA (uncorrected AG missed most hidden gap acidoses in patients with normal HCO₃⁻).
- Correct: AG + 0.25 × (40 − albumin g/L) (Figge, Crit Care Med 1998); after correction the AG performs reliably.
- Other causes of a falsely low AG: IgG paraproteinaemia, lithium, bromide/iodide.
- Reference ranges are lower with ion-selective electrodes (~3–11); each laboratory should use its own range; use the corrected AG also for the delta ratio.

</details>


## W05 · D2 – Stewart approach: SID, ATOT, pCO₂; hyperchloraemic and lactic acidosis

**1.** Plasma values: Na⁺ 140, K⁺ 4, Cl⁻ 112, lactate 2 mmol/L. Ignoring Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺, the apparent strong ion difference (SIDa) is:

- A. 34 mmol/L
- B. 24 mmol/L
- C. 28 mmol/L
- D. 42 mmol/L
- E. 30 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** SIDa = (Na⁺ + K⁺) − (Cl⁻ + lactate⁻) = (140 + 4) − (112 + 2) = 144 − 114 = **30 mmol/L**. Normal plasma SID is ~40–42 mmol/L, so a fall of ~10 mmol/L means a metabolic acidosis (mainly hyperchloraemic, with a small lactate contribution).

**Why not the others:**
- A – 34 comes from adding lactate as if it were a cation instead of subtracting it.
- B – 24 is a normal HCO₃⁻; HCO₃⁻ is part of the effective SID (SIDe = HCO₃⁻ + A⁻), not the SIDa.
- C – 28 is Na⁺ − Cl⁻, the bedside surrogate, which ignores K⁺ and lactate.
- D – 42 is the normal SID of healthy plasma, not this patient's value.

**Ref:** Kellum JA, Crit Care 2000;4:6–14; Fencl V et al., Am J Respir Crit Care Med 2000;162:2246–2251.

</details>

**2.** According to the Stewart (physicochemical) approach:

- A. Hypoalbuminaemia causes a metabolic alkalosis. — T / F
- B. The strong ion difference of 0.9% saline is zero. — T / F
- C. PaCO₂, strong ion difference and total weak acids (ATOT) are the independent determinants of plasma pH. — T / F
- D. Bicarbonate is a dependent variable. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – albumin is a weak acid (part of ATOT); a low ATOT is alkalinising, worth ~+3 mmol/L of BE per 10 g/L below 40.
- B (True) – Na⁺ 154 − Cl⁻ 154 = 0; large volumes dilute plasma SID (~40) towards 0, causing a hyperchloraemic acidosis.
- C (True) – the three independent variables are PaCO₂, SID and ATOT.
- D (True) – HCO₃⁻ (like H⁺ and OH⁻) is a dependent variable; it changes only as a result of changes in PaCO₂, SID or ATOT.

**EDIC trap:** all-true K′ items exist; judge each statement on its own merits.

**Ref:** Kellum JA, Crit Care 2000;4:6–14; Stewart PA, Can J Physiol Pharmacol 1983;61:1444–1461.

</details>

**3.** Which of the following is a cause of type B lactic acidosis?

- A. Cardiogenic shock
- B. Carbon monoxide poisoning
- C. Severe anaemia
- D. Metformin accumulation
- E. Hypovolaemic shock

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Metformin accumulation (AKI, overdose) inhibits mitochondrial respiration and hepatic gluconeogenesis from lactate. Lactate therefore rises without tissue hypoxia, which is type B lactic acidosis (metformin-associated lactic acidosis). Severe cases are treated with haemodialysis, which removes both metformin and lactate.

**Why not the others:**
- A – cardiogenic shock causes tissue hypoperfusion, i.e. type A.
- B – carbon monoxide reduces oxygen-carrying capacity (and impairs cytochrome oxidase), so tissue O₂ delivery is inadequate: type A.
- C – severe anaemia lowers arterial O₂ content and delivery: type A.
- E – hypovolaemic shock causes hypoperfusion: type A.

**EDIC trap:** the A/B classification is imperfect; in sepsis, hypoperfusion, adrenergic glycolysis and reduced hepatic clearance coexist.

**Ref:** Kraut JA, Madias NE, N Engl J Med 2014;371:2309–2319.

</details>

**4.** Regarding sodium bicarbonate in severe metabolic acidaemia:

- A. In BICARICU-2, bicarbonate did not reduce the use of kidney replacement therapy. — T / F
- B. BICAR-ICU enrolled patients with pH ≤ 7.20, PaCO₂ ≤ 45 mmHg and HCO₃⁻ ≤ 20 mmol/L. — T / F
- C. BICAR-ICU showed a significant reduction in its primary composite outcome in the overall population. — T / F
- D. BICARICU-2, in patients with AKI stage 2–3, showed no difference in 90-day mortality. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – BICARICU-2 **did** reduce kidney replacement therapy by day 28 (35 % vs 50 %).
- B (True) – BICAR-ICU enrolled patients with pH ≤ 7.20, PaCO₂ ≤ 45 mmHg and HCO₃⁻ ≤ 20 mmol/L, plus SOFA ≥ 4 or lactate ≥ 2 mmol/L.
- C (False) – the primary composite outcome (death by day 28 or organ failure on day 7) was 66 % vs 71 %, not significant. Benefit was seen only in the prespecified AKIN 2–3 stratum (70 % vs 82 %).
- D (True) – in BICARICU-2 (n = 640, AKI KDIGO 2–3), 90-day mortality was 62.1 % vs 61.7 %, no difference.

**EDIC trap:** bicarbonate reduces dialysis, not death.

**Ref:** Jaber S et al., Lancet 2018;392:31–40; Jung B et al., JAMA 2025 (doi:10.1001/jama.2025.20231).

</details>

**5.** A patient has SIDa 38 mmol/L, HCO₃⁻ 14 mmol/L and an albumin + phosphate charge (A⁻) of 10 mmol/L. The strong ion gap and its meaning are:

- A. 14 mmol/L – unmeasured anions present
- B. 14 mmol/L – unmeasured cations present
- C. 0 mmol/L – no unmeasured anions
- D. −14 mmol/L – laboratory error
- E. 24 mmol/L – hyperchloraemic acidosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** SIDe = HCO₃⁻ + A⁻ = 14 + 10 = 24 mmol/L. SIG = SIDa − SIDe = 38 − 24 = **14 mmol/L**. The SIG is normally ≈ 0, so a positive value means unmeasured strong anions (ketones, toxic alcohol metabolites, sulphates, "sepsis anions").

**Why not the others:**
- B – unmeasured cations would make SIDe exceed SIDa, giving a **negative** SIG.
- C – SIG is 0 only when SIDa equals SIDe, which is not the case here.
- D – the sign is wrong: SIG = SIDa − SIDe, not SIDe − SIDa.
- E – 24 is the SIDe, and the SIDa of 38 is near normal, so there is no hyperchloraemic acidosis.

**EDIC trap:** the SIG is an anion gap with the albumin and phosphate correction already built in.

**Ref:** Kellum JA, Crit Care 2000;4:6–14; Fencl V et al., Am J Respir Crit Care Med 2000;162:2246–2251.

</details>

**6.** Regarding crystalloids and the strong ion difference (in vivo, after metabolism of organic anions):

- A. Large-volume 0.9% saline dilutes plasma SID and causes acidosis. — T / F
- B. Plasma-Lyte 148 has an effective SID of approximately 50 mmol/L. — T / F
- C. Hyperchloraemic acidosis is characterised by a raised anion gap. — T / F
- D. Ringer's lactate has an effective SID of approximately 50 mmol/L. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – saline has an SID of 0, so large volumes dilute plasma SID (~40) and cause a hyperchloraemic (dilutional) acidosis.
- B (True) – Plasma-Lyte 148: Na 140 + K 5 + Mg 3 − Cl 98 ≈ 50 mmol/L, once acetate and gluconate are metabolised.
- C (False) – hyperchloraemic acidosis has a **normal** AG, because Cl⁻ replaces the lost HCO₃⁻.
- D (False) – Ringer's lactate has an effective SID of ~**28** mmol/L (130 + 4 + 3 − 109, once lactate is metabolised).

**EDIC trap:** fluids with an SID close to plasma HCO₃⁻ (~24–28) disturb acid–base status least. In SMART, balanced crystalloids reduced MAKE30 (14.3 % vs 15.4 %).

**Ref:** Kellum JA, Crit Care 2000;4:6–14; Semler MW et al. (SMART), N Engl J Med 2018;378:829–839.

</details>

**7.** A patient on adrenaline 0.3 µg/kg/min after cardiac surgery has lactate 6 mmol/L, capillary refill 2 s, ScvO₂ 76 % and falling vasopressor requirements. The most likely mechanism of the hyperlactataemia is:

- A. Global tissue hypoxia
- B. β₂-adrenergic stimulation of aerobic glycolysis
- C. Thiamine deficiency
- D. D-lactic acidosis
- E. Mesenteric ischaemia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Adrenaline stimulates β₂-receptors. This drives Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and aerobic glycolysis in skeletal muscle, and the pyruvate surplus is converted to lactate (type B). A capillary refill of 2 s, ScvO₂ of 76 % and falling vasopressor needs show adequate perfusion, and lactate usually falls as adrenaline is weaned.

**Why not the others:**
- A – normal capillary refill, normal ScvO₂ and improving haemodynamics argue against global tissue hypoxia.
- C – thiamine deficiency causes type B lactate, but nothing here suggests it (no alcohol, malnutrition or refeeding), and adrenaline explains the picture.
- D – D-lactate needs a short bowel or propylene glycol and is not detected by standard assays; a measured lactate of 6 is L-lactate.
- E – mesenteric ischaemia should be considered if lactate keeps rising with abdominal signs, but the improving picture argues against it.

**Ref:** Kraut JA, Madias NE, N Engl J Med 2014;371:2309–2319.

</details>

**8.** Regarding the urine anion gap in normal anion gap acidosis:

- A. It is typically negative in distal renal tubular acidosis. — T / F
- B. It is calculated as urine Na⁺ + K⁺ − Cl⁻. — T / F
- C. A positive value indicates high urinary ammonium excretion. — T / F
- D. It is typically positive in diarrhoea. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – in distal RTA, NH₄⁺ excretion is impaired, so the UAG is **positive**.
- B (True) – UAG = U-Na⁺ + U-K⁺ − U-Cl⁻, an indirect estimate of NH₄⁺, which is excreted with Cl⁻.
- C (False) – a positive value means **low** NH₄⁺ excretion; abundant NH₄Cl makes the UAG negative.
- D (False) – in diarrhoea the kidneys respond appropriately and excrete abundant NH₄⁺, so the UAG is negative.

**EDIC trap:** the UAG is unreliable in polyuria, ketonuria (or other unmeasured urinary anions) and when U-Na⁺ is < 20 mmol/L.

**Ref:** Kraut JA, Madias NE, Clin J Am Soc Nephrol 2012;7:671–679.

</details>

**9.** After 5 L of 0.9 % saline: pH 7.30, PaCO₂ 34 mmHg, Na⁺ 140, Cl⁻ 116, HCO₃⁻ 16 mmol/L, albumin 40 g/L, lactate 1.2. The disorder is best described as:

- A. Mixed high anion gap acidosis and metabolic alkalosis
- B. Lactic acidosis type A
- C. Respiratory acidosis with metabolic compensation
- D. High anion gap metabolic acidosis, inadequately compensated
- E. Normal anion gap (hyperchloraemic) metabolic acidosis, appropriately compensated

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** AG = 140 − (116 + 16) = **8** mmol/L. Albumin is normal, so no correction is needed, and this is a normal-AG acidosis; Na⁺ − Cl⁻ = 24 shows a low SID from the saline. Winter's formula: 1.5 × 16 + 8 = 32 ± 2 (30–34) → PaCO₂ 34 is appropriate. Check: 16 / (0.03 × 34) = 15.7; log 15.7 = 1.20 → pH 7.30 ✓.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the AG is normal, so there is no HAGMA, and the delta ratio does not apply.
- B – lactate is 1.2 mmol/L (normal) and the AG is not raised.
- C – acidaemia with a low HCO₃⁻ and a low PaCO₂ is a primary metabolic disorder; a respiratory acidosis would have a high PaCO₂.
- D – the AG is normal and PaCO₂ is within the Winter range.

**Ref:** Kraut JA, Madias NE, Clin J Am Soc Nephrol 2012;7:671–679; Semler MW et al., N Engl J Med 2018;378:829–839.

</details>

**10.** Regarding weak acids (ATOT) and acid–base analysis in the ICU:

- A. In hypoalbuminaemia, the anion gap should be corrected before interpretation. — T / F

**7.** A patient with COPD and chronic hypercapnia (baseline PaCO₂ 65 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 32 mmol/L) is intubated and ventilated to PaCO₂ 40 mmHg. Two hours later: pH 7.53, HCO₃⁻ 32 mmol/L. The best management is:

- A. Start hydrochloric acid infusion
- B. Ignore – the alkalosis is harmless
- C. Reduce minute ventilation, targeting the patient's usual PaCO₂
- D. Increase minute ventilation further
- E. Give sodium bicarbonate

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** This is post-hypercapnic alkalosis. HCO₃⁻ retained during chronic hypercapnia (32) persists while PaCO₂ has been corrected acutely: pH = 6.1 + log(32 / (0.03 × 40)) = 6.1 + 1.43 = **7.53**. Reducing minute ventilation towards the patient's usual PaCO₂ normalises pH; also correct Cl⁻ and K⁺ deficits.

**Why not the others:**
- A – HCl is invasive and reserved for severe refractory alkalosis; here the cause is the ventilator setting.
- B – alkalaemia causes hypokalaemia, low ionised calcium, arrhythmias, reduced cerebral blood flow and seizures.
- D – lowering PaCO₂ further would worsen the alkalaemia.
- E – bicarbonate would worsen the alkalosis.
