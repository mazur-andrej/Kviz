## W05 · D1 – Acid–base: Henderson–Hasselbalch, compensation, anion gap, delta ratio

**1.** A septic patient has Na⁺ 136 mmol/L, Cl⁻ 100 mmol/L, HCO₃⁻ 16 mmol/L and albumin 24 g/L. The albumin-corrected anion gap is approximately:

- A. 28 mmol/L
- B. 30 mmol/L
- C. 24 mmol/L
- D. 20 mmol/L
- E. 14 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

AG = 136 − (100 + 16) = 20; correction 0.25 × (40 − 24) = 4 → **24**.

</details>

**2.** A patient with metabolic acidosis has HCO₃⁻ 12 mmol/L and PaCO₂ 34 mmHg (4.5 kPa). Which statements are correct?

- A. The expected PaCO₂ by Winter's formula is approximately 26 ± 2 mmHg. — T / F
- B. The calculated pH is approximately 7.30. — T / F
- C. Respiratory compensation is appropriate. — T / F
- D. The measured PaCO₂ indicates a coexisting respiratory acidosis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

1.5 × 12 + 8 = 26 (24–28); 34 > 28. pH = 6.1 + log(12 / 1.02) = 6.1 + 1.07 ≈ 7.17, not 7.30.

</details>

**3.** A patient with normal albumin has an anion gap of 30 mmol/L and HCO₃⁻ of 18 mmol/L. The most likely interpretation is:

- A. Pure hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis
- B. High anion gap metabolic acidosis with a coexisting metabolic alkalosis
- C. Pure high anion gap metabolic acidosis
- D. Laboratory error, because the anion gap cannot exceed 25 mmol/L
- E. Combined high and normal anion gap metabolic acidosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

Delta ratio = (30 − 12) / (24 − 18) = 18 / 6 = **3** (> 2) → associated metabolic alkalosis (e.g. vomiting, NG tube, diuretics).

</details>

**4.** Regarding expected compensation in acid–base disorders:

- A. In chronic respiratory acidosis, HCO₃⁻ rises by about 3.5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg rise in PaCO₂. — T / F
- B. In acute respiratory alkalosis, HCO₃⁻ falls by about 5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg fall in PaCO₂. — T / F
- C. Compensation for a metabolic acidosis may return pH to above 7.40. — T / F
- D. Renal compensation for a respiratory disorder is complete within 6 hours. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

Acute respiratory alkalosis: ↓ 2 mmol/L per 10 mmHg; 4–5 mmol/L is the chronic value (B). Compensation never overcompensates (C); renal compensation takes 3–5 days (D).

</details>

**5.** An arterial sample shows PaCO₂ 48 mmHg and HCO₃⁻ 36 mmol/L. Using the Henderson equation, which pH is internally consistent with these values?

- A. 7.50
- B. 7.60
- C. 7.40
- D. 7.30
- E. 7.36

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

[H⁺] = 24 × 48 / 36 = **32 nmol/L** → 8 nmol/L less than 40 → pH ≈ 7.48–7.50 (exactly 7.50). This is a metabolic alkalosis with appropriate compensation (40 + 0.7 × 12 ≈ 48).

</details>

**O1. (open)** A septic patient has pH 7.19, PaCO₂ 38 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 14 mmol/L, Na⁺ 140, Cl⁻ 104 and albumin 20 g/L. Work through the blood gas step by step and interpret it.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Acidaemia with low HCO₃⁻ → primary metabolic acidosis.
- Winter's formula: expected PaCO₂ = 1.5 × 14 + 8 = 29 ± 2 (27–31 mmHg); measured 38 mmHg is too high → coexisting respiratory acidosis (fatigue, sedation, impending respiratory failure).
- AG = 140 − (104 + 14) = 22 mmol/L.
- Albumin-corrected AG = 22 + 0.25 × (40 − 20) = 27 mmol/L → HAGMA (e.g. lactate).
- Delta ratio = (27 − 12) / (24 − 14) = 1.5 → within 0.8–2.0 = pure HAGMA, no additional NAGMA or metabolic alkalosis.
- Conclusion: high anion gap metabolic acidosis with an inadequate respiratory response (mixed metabolic + respiratory acidosis); interpret ranges, not decimals.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why the uncorrected anion gap can be misleading in the critically ill, and how to avoid this pitfall.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- AG represents unmeasured anions, the largest part of which is albumin (negatively charged at physiological pH).
- Hypoalbuminaemia is almost universal in ICU patients (Fencl, AJRCCM 2000) and lowers the AG by ~2.5 mmol/L per 10 g/L fall in albumin.
- Hence a "normal" AG may hide a HAGMA (uncorrected AG missed most hidden gap acidoses in patients with normal HCO₃⁻).
- Correct: AG + 0.25 × (40 − albumin g/L) (Figge, Crit Care Med 1998); after correction the AG performs reliably.
- Other causes of a falsely low AG: IgG paraproteinaemia, lithium, bromide/iodide.
- Reference ranges are lower with ion-selective electrodes (~3–11); each laboratory should use its own range; use the corrected AG also for the delta ratio.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Using the Stewart approach, explain why a patient resuscitated with large volumes of 0.9 % saline develops a metabolic acidosis, and why the choice of crystalloid matters.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Plasma pH is determined by three independent variables: pCO₂, SID and ATOT; HCO₃⁻ is a dependent variable.
- Normal plasma SID is ~40–42 mmol/L; ↓ SID = acidosis.
- 0.9 % NaCl has an SID of 0 (Na 154 − Cl 154); large volumes dilute plasma SID towards 0 → hyperchloraemic (dilutional) acidosis with a normal anion gap.
- Ringer's lactate (~28) and Plasma-Lyte 148 (~50) have higher in vivo SIDs because organic anions are metabolised; fluids with an SID close to plasma HCO₃⁻ (~24–28) change acid–base status the least.
- SIG remains ≈ 0 (no unmeasured anions), distinguishing it from lactic/ketoacidosis.
- Clinical relevance: SMART (NEJM 2018) – balanced crystalloids reduced MAKE30 (14.3 % vs 15.4 %).

</details>

**O2. (open)** A patient in septic shock has pH 7.15, PaCO₂ 35 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 12 mmol/L, lactate 8 mmol/L and KDIGO stage 3 AKI. Discuss whether you would give sodium bicarbonate, citing the evidence.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Priority is treating the cause of lactic acidosis (perfusion, source control); bicarbonate is not used to improve haemodynamics in hypoperfusion-induced lactic acidaemia (SSC 2021).
- SSC 2021: consider bicarbonate in septic shock with pH ≤ 7.2 and AKI (AKIN 2–3) – weak recommendation, low-quality evidence.
- BICAR-ICU (Lancet 2018, n = 389): primary composite 66 % vs 71 %, NS; lower composite in the prespecified AKIN 2–3 stratum (70 % vs 82 %); less RRT overall.
- BICARICU-2 (JAMA 2025, n = 640, AKI KDIGO 2–3): no difference in 90-day mortality (62.1 % vs 61.7 %) but less KRT by day 28 (35 % vs 50 %).
- This patient meets the trial criteria (pH ≤ 7.20, PaCO₂ ≤ 45, HCO₃⁻ ≤ 20, AKI) → bicarbonate is reasonable to reduce the need for RRT, not to reduce mortality.
- Risks/monitoring: hypernatraemia, hyperosmolality, ↓ iCa²⁺, CO₂ generation requiring increased ventilation.

</details>

**O3. (open)** A 64-year-old with faecal peritonitis has received 5 L of 0.9 % saline. Ventilated. pH 7.14, pCO₂ 5.5 kPa (41 mmHg), HCO₃⁻ 14 mmol/L, BE −13 mmol/L, Na⁺ 140, Cl⁻ 114, albumin 22 g/L, lactate 6 mmol/L. Analyse the acid–base status step by step (bedside Stewart) and state the clinical consequences.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Consistency check: 6.1 + log(14 / (0.23 × 5.5)) = 6.1 + log 11.1 = **7.14** ✓.
- Step 1: Na − Cl = 26 → 26 − 34 = **BE −8** → hyperchloraemic (low-SID) acidosis – saline.
- Step 2: albumin 22 g/L → 0.3 × 18 = **+5** (≈ 5.4) → hypoalbuminaemic alkalosis.
- Step 3: predicted BE −8 + 5 = **−3**; measured −13 → **~10 mmol/L of strong anions**: lactate 6 + **~4–5 unmeasured** (check ketones, creatinine/AKI anions, phosphate, osmolal gap).
- Step 4: expected pCO₂ = 14/5 + 1 = **3.8 ± 0.3 kPa** (Winters 1.5 × 14 + 8 = 29 ± 2 mmHg); actual 5.5 kPa → **superimposed respiratory acidosis** (inadequate minute ventilation / ARDS / dead space).

</details>
