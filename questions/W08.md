## W08 · D1 – Arrhythmias in the ICU: AF, VT/electrical storm, bradyarrhythmias

**1.** A 68-year-old man with septic shock on noradrenaline develops new atrial fibrillation at 165/min. BP falls to 70/40 mmHg with rising lactate despite adequate filling. According to ERC 2025, the most appropriate immediate step is:

- A. IV digoxin loading
- B. Synchronised cardioversion with an initial shock at maximum defibrillator output
- C. Synchronised cardioversion starting at 50 J with stepwise escalation
- D. Wait 48 hours for spontaneous conversion
- E. IV diltiazem bolus

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** AF with shock, ischaemia or acute heart failure is an indication for emergency synchronised electrical cardioversion (ESC 2024, class I C). ERC 2025 changed the energy advice for AF: the first synchronised shock should be delivered at **maximum defibrillator output** instead of escalating, because the first-shock success is higher and repeated shocks are avoided. If it fails, give amiodarone 300 mg IV and shock again.

**Why not the others:**
- A – digoxin acts slowly (hours) and does not treat shock caused by the arrhythmia.
- C – a low start with stepwise escalation is the old approach; ERC 2025 recommends maximum energy for AF (70–120 J is for flutter/SVT).
- D – "wait-and-see" within 48 h is only for haemodynamically stable patients.
- E – diltiazem is negatively inotropic and a vasodilator, so it would worsen the hypotension.

**Ref:** Soar J et al., ERC Guidelines 2025 Adult ALS, Resuscitation 2025;215:110769; Van Gelder IC et al., 2024 ESC AF Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2024;45:3314–3414.

</details>

**2.** Regarding rate control of atrial fibrillation according to the 2024 ESC guidelines:

- A. Lenient rate control with a resting heart rate < 110/min should be considered as the initial target. — T / F
- B. Diltiazem and verapamil should be avoided when LVEF is ≤ 40 %. — T / F
- C. Digoxin can be used irrespective of left ventricular ejection fraction. — T / F
- D. In haemodynamic instability, IV amiodarone, digoxin, esmolol or landiolol may be considered. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – ESC 2024 recommends lenient rate control (resting HR < 110/min) as the initial target (IIa B), based on RACE II.
- B (True) – non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers are negatively inotropic and can cause decompensation in HFrEF, so they are used only if LVEF > 40%.
- C (True) – digoxin (like beta-blockers) can be used at any LVEF; it is slower and less effective at high sympathetic tone, but it does not lower blood pressure.
- D (True) – in haemodynamic instability or severely reduced LVEF, IV amiodarone, digoxin, esmolol or landiolol may be considered (IIb B).

**EDIC trap:** All four are true – a K′ item can have four true statements.

**Ref:** Van Gelder IC et al., 2024 ESC AF Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2024;45:3314–3414; Van Gelder IC et al. (RACE II), N Engl J Med 2010;362:1363–1373.

</details>

**3.** In the STRESS-L trial of landiolol in septic shock with heart rate ≥ 95/min:

- A. There was no improvement in organ failure and the trial was stopped early with a possible signal of harm
- B. Landiolol significantly reduced 28-day mortality
- C. Landiolol significantly reduced the mean SOFA score
- D. Landiolol was compared with esmolol
- E. Only patients with atrial fibrillation were enrolled

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** STRESS-L (126 adults with septic shock, noradrenaline > 0.1 µg/kg/min for > 24 h, HR ≥ 95/min) randomised landiolol titrated to HR 80–94/min vs standard care. Mean SOFA over 14 days did not differ (8.8 vs 8.1). The trial was stopped early because benefit was unlikely; 28-day mortality was 37.1% vs 25.4% and 90-day mortality 43.5% vs 28.6% (not significant), a possible signal of harm.

**Why not the others:**
- B – 28-day mortality was numerically **higher** with landiolol, not lower.
- C – there was no difference in mean SOFA score, the primary outcome.
- D – the comparator was standard care, not esmolol.
- E – most patients had sinus tachycardia; the entry criterion was heart rate, not AF.

**EDIC trap:** Do not confuse STRESS-L with Morelli's single-centre esmolol trial (JAMA 2013), which suggested benefit.

**Ref:** Whitehouse T et al. (STRESS-L), JAMA 2023;330:1641–1652.

</details>

**4.** Concerning anticoagulation and cardioversion in atrial fibrillation (ESC 2024):

- A. The CHA₂DS₂-VA score adds one point for female sex. — T / F
- B. A score of ≥ 2 is an indicator to start oral anticoagulation. — T / F
- C. Early cardioversion without anticoagulation or TOE is acceptable if AF has lasted up to 72 hours. — T / F
- D. Atrial fibrillation triggered by a reversible precipitant never requires long-term anticoagulation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – CHA₂DS₂-**VA** (ESC 2024) dropped female sex; it is no longer counted as a risk factor.
- B (True) – a score ≥ 2 means oral anticoagulation is recommended (I C); a score of 1 means it should be considered (IIa C).
- C (False) – the threshold in ESC 2024 is **24 h**: early cardioversion without anticoagulation or TOE is not recommended if AF has lasted > 24 h (III C).
- D (False) – AF triggered by a reversible precipitant (e.g. sepsis, surgery) often recurs; long-term OAC should be **considered** in patients at increased thromboembolic risk (IIa C).

**EDIC trap:** Older sources use 48 h and CHA₂DS₂-VASc – ESC 2024 uses 24 h and CHA₂DS₂-VA.

**Ref:** Van Gelder IC et al., 2024 ESC AF Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2024;45:3314–3414.

</details>

**5.** A 74-year-old woman with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF 25 %) has haemodynamically stable recent-onset AF. Pharmacological cardioversion is requested. The most appropriate agent is:

- A. Vernakalant
- B. Verapamil
- C. Flecainide
- D. Propafenone
- E. Amiodarone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** In structural heart disease – HFrEF, coronary artery disease or severe LVH – IV amiodarone is the recommended agent for pharmacological cardioversion (ESC 2024, I A). Its effect is delayed (often 8–12 h or more), but it is safe in a low-EF heart and also slows the ventricular rate in the meantime. Watch for hypotension, bradycardia and QT prolongation with IV use.

**Why not the others:**
- A – vernakalant is contraindicated in HFrEF, recent ACS and severe aortic stenosis.
- B – verapamil is a rate-control drug, not a cardioversion drug, and is contraindicated with LVEF ≤ 40%.
- C – flecainide (class IC) is contraindicated in CAD and HFrEF because it is proarrhythmic in a scarred ventricle (CAST).
- D – propafenone (class IC) has the same contraindications as flecainide.

**Ref:** Van Gelder IC et al., 2024 ESC AF Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2024;45:3314–3414.

</details>

**6.** Regarding electrical storm (ESC 2022):

- A. It is defined as ≥ 3 episodes of sustained ventricular arrhythmia within 7 days, each requiring intervention. — T / F
- B. Catheter ablation is contraindicated during electrical storm. — T / F
- C. Sedation is part of the initial management. — T / F
- D. A non-selective beta-blocker such as propranolol combined with amiodarone is commonly used. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – electrical storm is ≥ 3 episodes of sustained ventricular arrhythmia within **24 hours**, separated by ≥ 5 min, each needing intervention (e.g. ICD shock or ATP).
- B (False) – catheter ablation is a key treatment of storm and is preferred in incessant monomorphic VT.
- C (True) – sedation breaks the cycle of shocks, fear and sympathetic drive; deep sedation or intubation is used if the storm is refractory.
- D (True) – non-selective beta-blockade (propranolol) plus amiodarone is the most common drug combination; landiolol is an option for amiodarone-resistant VT.

**EDIC trap:** "7 days" is a wrong definition; the window is 24 h.

**Ref:** Zeppenfeld K et al., 2022 ESC Guidelines for ventricular arrhythmias, Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997–4126.

</details>

**7.** A patient on haloperidol and ciprofloxacin with QTc 590 ms develops recurrent runs of torsade de pointes. Serum magnesium is normal. The first-line drug is:

- A. Adenosine
- B. Intravenous magnesium sulfate
- C. Procainamide
- D. Sotalol
- E. Amiodarone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** This is drug-induced long-QT torsade de pointes (haloperidol + ciprofloxacin, QTc 590 ms). IV magnesium sulfate (e.g. 2 g) stops and prevents recurrences even when serum magnesium is normal, probably by blocking early afterdepolarisations. Also correct K⁺ to high-normal and stop the QT-prolonging drugs; for recurrences raise the heart rate with isoprenaline or overdrive pacing.

**Why not the others:**
- A – adenosine is for re-entrant SVT and has no role in TdP.
- C – procainamide (class IA) prolongs the QT interval and can worsen TdP.
- D – sotalol has class III action and is itself a common cause of TdP.
- E – amiodarone prolongs QT further and is avoided in long-QT TdP.

**EDIC trap:** Unstable or pulseless TdP needs an unsynchronised shock (defibrillation).

**Ref:** Zeppenfeld K et al., 2022 ESC Guidelines for ventricular arrhythmias, Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997–4126.

</details>

**8.** Bradycardia with adverse features (ERC 2025):

- A. Transcutaneous pacing can be used as a bridge to transvenous pacing. — T / F
- B. The initial atropine dose is 500 µg IV, repeated up to a total of 3 mg. — T / F
- C. Isoprenaline or adrenaline are second-line options when atropine fails. — T / F
- D. Atropine is the drug of choice after heart transplantation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – transcutaneous pacing is a bridge when drugs fail and transvenous pacing is not yet available.
- B (True) – ERC 2025: atropine 500 µg IV, repeated every 3–5 min up to a total of 3 mg.
- C (True) – if atropine is ineffective, isoprenaline (start 5 µg/min) or adrenaline 2–10 µg/min are second-line drugs; aminophylline and glucagon are used in specific situations.
- D (False) – after heart transplantation the heart is denervated, so atropine does not work and can cause paradoxical high-degree AV block; aminophylline is the alternative.

**EDIC trap:** Atropine is also unlikely to work in high-degree AV block with broad QRS (block below the His bundle) – go to pacing.

**Ref:** Soar J et al., ERC Guidelines 2025 Adult ALS, Resuscitation 2025;215:110769; Glikson M et al., 2021 ESC Pacing Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2021;42:3427–3520.

</details>

**9.** In a large cohort of patients hospitalised with severe sepsis (Walkey, JAMA 2011), new-onset atrial fibrillation was associated with:

- A. No difference in stroke
- B. Higher in-hospital stroke and mortality
- C. A protective effect of anticoagulation on mortality
- D. Lower in-hospital mortality
- E. Reduced length of stay

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** In over 49,000 patients with severe sepsis, new-onset AF occurred in 5.9%. It was associated with more in-hospital ischaemic stroke (2.6% vs 0.6%) and higher in-hospital mortality (56% vs 39%). This is an observational association – NOAF is a marker of illness severity, not proven to cause the outcomes.

**Why not the others:**
- A – stroke was about four times more frequent with NOAF.
- C – the study did not show a protective effect of anticoagulation; ICU data do not support routine therapeutic anticoagulation in acute sepsis.
- D – mortality was higher, not lower.
- E – NOAF is associated with a longer, not shorter, stay.

**Ref:** Walkey AJ et al., JAMA 2011;306:2248–2254; Wetterslev M et al., Intensive Care Med 2019;45:928–938.

</details>

**10.** Regarding sustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (ESC 2022):

- A. In PROCAMIO, amiodarone terminated more tachycardias than procainamide. — T / F
- B. Haemodynamic instability is an indication for IV amiodarone before any attempt at cardioversion. — T / F
- C. A haemodynamically tolerated VT may be left untreated if blood pressure is normal. — T / F
- D. Procainamide should be avoided in severe heart failure and acute myocardial infarction. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – in PROCAMIO, IV **procainamide** terminated more tolerated wide-QRS tachycardias and caused fewer major cardiac adverse events than amiodarone.
- B (False) – unstable SMVT needs **immediate synchronised cardioversion**; drugs come after or if the shock fails.
- C (False) – even haemodynamically tolerated SMVT should be promptly terminated (cardioversion, antiarrhythmics or pacing), because it can deteriorate.
- D (True) – procainamide is negatively inotropic and hypotensive; avoid it in severe HF, acute MI and end-stage renal failure (active metabolite NAPA accumulates).

**Ref:** Ortiz M et al. (PROCAMIO), Eur Heart J 2017;38:1329–1335; Zeppenfeld K et al., 2022 ESC Guidelines for ventricular arrhythmias, Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997–4126.

</details>

**11.** A 72-year-old woman is in the ICU 2 days after a large intracerebral haemorrhage. She receives haloperidol for agitation and clarithromycin for pneumonia. K⁺ 3.1 mmol/L, Mg²⁺ 0.55 mmol/L, QTc 610 ms; she has repeated self-terminating runs of polymorphic VT with "twisting" QRS axis, each preceded by a pause, and is haemodynamically stable between runs. Which combination is most appropriate?

- A. Procainamide 10–15 mg/kg IV
- B. Adenosine 6 mg IV to clarify the rhythm
- C. Magnesium sulfate 2 g IV, potassium to high-normal, stop haloperidol and clarithromycin; isoprenaline or overdrive pacing if runs recur
- D. Amiodarone 300 mg IV, then infusion
- E. Synchronised cardioversion at 120 J and then sotalol

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Pause-dependent polymorphic VT with a twisting axis and QTc 610 ms is torsade de pointes. There are several additive causes: QT-prolonging drugs (haloperidol, clarithromycin), hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia and acute brain injury. Treatment is IV magnesium 2 g (works even at normal levels), K⁺ to high-normal (~4.5–5 mmol/L), stopping the drugs, and raising the heart rate (isoprenaline or overdrive pacing) to shorten QT if runs recur.

**Why not the others:**
- A – procainamide prolongs QT and can worsen TdP.
- B – adenosine is for re-entrant SVT; the rhythm here is already clear.
- D – amiodarone prolongs QT further.
- E – the patient is stable between self-terminating runs, and sotalol is a QT-prolonging drug.

**EDIC trap:** If TdP becomes pulseless or unstable, defibrillate (unsynchronised) – synchronisation often fails.

**Ref:** Zeppenfeld K et al., 2022 ESC Guidelines for ventricular arrhythmias, Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997–4126.

</details>

**12.** A 64-year-old man has repeated runs of polymorphic VT/VF on the day after primary PCI for an anterior STEMI. According to the 2022 ESC guidelines:

- A. IV lidocaine may be considered if beta-blockers and amiodarone are ineffective or amiodarone is contraindicated. — T / F
- B. An IV beta-blocker is recommended for recurrent polymorphic VT/VF unless contraindicated. — T / F
- C. Routine prophylactic antiarrhythmic drugs other than beta-blockers are recommended after ACS to prevent VT. — T / F
- D. Repeat urgent coronary angiography should be considered if ongoing ischaemia (e.g. stent thrombosis) is suspected. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – IV lidocaine may be considered (IIb) if beta-blockers and amiodarone fail or amiodarone is contraindicated; IV amiodarone itself is IIa.
- B (True) – IV beta-blockers are recommended (class I) for recurrent polymorphic VT/VF in STEMI, as they reduce sympathetic drive and ischaemia.
- C (False) – routine prophylactic antiarrhythmic drugs other than beta-blockers are **not** recommended after ACS (class III).
- D (True) – residual ischaemia (e.g. stent thrombosis) is the main driver of polymorphic VT/VF after MI; urgent repeat angiography/revascularisation and K⁺/Mg²⁺ repletion are class I.

**EDIC trap:** Polymorphic VT in ACS is ischaemic, not long-QT – the answer is revascularisation and beta-blockers, not magnesium alone.

**Ref:** Zeppenfeld K et al., 2022 ESC Guidelines for ventricular arrhythmias, Eur Heart J 2022;43:3997–4126 (Table 22, Figure 14).

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 70-year-old ventilated patient with septic shock on norepinephrine develops new AF at 140/min with stable blood pressure. Outline your management, including anticoagulation.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Haemodynamically stable – no immediate cardioversion; NOAF is a marker of severity.
- Treat triggers first: sepsis source control, volume status (hypovolaemia or overload), catecholamine dose, hypoxaemia/hypercapnia, acidosis, K⁺ and Mg²⁺, CVC tip in the RA.
- Wait-and-see within 48 h is reasonable (IIa B) – up to 83% revert spontaneously after treating the cause.
- Rate control target < 110/min; BB or digoxin; diltiazem/verapamil only if LVEF > 40%; with instability or low EF consider amiodarone, digoxin, esmolol or landiolol (IIb).
- Do not routinely beta-block compensatory sinus tachycardia (STRESS-L: no benefit, signal of harm).
- If becomes unstable: synchronised cardioversion with maximum energy (ERC 2025), then amiodarone 300 mg if it fails.
- Anticoagulation: CHA₂DS₂-VA; in acute sepsis benefit/risk unclear, no routine therapeutic anticoagulation; no elective cardioversion without OAC/TOE if AF > 24 h.
- After discharge consider long-term OAC in patients at increased risk (IIa C).

**O2. (open)** A patient with an ICD receives five shocks for monomorphic VT within 6 hours. Outline your management of this electrical storm.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Meets the definition: ≥ 3 sustained ventricular arrhythmia episodes in 24 h, separated by ≥ 5 min, each requiring intervention.
- Unstable VT → immediate synchronised cardioversion (unsynchronised if synchronisation fails).
- Sedation to break the shock–fear–sympathetic cycle; deep sedation/intubation if refractory.

## W08 · D2 – Pulmonary embolism: risk stratification, thrombolysis, catheters, ECMO, PERT

**1.** A 60-year-old woman with confirmed PE has BP 118/70 mmHg, HR 112/min, sPESI 2, RV/LV ratio 1.3 on CT and elevated troponin. According to ESC 2019 she is classified as:

- A. High risk
- B. Intermediate-low risk
- C. Intermediate-high risk
- D. Low risk
- E. Cannot be classified without PESI class

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** She is normotensive (no arrest, shock or persistent hypotension), so she is not high risk. sPESI ≥ 1 puts her above low risk, and she has **both** RV dysfunction (RV/LV ratio ≥ 1.0 on CT) **and** a raised troponin – that combination defines intermediate-high risk. These patients get anticoagulation, close monitoring and rescue reperfusion if they deteriorate.

**Why not the others:**
- A – high risk needs haemodynamic instability; BP 118/70 is normal.
- B – intermediate-low risk means only one or neither of RV dysfunction and troponin is positive.
- D – low risk requires sPESI 0 and no RV dysfunction or biomarker rise.
- E – sPESI can be used instead of PESI class (sPESI ≥ 1 is equivalent to PESI III–V).

**Ref:** Konstantinides SV et al., 2019 ESC PE Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2020;41:543–603.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the PEITHO trial:

- A. Tenecteplase did not significantly reduce the composite of death or haemodynamic decompensation within 7 days. — T / F
- B. It enrolled hypotensive patients (SBP < 90 mmHg) with RV dysfunction. — T / F
- C. Tenecteplase significantly reduced 30-day mortality. — T / F
- D. Stroke, mostly haemorrhagic, was more frequent with tenecteplase. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – tenecteplase significantly **reduced** the primary composite of death or haemodynamic decompensation within 7 days (2.6% vs 5.6%; OR 0.44).
- B (False) – PEITHO enrolled 1006 **normotensive** intermediate-risk patients with RV dysfunction and a positive troponin.
- C (False) – 30-day mortality did not differ significantly (2.4% vs 3.2%); the benefit came from fewer decompensations.
- D (True) – stroke was 2.4% vs 0.2%, mostly haemorrhagic, and major extracranial bleeding 6.3% vs 1.2%.

**EDIC trap:** PEITHO is positive for the primary end-point but negative for mortality – so thrombolysis in intermediate-high risk is rescue, not routine (ESC 2019 I B).

**Ref:** Meyer G et al. (PEITHO), N Engl J Med 2014;370:1402–1411.

</details>

**3.** Which of the following meets the ESC 2019 definition of haemodynamic instability in PE?

- A. Elevated lactate with normal blood pressure
- B. SBP 95 mmHg with heart rate 120/min
- C. RV/LV ratio > 1.0 on CT
- D. SBP drop of 30 mmHg for 10 minutes
- E. SBP < 90 mmHg for more than 15 minutes not explained by arrhythmia, hypovolaemia or sepsis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** ESC 2019 defines haemodynamic instability in PE as one of three: cardiac arrest, obstructive shock (SBP < 90 mmHg or vasopressors needed, plus end-organ hypoperfusion), or persistent hypotension. Persistent hypotension means SBP < 90 mmHg or a drop ≥ 40 mmHg for **> 15 min**, not caused by new arrhythmia, hypovolaemia or sepsis. Any one of these makes the PE high risk.

**Why not the others:**
- A – raised lactate without hypotension is not one of the ESC criteria (it is used in the AHA/ACC 2026 categories).
- B – SBP 95 mmHg is above the 90 mmHg threshold; tachycardia alone is not instability.
- C – RV/LV ratio > 1.0 is RV dysfunction, which counts for intermediate risk.
- D – the drop must be ≥ 40 mmHg and last > 15 min.

**Ref:** Konstantinides SV et al., 2019 ESC PE Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2020;41:543–603.

</details>

**4.** Haemodynamic management of a patient with high-risk PE:

- A. Large fluid boluses (> 2 L) are recommended to support the RV. — T / F
- B. Dobutamine may be used in low cardiac output. — T / F
- C. Induction of anaesthesia and positive pressure ventilation can precipitate cardiovascular collapse. — T / F
- D. Noradrenaline is contraindicated because it raises pulmonary vascular resistance. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – the failing RV is overfilled easily; ESC 2019 limits fluids to **≤ 500 mL**, as more worsens septal shift and LV filling.
- B (True) – dobutamine (2–20 µg/kg/min) may be used for low cardiac output; it can worsen hypotension, so combine it with noradrenaline if needed.
- C (True) – induction causes vasodilation and less preload, and positive pressure raises RV afterload, which can cause collapse; AHA/ACC 2026 calls avoidable intubation harmful in categories C–E.
- D (False) – noradrenaline is the vasopressor of choice; it restores systemic pressure and RV coronary perfusion.

**Ref:** Konstantinides SV et al., 2019 ESC PE Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2020;41:543–603; Creager MA et al., 2026 AHA/ACC PE Guideline, Circulation 2026;153:e977–e1051.

</details>

**5.** A patient with intermediate-high risk PE on LMWH becomes hypotensive (SBP 80 mmHg) on day 2. There is no contraindication to fibrinolysis. The best next step according to ESC 2019 is:

- A. Continue LMWH and add noradrenaline only
- B. Switch to a DOAC
- C. Administer 2 L of crystalloid
- D. Insert an inferior vena cava filter
- E. Rescue systemic thrombolysis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Her PE is now high risk (SBP 80 mmHg) on anticoagulation. ESC 2019 recommends rescue systemic thrombolysis for haemodynamic deterioration (class I B); PEITHO showed most decompensations occur in the first days. Surgical embolectomy or catheter-directed treatment are alternatives (IIa) if thrombolysis is contraindicated or the centre has the expertise.

**Why not the others:**
- A – noradrenaline is supportive, but on its own it does not remove the clot.
- B – changing the anticoagulant does not treat obstructive shock; UFH is preferred if reperfusion is planned.
- C – 2 L of fluid would worsen RV dilatation; the maximum is ≤ 500 mL.
- D – an IVC filter does not treat the existing clot; it is for patients who cannot be anticoagulated.

**Ref:** Konstantinides SV et al., 2019 ESC PE Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2020;41:543–603.

</details>

**6.** Regarding catheter-directed therapy trials:

- A. PEERLESS compared large-bore mechanical thrombectomy with catheter-directed thrombolysis. — T / F
- B. HI-PEITHO showed a significant reduction in all-cause mortality. — T / F
- C. PRAGUE-26 reduced the 7-day composite mainly through fewer cardiorespiratory decompensations. — T / F
- D. HI-PEITHO compared ultrasound-facilitated catheter-directed thrombolysis plus anticoagulation with anticoagulation alone. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – PEERLESS (550 intermediate-risk patients) compared large-bore mechanical thrombectomy with CDT; the win ratio favoured thrombectomy (less deterioration/bailout, fewer ICU stays).
- B (False) – HI-PEITHO showed no difference in mortality; the 7-day composite (4.0% vs 10.3%) was reduced by fewer decompensations.
- C (True) – PRAGUE-26 cut the 7-day composite from 6.8% to 0.7%, mainly through fewer cardiorespiratory decompensations; there were 2 intracranial haemorrhages with CDT.
- D (True) – HI-PEITHO compared ultrasound-facilitated CDT (low-dose alteplase) plus anticoagulation with anticoagulation alone.

**EDIC trap:** Catheter-directed therapy has not yet been shown to reduce mortality.

**Ref:** PEERLESS, Circulation 2025;151:260–273; Rosenfield K et al. (HI-PEITHO), N Engl J Med 2026; Kroupa J et al. (PRAGUE-26), N Engl J Med 2026.

</details>

**7.** The approved systemic alteplase regimen for high-risk PE is:

- A. 50 mg bolus followed by 50 mg over 12 hours
- B. 1 mg/h for 24 hours
- C. 10 mg bolus only
- D. 0.9 mg/kg over 60 minutes, max 90 mg
- E. 100 mg over 2 hours

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** The approved systemic regimen for high-risk PE is alteplase **100 mg IV over 2 hours** (ESC 2019). The benefit is greatest when given within 48 h of symptom onset, but it can still help when symptoms have lasted 6–14 days. In cardiac arrest or extreme instability, an off-label accelerated regimen of 0.6 mg/kg over 15 min (max 50 mg) is used.

**Why not the others:**
- A – a 50 mg bolus followed by 50 mg over 12 h is not a standard PE regimen.
- B – 1 mg/h is the type of low-dose infusion used in catheter-directed thrombolysis, not systemic treatment.
- C – a 10 mg bolus alone is far below an effective systemic thrombolytic dose.
- D – 0.9 mg/kg over 60 min (max 90 mg) is the regimen for acute ischaemic stroke.

**Ref:** Konstantinides SV et al., 2019 ESC PE Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2020;41:543–603.

</details>

**8.** Regarding VA-ECMO and PERT in PE:

- A. ESC 2019 states that ECMO may be considered in refractory circulatory collapse or cardiac arrest. — T / F
- B. ECMO in PE is supported by large randomised trials. — T / F

- B (False) – in HI-PEITHO (7-day composite 4.0% vs 10.3%) the benefit came mainly from fewer cardiorespiratory decompensations, not deaths.
- C (False) – neither trial showed a significant reduction in all-cause mortality.
- D (True) – in PEERLESS, large-bore mechanical thrombectomy had far fewer ICU stays than CDT (41.6% vs 98.6%) and less deterioration/bailout.

**EDIC trap:** Catheter-directed therapy prevents early decompensation; a mortality benefit has not yet been shown.

**Ref:** Kroupa J et al. (PRAGUE-26), N Engl J Med 2026; Rosenfield K et al. (HI-PEITHO), N Engl J Med 2026; PEERLESS, Circulation 2025;151:260–273.

**7.** A 66-year-old presents with tearing chest pain, BP 175/100 mmHg, HR 105/min. CT shows type B dissection without malperfusion. The best initial drug strategy is:

- A. No BP lowering to avoid spinal ischaemia
- B. Oral amlodipine
- C. IV nitroprusside alone
- D. IV labetalol or esmolol first, then add a vasodilator if SBP remains > 120 mmHg
- E. IV hydralazine

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Medical therapy for all acute aortic syndromes aims to reduce dP/dt and wall stress: HR ≤ 60/min and SBP < 120 mmHg (ESC 2024). A beta-blocker is given first – IV labetalol, or esmolol – with good analgesia. A vasodilator (nitroprusside, nitroglycerin, nicardipine) is added only once the rate is controlled. Uncomplicated type B dissection is managed medically.

**Why not the others:**
- A – BP lowering is the core treatment; a higher BP is accepted only with malperfusion.
- B – oral amlodipine is slow, not titratable, and does not control heart rate.
- C – nitroprusside alone causes reflex tachycardia, which increases shear stress.
- E – hydralazine is unpredictable and also causes reflex tachycardia.

**Ref:** Mazzolai L et al., 2024 ESC Guidelines on peripheral arterial and aortic diseases, Eur Heart J 2024;45:3538–3700.

**9.** In a patient with acute type A aortic dissection, which statement is correct?

- A. Age > 80 years is an absolute contraindication to surgery
- B. Medical therapy is equivalent to surgery
- C. Immediate surgical repair is recommended; untreated mortality is about 1–2 % per hour early
- D. TEVAR is the first-line treatment
- E. Surgery should be delayed until BP is normal for 48 hours

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Untreated acute type A dissection has ~50% mortality in the first 48 h, about 1–2% per hour, from rupture, tamponade, acute AR or coronary malperfusion. **Why not the others:**
- A – age > 80 years is not an absolute contraindication.
- B – medical therapy alone has a much higher mortality than surgery in type A.
- D – TEVAR is first-line for complicated type B, not type A.
- E – delaying surgery for 48 h exposes the patient to the highest-risk period.

**11.** A 70-year-old has acute spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage, GCS 14, SBP 210 mmHg, 2 hours after onset. According to ESC 2024 the most appropriate BP target is:

- A. No treatment unless SBP > 240 mmHg
- B. SBP 140–160 mmHg, lowered within 6 hours
- C. SBP < 185 mmHg only before thrombolysis
- D. SBP < 110 mmHg within 1 hour
- E. Reduce MAP by 50 % immediately

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** ESC 2024 recommends that immediate BP lowering to SBP 140–160 mmHg within 6 h of onset should be considered (IIa A). **Why not the others:**
- A – waiting until SBP > 240 mmHg is not recommended; early lowering is advised.
- C – < 185/110 mmHg is the pre-thrombolysis target in ischaemic stroke; thrombolysis is contraindicated in ICH.

- E – a 50% MAP fall risks cerebral and renal ischaemia.

**13.** A patient with aortic valve endocarditis (S. aureus) develops a new PR prolongation progressing to second-degree AV block. TEE shows a periannular abscess. Haemodynamics are stable. According to ESC 2023, surgery should be:

- A. Elective after completing antibiotics
- B. Performed only if a stroke occurs
- C. Urgent (within 3–5 days)
- D. Replaced by percutaneous drainage
- E. Not indicated; continue antibiotics for 6 weeks

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** A periannular abscess with new, progressing AV block shows locally uncontrolled infection spreading into the conduction tissue. ESC 2023 lists this as an indication for **urgent** surgery (within 3–5 days). Antibiotics alone rarely clear an abscess, and the block can progress to complete heart block. Emergency surgery (< 24 h) is kept for refractory pulmonary oedema or shock.

**Why not the others:**
- A – waiting until antibiotics are completed lets the abscess spread (fistula, pseudoaneurysm, complete block).
- B – stroke is not what makes surgery necessary here; uncontrolled infection is.
- D – there is no percutaneous drainage for a periannular abscess; it needs surgical debridement.
- E – an abscess is a clear indication for surgery.

**14.** A 74-year-old with known severe aortic stenosis requires emergency laparotomy and becomes hypotensive after induction.

- B. Tachycardia should be avoided. — T / F

**15.** A patient with acute severe aortic regurgitation from endocarditis is in cardiogenic shock awaiting surgery. Which intervention is contraindicated?

- A. Intra-aortic balloon pump
- B. Emergency surgery
- C. Nitroprusside with careful monitoring
- D. Dobutamine
- E. Avoiding bradycardia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** An IABP inflates in diastole, which raises aortic diastolic pressure and increases the regurgitant volume into the non-dilated LV. It is therefore contraindicated in significant AR (and in aortic dissection). Acute severe AR with cardiogenic shock from endocarditis needs emergency surgery (within 24 h). Vasodilators and inotropes are used as a bridge.

**Why not the others:**
- B – emergency surgery is the definitive treatment.
- C – nitroprusside lowers afterload and regurgitation, so it is a suitable bridge if BP allows.
- D – dobutamine supports contractility and raises heart rate, which shortens diastole.
- E – avoiding bradycardia is correct, as a longer diastole increases regurgitation.

**EDIC trap:** IABP helps in acute MR and ventricular septal rupture, but not in AR.

**Ref:** Praz F et al., 2025 ESC/EACTS Valvular Heart Disease Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2025; Delgado V et al., 2023 ESC Endocarditis Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2023;44:3948–4042.

</details>
