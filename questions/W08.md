## W08 · D1 – Arrhythmias in the ICU: AF, VT/electrical storm, bradyarrhythmias

**1.** A 68-year-old man with septic shock on noradrenaline develops new atrial fibrillation at 165/min. BP falls to 70/40 mmHg with rising lactate despite adequate filling. According to ERC 2025, the most appropriate immediate step is:

- A. IV digoxin loading
- B. Synchronised cardioversion with an initial shock at maximum defibrillator output
- C. Synchronised cardioversion starting at 50 J with stepwise escalation
- D. Wait 48 hours for spontaneous conversion
- E. IV diltiazem bolus

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Instability → emergency synchronised cardioversion (ESC *I C*); ERC 2025 recommends an initial shock at maximum energy for AF. No diltiazem in hypotension; wait-and-see only in stable patients.

</details>

**2.** Regarding rate control of atrial fibrillation according to the 2024 ESC guidelines:

- A. Lenient rate control with a resting heart rate < 110/min should be considered as the initial target. — T / F
- B. Diltiazem and verapamil should be avoided when LVEF is ≤ 40 %. — T / F
- C. Digoxin can be used irrespective of left ventricular ejection fraction. — T / F
- D. In haemodynamic instability, IV amiodarone, digoxin, esmolol or landiolol may be considered. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.** B: non-dihydropyridine CCBs are negatively inotropic – use only if LVEF > 40%. D: IIb B.

</details>

**3.** In the STRESS-L trial of landiolol in septic shock with heart rate ≥ 95/min:

- A. There was no improvement in organ failure and the trial was stopped early with a possible signal of harm
- B. Landiolol significantly reduced 28-day mortality
- C. Landiolol significantly reduced the mean SOFA score
- D. Landiolol was compared with esmolol
- E. Only patients with atrial fibrillation were enrolled

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** SOFA 8.8 vs 8.1 (NS), mortality numerically higher with landiolol; stopped for unlikely benefit and a signal of harm. Most patients had sinus tachycardia; the control was standard care.

</details>

**4.** Concerning anticoagulation and cardioversion in atrial fibrillation (ESC 2024):

- A. The CHA₂DS₂-VA score adds one point for female sex. — T / F
- B. A score of ≥ 2 is an indicator to start oral anticoagulation. — T / F
- C. Early cardioversion without anticoagulation or TOE is acceptable if AF has lasted up to 72 hours. — T / F
- D. Atrial fibrillation triggered by a reversible precipitant never requires long-term anticoagulation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.** A: CHA₂DS₂-VA (ESC 2024) dropped female sex as a risk modifier. C: the threshold is 24 h. D: consider long-term OAC, IIa C.

</details>

**5.** A 74-year-old woman with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF 25 %) has haemodynamically stable recent-onset AF. Pharmacological cardioversion is requested. The most appropriate agent is:

- A. Vernakalant
- B. Verapamil
- C. Flecainide
- D. Propafenone
- E. Amiodarone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** In HFrEF/CAD the drug of choice is amiodarone (with delayed onset). Flecainide/propafenone are contraindicated in structural disease, vernakalant in HFrEF; verapamil is not a cardioversion drug and is contraindicated in HFrEF.

</details>

**6.** Regarding electrical storm (ESC 2022):

- A. It is defined as ≥ 3 episodes of sustained ventricular arrhythmia within 7 days, each requiring intervention. — T / F
- B. Catheter ablation is contraindicated during electrical storm. — T / F
- C. Sedation is part of the initial management. — T / F
- D. A non-selective beta-blocker such as propranolol combined with amiodarone is commonly used. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – T.** A: the definition is ≥ 3 episodes within 24 hours. B: ablation is preferred in incessant monomorphic VT.

</details>

**7.** A patient on haloperidol and ciprofloxacin with QTc 590 ms develops recurrent runs of torsade de pointes. Serum magnesium is normal. The first-line drug is:

- A. Adenosine
- B. Intravenous magnesium sulfate
- C. Procainamide
- D. Sotalol
- E. Amiodarone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** IV Mg²⁺ is effective even with normal levels. Amiodarone, sotalol and procainamide prolong QT. For recurrences increase HR (isoprenaline/pacing).

</details>

**8.** Bradycardia with adverse features (ERC 2025):

- A. Transcutaneous pacing can be used as a bridge to transvenous pacing. — T / F
- B. The initial atropine dose is 500 µg IV, repeated up to a total of 3 mg. — T / F
- C. Isoprenaline or adrenaline are second-line options when atropine fails. — T / F
- D. Atropine is the drug of choice after heart transplantation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.** D: denervated heart – do not give atropine; consider aminophylline.

</details>

**9.** In a large cohort of patients hospitalised with severe sepsis (Walkey, JAMA 2011), new-onset atrial fibrillation was associated with:

- A. No difference in stroke
- B. Higher in-hospital stroke and mortality
- C. A protective effect of anticoagulation on mortality
- D. Lower in-hospital mortality
- E. Reduced length of stay

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Stroke 2.6% vs 0.6%, mortality 56% vs 39%. This is an observational association.

</details>

**10.** Regarding sustained monomorphic ventricular tachycardia (ESC 2022):

- A. In PROCAMIO, amiodarone terminated more tachycardias than procainamide. — T / F
- B. Haemodynamic instability is an indication for IV amiodarone before any attempt at cardioversion. — T / F
- C. A haemodynamically tolerated VT may be left untreated if blood pressure is normal. — T / F
- D. Procainamide should be avoided in severe heart failure and acute myocardial infarction. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.** A: procainamide was more effective, with fewer major cardiac adverse events. B: an unstable VT requires immediate synchronised cardioversion. C: even tolerated SMVT should be promptly terminated.

</details>

**11.** A 72-year-old woman is in the ICU 2 days after a large intracerebral haemorrhage. She receives haloperidol for agitation and clarithromycin for pneumonia. K⁺ 3.1 mmol/L, Mg²⁺ 0.55 mmol/L, QTc 610 ms; she has repeated self-terminating runs of polymorphic VT with "twisting" QRS axis, each preceded by a pause, and is haemodynamically stable between runs. Which combination is most appropriate?

- A. Procainamide 10–15 mg/kg IV
- B. Adenosine 6 mg IV to clarify the rhythm
- C. Magnesium sulfate 2 g IV, potassium to high-normal, stop haloperidol and clarithromycin; isoprenaline or overdrive pacing if runs recur
- D. Amiodarone 300 mg IV, then infusion
- E. Synchronised cardioversion at 120 J and then sotalol

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Torsade de pointes with several additive causes: QT-prolonging drugs (haloperidol, macrolide), hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia and acute brain injury. Mg²⁺ works even at normal levels; correct K⁺; remove the drugs; pause-dependent recurrences respond to a higher heart rate (isoprenaline or overdrive pacing). Amiodarone, sotalol and procainamide prolong QT further. Unstable or pulseless TdP → defibrillation (unsynchronised).

</details>

**12.** A 64-year-old man has repeated runs of polymorphic VT/VF on the day after primary PCI for an anterior STEMI. According to the 2022 ESC guidelines:

- A. IV lidocaine may be considered if beta-blockers and amiodarone are ineffective or amiodarone is contraindicated. — T / F
- B. An IV beta-blocker is recommended for recurrent polymorphic VT/VF unless contraindicated. — T / F
- C. Routine prophylactic antiarrhythmic drugs other than beta-blockers are recommended after ACS to prevent VT. — T / F
- D. Repeat urgent coronary angiography should be considered if ongoing ischaemia (e.g. stent thrombosis) is suspected. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.** B: class I. A: class IIb (amiodarone IIa). D: ischaemia is the main driver of polymorphic VT/VF after MI – urgent angiography/revascularisation and K⁺/Mg²⁺ repletion are class I in the ESC algorithm. C: prophylactic antiarrhythmics other than BB are class III.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 70-year-old ventilated patient with septic shock on norepinephrine develops new AF at 140/min with stable blood pressure. Outline your management, including anticoagulation.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Haemodynamically stable – no immediate cardioversion; NOAF is a marker of severity.
- Treat triggers first: sepsis source control, volume status (hypovolaemia or overload), catecholamine dose, hypoxaemia/hypercapnia, acidosis, K⁺ and Mg²⁺, CVC tip in the RA.
- Wait-and-see within 48 h is reasonable (IIa B) – up to 83% revert spontaneously after treating the cause.
- Rate control target < 110/min; BB or digoxin; diltiazem/verapamil only if LVEF > 40%; with instability or low EF consider amiodarone, digoxin, esmolol or landiolol (IIb).
- Do not routinely beta-block compensatory sinus tachycardia (STRESS-L: no benefit, signal of harm).
- If becomes unstable: synchronised cardioversion with maximum energy (ERC 2025), then amiodarone 300 mg if it fails.
- Anticoagulation: CHA₂DS₂-VA; in acute sepsis benefit/risk unclear, no routine therapeutic anticoagulation; no elective cardioversion without OAC/TOE if AF > 24 h.
- After discharge consider long-term OAC in patients at increased risk (IIa C).

</details>

**O2. (open)** A patient with an ICD receives five shocks for monomorphic VT within 6 hours. Outline your management of this electrical storm.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Meets the definition: ≥ 3 sustained ventricular arrhythmia episodes in 24 h, separated by ≥ 5 min, each requiring intervention.
- Unstable VT → immediate synchronised cardioversion (unsynchronised if synchronisation fails).
- Sedation to break the shock–fear–sympathetic cycle; deep sedation/intubation if refractory.
- Non-selective BB (propranolol) + amiodarone; landiolol for amiodarone-resistant VT; procainamide an option (PROCAMIO) except in severe HF, acute MI, end-stage renal failure.
- Correct K⁺ and Mg²⁺, look for ischaemia (urgent angiography if suspected), stop proarrhythmic drugs.
- Catheter ablation – preferred for incessant monomorphic VT.
- MCS for stabilisation (prophylactic before ablation better than rescue).

</details>


## W08 · D2 – Pulmonary embolism: risk stratification, thrombolysis, catheters, ECMO, PERT

**1.** A 60-year-old woman with confirmed PE has BP 118/70 mmHg, HR 112/min, sPESI 2, RV/LV ratio 1.3 on CT and elevated troponin. According to ESC 2019 she is classified as:

- A. High risk
- B. Intermediate-low risk
- C. Intermediate-high risk
- D. Low risk
- E. Cannot be classified without PESI class

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Normotension + sPESI ≥ 1 + RV dysfunction **and** positive troponin = intermediate-high risk.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the PEITHO trial:

- A. Tenecteplase did not significantly reduce the composite of death or haemodynamic decompensation within 7 days. — T / F
- B. It enrolled hypotensive patients (SBP < 90 mmHg) with RV dysfunction. — T / F
- C. Tenecteplase significantly reduced 30-day mortality. — T / F
- D. Stroke, mostly haemorrhagic, was more frequent with tenecteplase. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.** A: the primary composite was significantly reduced, 2.6 vs 5.6%. B: it enrolled normotensive intermediate-risk patients (RV dysfunction + positive troponin). C: 2.4 vs 3.2%, NS. D: 2.4 vs 0.2%.

</details>

**3.** Which of the following meets the ESC 2019 definition of haemodynamic instability in PE?

- A. Elevated lactate with normal blood pressure
- B. SBP 95 mmHg with heart rate 120/min
- C. RV/LV ratio > 1.0 on CT
- D. SBP drop of 30 mmHg for 10 minutes
- E. SBP < 90 mmHg for more than 15 minutes not explained by arrhythmia, hypovolaemia or sepsis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** Persistent hypotension = SBP < 90 mmHg or a drop ≥ 40 mmHg for > 15 min. Cardiac arrest and obstructive shock are the other criteria.

</details>

**4.** Haemodynamic management of a patient with high-risk PE:

- A. Large fluid boluses (> 2 L) are recommended to support the RV. — T / F
- B. Dobutamine may be used in low cardiac output. — T / F
- C. Induction of anaesthesia and positive pressure ventilation can precipitate cardiovascular collapse. — T / F
- D. Noradrenaline is contraindicated because it raises pulmonary vascular resistance. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.** A: ≤ 500 mL. D: noradrenaline is the vasopressor of choice – it restores systemic pressure and RV coronary perfusion.

</details>

**5.** A patient with intermediate-high risk PE on LMWH becomes hypotensive (SBP 80 mmHg) on day 2. There is no contraindication to fibrinolysis. The best next step according to ESC 2019 is:

- A. Continue LMWH and add noradrenaline only
- B. Switch to a DOAC
- C. Administer 2 L of crystalloid
- D. Insert an inferior vena cava filter
- E. Rescue systemic thrombolysis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** Rescue thrombolysis on haemodynamic deterioration is *I B*; embolectomy or catheter-directed treatment are alternatives (*IIa*).

</details>

**6.** Regarding catheter-directed therapy trials:

- A. PEERLESS compared large-bore mechanical thrombectomy with catheter-directed thrombolysis. — T / F
- B. HI-PEITHO showed a significant reduction in all-cause mortality. — T / F
- C. PRAGUE-26 reduced the 7-day composite mainly through fewer cardiorespiratory decompensations. — T / F
- D. HI-PEITHO compared ultrasound-facilitated catheter-directed thrombolysis plus anticoagulation with anticoagulation alone. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.** B: no difference in mortality.

</details>

**7.** The approved systemic alteplase regimen for high-risk PE is:

- A. 50 mg bolus followed by 50 mg over 12 hours
- B. 1 mg/h for 24 hours
- C. 10 mg bolus only
- D. 0.9 mg/kg over 60 minutes, max 90 mg

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** The accelerated regimen 0.6 mg/kg over 15 min (max 50 mg) is off-label, mainly in cardiac arrest. 0.9 mg/kg is the regimen for ischaemic stroke.

</details>

**8.** Regarding VA-ECMO and PERT in PE:

- A. ESC 2019 states that ECMO may be considered in refractory circulatory collapse or cardiac arrest. — T / F
- B. ECMO in PE is supported by large randomised trials. — T / F
- C. ECMO in PE is associated with a low rate of complications. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.** A: IIb C. B: there are no RCTs. C: bleeding and vascular complications are frequent.

</details>

**9.** In the 2026 AHA/ACC acute PE classification, a normotensive patient with signs of shock and end-organ dysfunction (normotensive shock) belongs to category:

- A. C3
- B. C1
- C. E2
- D. B
- E. D

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** Category D = incipient cardiopulmonary failure (D2 = normotensive shock with organ dysfunction). Category E = overt failure (E1 hypotension/shock, E2 refractory shock/arrest).

</details>

**10.** Anticoagulation in acute PE:

- A. Unfractionated heparin is preferred when creatinine clearance is ≤ 30 mL/min. — T / F
- B. LMWH is generally preferred over unfractionated heparin. — T / F
- C. Unfractionated heparin is preferred in haemodynamically unstable patients who may need reperfusion. — T / F
- D. Routine IVC filter placement in anticoagulated patients is recommended. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.** D: AHA/ACC 2026 – harmful.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 60-year-old woman with confirmed PE is normotensive with HR 112/min, sPESI 2, RV/LV ratio 1.3 and elevated troponin. Classify her risk and outline your management, including what you would do if she deteriorates.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- ESC 2019: intermediate-high risk (no instability, sPESI ≥ 1, RV dysfunction and positive troponin); AHA/ACC 2026 category C or higher – measure lactate.
- Anticoagulation – LMWH preferred over UFH (UFH if reperfusion likely or CrCl ≤ 30 mL/min).
- No routine primary systemic thrombolysis – PEITHO: ↓ decompensation (2.6 vs 5.6%) but no mortality benefit and ↑ major bleeding/haemorrhagic stroke.
- Close monitoring; activate PERT (AHA/ACC 2026 Class 1 for C–E).
- If haemodynamic deterioration: rescue systemic thrombolysis (I B); embolectomy or catheter-directed treatment as alternatives (IIa).
- Catheter-directed therapy may reduce early decompensation in selected patients with additional severity signs (HI-PEITHO, PRAGUE-26) – no proven mortality benefit.
- Haemodynamic support: fluids ≤ 500 mL, norepinephrine, dobutamine for low output; avoid intubation where possible.
- Refractory collapse/arrest: VA-ECMO as a bridge (IIb C) or ECPR.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why PEITHO and the 2026 catheter-directed trials (HI-PEITHO, PRAGUE-26) are interpreted differently despite all reducing early decompensation.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- All three targeted intermediate-high-risk PE and reduced early (7-day) decompensation-driven composites.
- PEITHO used full-dose systemic tenecteplase: major extracranial bleeding 6.3 vs 1.2%, stroke 2.4 vs 0.2% (mostly haemorrhagic).
- PEITHO: no reduction in 7-d or 30-d mortality → routine systemic thrombolysis not recommended.
- CDT uses a lower thrombolytic dose delivered into the thrombus, aiming for less bleeding.
- HI-PEITHO: 4.0 vs 10.3%, no significant difference in major bleeding, no intracranial haemorrhage.
- PRAGUE-26: 0.7 vs 6.8%, major bleeding 1.4 vs 2.2% (NS), but 2 intracranial haemorrhages with CDT.
- None showed a mortality benefit; selection, availability and centre expertise (PERT) determine use.

</details>


## W08 · D3 – Acute aortic dissection and cardiac tamponade

**1.** CT angiography shows an intimal flap originating in the descending aorta distal to the left subclavian artery, with retrograde extension into the ascending aorta. This is classified as:

- A. DeBakey III
- B. Penetrating aortic ulcer
- C. Stanford B, because the entry is in the descending aorta
- D. Intramural haematoma
- E. Stanford A

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** The Stanford classification depends on involvement of the ascending aorta, not on the entry site.

</details>

**2.** Regarding initial medical therapy of acute aortic dissection (ESC 2024):

- A. Opioids should be avoided because they mask ongoing pain from extension of the dissection. — T / F
- B. IV labetalol or esmolol are first-line agents. — T / F
- C. A vasodilator should be started before beta-blockade to lower BP quickly. — T / F
- D. The target is systolic BP < 120 mmHg and heart rate ≤ 60/min. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.** A: adequate opioid analgesia is part of management – it reduces sympathetic drive. C: vasodilator only after rate control – reflex tachycardia.

</details>

**3.** A haemodynamically unstable patient in the emergency department is suspected to have acute aortic dissection; CT is not immediately possible. The most appropriate imaging is:

- A. Aortography
- B. Chest radiograph
- C. MRI angiography
- D. Transoesophageal echocardiography
- E. D-dimer measurement

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.** TOE when unstable or CT unavailable. MRI has limited acute availability and is unsuitable for an unstable patient; D-dimer is part of the algorithm at low probability, not imaging.

</details>

**4.** Which are features of complicated type B aortic dissection that favour TEVAR?

- A. Rupture or impending rupture — T / F
- B. Rapid aortic expansion — T / F
- C. Refractory pain or refractory hypertension — T / F
- D. Malperfusion — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.** Complicated = rupture, malperfusion, rapid expansion, refractory pain/HT.

</details>

**5.** The approximate mortality of untreated acute type A dissection in the first 48 hours is:

- A. 10 % per hour
- B. 1–2 % per hour
- C. Mortality is similar to treated type B dissection
- D. 5 % in total
- E. 0.1 % per hour

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** Hence immediate surgery; age alone is not a contraindication.

</details>

**6.** Echocardiographic findings supporting cardiac tamponade:

- A. Small inferior vena cava collapsing > 50 % with inspiration — T / F
- B. Diastolic right ventricular collapse — T / F
- C. Absence of respiratory variation of mitral and tricuspid inflow — T / F
- D. Right atrial collapse — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – T.** A: a dilated, non-collapsing IVC is typical. C: exaggerated respiratory variation of inflow is typical (ventricular interdependence).

</details>

**7.** A patient with acute type A dissection develops haemopericardium with SBP 60 mmHg in the emergency department; the operating room will be ready in 30 minutes. The most appropriate approach is:

- A. Complete pericardial drainage aiming for SBP 140 mmHg
- B. Transfer for TEVAR
- C. Intubation and high PEEP before any drainage
- D. IV nitroprusside to reduce shear stress

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** Controlled drainage of small volumes (Hayashi 2012) as a bridge to emergency surgery; no nitroprusside in hypotension; intubation with positive pressure in tamponade may precipitate collapse.

</details>

**8.** Clinical signs of cardiac tamponade include:

- B. Widened pulse pressure with bounding pulses — T / F

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.** B: a wide pulse pressure is more typical of AR.

</details>

**9.** According to the 2025 ESC pericardial guidelines, a definite tamponade triage score indicating immediate pericardiocentesis is:

- A. Effusion > 10 mm in end-diastole
- B. > 2 points
- C. > 4 points
- D. > 6 points from at least two categories
- E. Any circumferential effusion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.** The score combines aetiology, clinical picture and imaging (expert consensus).

</details>

**10.** Regarding pericardial drainage (ESC 2025):

- A. Pericardiocentesis should be guided by echocardiography, CT or fluoroscopy. — T / F
- B. Needle pericardiocentesis alone is preferred over surgical drainage for purulent effusion. — T / F
- C. A large effusion is defined as > 10 mm in end-diastole. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.** A: I C. B: surgical drainage is preferred for purulent effusion (I C). C: large = > 20 mm (10–20 mm = moderate). D: the patient is preload-dependent – diuretics worsen filling.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 64-year-old hypertensive man presents with sudden tearing chest pain radiating to the back, BP 190/100 mmHg and HR 105/min. Outline your diagnostic approach and initial medical management.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Suspect AAS; ADD-RS + D-dimer helps only at low pre-test probability (< 500 ng/mL makes dissection unlikely).
- ECG-gated CT angiography neck to pelvis is the method of choice (sensitivity 100%, specificity 98%); TOE if unstable or CT unavailable; negative TTE does not exclude.
- Arterial line (ideally right radial), ICU, careful IV opioid analgesia.
- Targets SBP < 120 mmHg and HR ≤ 60/min to reduce dP/dt.
- Rate first: IV labetalol or esmolol (non-dihydropyridine CCB if BB contraindicated).
- Vasodilator (nitroprusside, nitroglycerin, nicardipine) only after rate control – avoid reflex tachycardia.
- Classify: Stanford A → immediate surgery (1–2%/h mortality untreated); B uncomplicated → medical, complicated → TEVAR.
- Tolerate higher BP in malperfusion; treat hypotension as a warning sign (tamponade, rupture, AR, MI).

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain the pathophysiology of cardiac tamponade and why management of haemopericardium in type A dissection differs from tamponade of other causes.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Raised intrapericardial pressure exceeds chamber filling pressure → right chamber collapse, ventricular interdependence, ↓ LV filling and output.
- Rate of accumulation matters more than volume – small acute haemopericardium can cause tamponade.
- Patient is preload- and HR-dependent: avoid diuretics and vasodilators, fluids cautiously; induction and positive pressure may cause collapse – drain before intubation if possible.
- Usual treatment: image-guided pericardiocentesis (I C); surgical drainage if not feasible or purulent.
- In type A dissection, definitive treatment is emergency aortic surgery.
- Uncontrolled full drainage may raise BP and increase aortic bleeding.
- Controlled drainage of small volumes (mean ~40 mL) targeting SBP ~90 mmHg as a bridge (Hayashi 2012: SBP ~64 → ~95 mmHg, all reached surgery).

</details>


## W08 · D4 – Hypertensive crises: emergency vs urgency, drugs, target reductions

**1.** A 52-year-old man has BP 230/130 mmHg, headache and no evidence of acute organ damage (normal neurology, ECG, troponin, creatinine, fundoscopy). The most appropriate management is:

**O1. (open)** Compare the ICU haemodynamic management of acute severe mitral regurgitation and acute severe aortic regurgitation, including the role of IABP and heart rate.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Both: non-dilated chamber cannot accommodate sudden volume → pulmonary oedema and shock; murmurs may be soft/short; TOE often needed.

</details>
