## W23 · D1 – Primary survey and damage-control resuscitation

**1.** A 28-year-old motorcyclist arrives with a near-amputation of the right thigh spurting blood, GCS 13, RR 28/min and HR 140/min. According to ATLS 11, what is the first action?

- A. Rapid sequence intubation with video laryngoscopy
- B. Application of a cervical collar
- C. Tourniquet or direct pressure on the thigh wound
- D. Bilateral needle decompression of the chest
- E. Two large-bore cannulas and 1 L of warm crystalloid

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** ATLS 11 introduced the xABCDE sequence: exsanguinating external haemorrhage is controlled first, before the airway. A tourniquet for life-threatening open extremity bleeding is a 1B recommendation in the European 2023 guideline.

**Why not the others:**
- A – airway follows control of catastrophic bleeding, and induction in profound shock without resuscitation risks arrest.
- B – spinal motion restriction is now selective and never precedes haemorrhage control.
- D – there are no signs of tension pneumothorax.
- E – crystalloid is only a bridge; the source of loss must be stopped first.

**Ref:** ATLS 11th ed., ACS 2025; Rossaint R, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

**2.** Regarding haemorrhagic shock classification and recognition in adults:

- A. Systolic hypotension typically appears only after loss of more than 30 % of blood volume. — T / F
- B. A base deficit of −8 mmol/L corresponds to ATLS class III shock. — T / F
- C. A normal haemoglobin on arrival makes major haemorrhage unlikely. — T / F
- D. Beta-blockade can mask the tachycardic response to blood loss. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – SBP falls in class III (31–40 % loss); classes I–II are compensated.
- B (True) – class III corresponds to base deficit −6 to −10.
- C (False) – Hb falls only after haemodilution; serial measurement is recommended.
- D (True) – age, beta-blockers, pacemakers and athletic training blunt tachycardia.

**Ref:** ATLS 11th ed., ACS 2025; Rossaint R, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

**3.** A 35-year-old man with a stab wound to the abdomen has HR 125/min and BP 78/45 mmHg; there is no head injury. The surgeons are preparing the theatre. What blood-pressure target should guide fluid and blood administration until bleeding is controlled?

- A. MAP ≥ 80 mmHg
- B. SBP 120–140 mmHg
- C. MAP ≥ 65 mmHg with noradrenaline if needed
- D. Normal urine output of 0.5 mL/kg/h
- E. SBP 80–90 mmHg (MAP 50–60 mmHg)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Without TBI, the European guideline recommends restricted volume replacement with a target SBP of 80–90 mmHg (MAP 50–60) until major bleeding has been stopped (1B). Bickell's trial in penetrating torso injury showed better survival with delayed fluid resuscitation (70 vs 62 %).

**Why not the others:**
- A – MAP ≥ 80 mmHg is the target in severe TBI.
- B – normotension before haemorrhage control increases bleeding ("popping the clot") and dilution.
- C – MAP 65 is the sepsis target, not for uncontrolled bleeding.
- D – urine output is too slow and unreliable a target in the hyperacute phase.

**Ref:** Bickell WH, N Engl J Med 1994; Rossaint R, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

- B. More patients achieved haemostasis in the 1:1:1 group. — T / F

**5.** A 42-year-old woman is admitted after a fall from height with a severe head injury (GCS 6), a pelvic fracture and HR 120/min. All of the following are appropriate during initial resuscitation EXCEPT:

- A. Target MAP ≥ 80 mmHg
- B. Ringer's lactate as the main resuscitation fluid
- C. Pelvic binder centred over the greater trochanters
- D. Tranexamic acid 1 g over 10 min followed by 1 g over 8 h
- E. Keeping ionised calcium in the normal range

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Ringer's lactate is hypotonic and the European guideline recommends avoiding hypotonic solutions in severe head trauma (1B); 0.9 % saline or a balanced isotonic crystalloid (and blood products) should be used.

**Why not the others:**
- A – with severe TBI, MAP ≥ 80 mmHg is recommended instead of permissive hypotension.
- C – a binder at the level of the greater trochanters closes the pelvic ring.
- D – TXA within 3 h is recommended in bleeding trauma (1A).
- E – hypocalcaemia worsens coagulopathy; keep iCa²⁺ 1.1–1.3 mmol/L.

**Ref:** Rossaint R, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

**6.** Regarding acute traumatic coagulopathy:

- A. It is present on hospital arrival in a substantial proportion of severely injured patients, before major fluid administration. — T / F
- B. Activation of protein C by hypoperfusion contributes to its development. — T / F
- C. Platelet count is the first haemostatic parameter to reach critically low levels. — T / F
- D. Standard laboratory coagulation tests run at 37 °C underestimate the effect of hypothermia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – about a quarter to a third of major trauma patients are coagulopathic on arrival; it is endogenous, not only dilutional.
- B (True) – thrombomodulin–activated protein C causes anticoagulation and hyperfibrinolysis via PAI-1 consumption.
- C (False) – fibrinogen is the first factor to fall to critical levels.
- D (True) – samples are warmed to 37 °C, masking hypothermic enzyme slowing.

**Ref:** Rossaint R, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

**7.** A 55-year-old man in a motor-vehicle collision has AIS 4 for a head injury, AIS 3 for a lung contusion, AIS 2 for a second chest injury (rib fractures) and AIS 3 for a femoral fracture. What is his Injury Severity Score?

- A. 34
- B. 29
- C. 38
- D. 41
- E. 12

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** ISS is the sum of the squares of the highest AIS in the three most severely injured body regions: head 4² (16) + chest 3² (9) + extremities 3² (9) = 34. Only the highest AIS in each region counts.

**Why not the others:**
- B – this would mis-sum the regions (e.g. 16 + 9 + 4 using the second chest injury).
- C – 38 adds both chest injuries (16 + 9 + 4 + 9), counting one region twice.
- D – there is no combination of the three regions giving 41.
- E – AIS values must be squared, not summed.

**EDIC trap:** the New ISS (NISS) uses the three highest AIS regardless of region; here NISS would also be 34.

**Ref:** ATLS 11th ed., ACS 2025.

</details>

**9.** A 23-year-old woman is ventilated after a crush injury to the chest. Ten minutes after intubation her SpO₂ falls to 78 %, BP to 70/40 mmHg, airway pressures rise and breath sounds are absent on the right. What is the most appropriate immediate action?

- A. Urgent chest X-ray to confirm the diagnosis
- B. eFAST before any intervention
- C. Increase PEEP and FiO₂
- D. Decompression of the right chest (finger thoracostomy or needle) followed by a chest drain
- E. Fluid bolus and noradrenaline

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis and needs immediate decompression; in a ventilated patient a finger thoracostomy (4th/5th intercostal space, anterior to the mid-axillary line) is effective, followed by a chest drain.

**Why not the others:**
- A – waiting for imaging delays life-saving treatment.
- B – ultrasound may support but must not delay decompression in obstructive shock.
- C – higher PEEP worsens tension physiology.
- E – fluids/vasopressors do not treat obstructive shock.

**Ref:** ATLS 11th ed., ACS 2025.

</details>

**10.** Regarding damage-control surgery and the ICU phase:

- A. Damage-control surgery is indicated in haemorrhagic shock with coagulopathy, hypothermia and acidosis. — T / F
- B. The ICU phase aims to rewarm the patient and correct coagulopathy and acidosis before definitive repair. — T / F
- C. Planned re-look surgery is typically performed 24–48 hours after the index operation. — T / F

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- B (True) – restoring physiology is the purpose of stage 2.

**11.** A 31-year-old man after a motorcycle crash receives 6 units of red cells in the first hour through a massive transfusion protocol. Ionised calcium is 0.82 mmol/L. What is the most appropriate management?

- A. Calcium chloride IV, aiming for ionised calcium 1.1–1.3 mmol/L
- B. No treatment; hypocalcaemia resolves when transfusion stops
- C. Sodium bicarbonate to correct acidosis, which will raise ionised calcium
- D. Switch to whole blood, which contains no citrate
- E. Recombinant factor VIIa

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Citrate in blood products chelates calcium; hypocalcaemia impairs coagulation and contractility and is associated with mortality. The European guideline recommends monitoring and keeping ionised calcium in the normal range with calcium chloride (1C).

**Why not the others:**
- B – ongoing hypocalcaemia worsens bleeding and haemodynamics.
- C – alkalinisation actually **lowers** ionised calcium.
- D – whole blood is also citrated.
- E – rFVIIa is not recommended first line (1B) and does not treat hypocalcaemia.

**Ref:** Rossaint R, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

**O1. (open)** List the principles of damage-control resuscitation in a bleeding trauma patient without head injury.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Minimise time to haemorrhage control (surgery/angioembolisation), tourniquets, pelvic binder.
- Permissive hypotension SBP 80–90 / MAP 50–60 mmHg until bleeding stops; noradrenaline if needed.
- Minimise crystalloid (isotonic only), avoid colloids; early MTP with plasma:RBC ≥ 1:2 (1:1:1) or factor concentrates; Hb 70–90 g/L.
- TXA 1 g + 1 g within 3 h; fibrinogen when < 1.5 g/L; platelets > 50 × 10⁹/L.
- Ionised calcium 1.1–1.3 mmol/L; prevent hypothermia; correct acidosis by restoring perfusion.
- Repeated lactate/base deficit and coagulation (standard or VEM) monitoring with goal-directed therapy.
- Damage-control surgery when physiology is deranged.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain the mechanisms of acute traumatic coagulopathy.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Endogenous coagulopathy driven by tissue injury plus hypoperfusion, present on arrival.
- Thrombomodulin–thrombin complex activates protein C → inactivation of factors Va/VIIIa and consumption of PAI-1 → hyperfibrinolysis.
- Endothelial glycocalyx shedding (autoheparinisation), platelet dysfunction, early fibrinogen depletion.
- Amplified by hypothermia, acidosis, hypocalcaemia and dilution (crystalloids, red cells only).
- Fibrinolytic phenotypes: hyperfibrinolysis, physiological, shutdown.

</details>


## W23 · D2 – Haemorrhagic shock and tranexamic acid (CRASH-2, CRASH-3, PATCH-Trauma)

**1.** A 38-year-old man with a crush injury of the pelvis arrives 4.5 hours after injury because of a prolonged extrication. He is bleeding with BP 85/50 mmHg and has not received tranexamic acid. What is the most appropriate approach to TXA?

- A. Give 1 g over 10 min followed by 1 g over 8 h
- B. Give a 2 g bolus because of the delay
- C. Do not start TXA now
- D. Give TXA only if ROTEM shows a maximum lysis > 3 %
- E. Give 3 g over 24 h

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In the CRASH-2 exploratory analysis, TXA given more than 3 h after injury increased death due to bleeding (RR 1.44). The European guideline recommends TXA within 3 h of injury only.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the standard regimen, but only within 3 h.
- B – a larger dose does not compensate for the time window.
- D – ROTEM-guided late TXA is not evidence-based; hyperfibrinolysis is rare by this time and shutdown is more common.
- E – this is the HALT-IT (GI bleeding) regimen, which was ineffective.

**Ref:** CRASH-2 collaborators, Lancet 2011; Rossaint R, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**3.** A 46-year-old woman is admitted 1 hour after a fall with an isolated head injury (GCS 11) and a small subdural haematoma on CT. There is no extracranial bleeding. What does CRASH-3 suggest about tranexamic acid in her case?

- A. It reduces head-injury death in mild-to-moderate TBI when given within 3 hours
- B. It is contraindicated because it increases thrombotic events in TBI
- C. Benefit is greatest in patients with bilateral unreactive pupils
- D. It should only be given after a repeat CT shows haematoma expansion
- E. It reduces death only when given more than 3 hours after injury

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** CRASH-3's primary outcome was neutral overall, but in mild-to-moderate TBI (GCS 9–15) head-injury death fell from 7.5 to 5.8 % (RR 0.78), with greater benefit the earlier TXA was given.

**Why not the others:**
- B – vascular occlusive events were 1.6 % in both groups.
- C – no benefit was seen in severe TBI or with unreactive pupils.
- D – benefit is time-dependent; waiting for expansion loses it.
- E – the trial included only patients treated within 3 h.

**Ref:** CRASH-3 trial collaborators, Lancet 2019.

</details>

**5.** A 60-year-old man on warfarin for a mechanical mitral valve has a splenic laceration with active bleeding and an INR of 3.4. Angioembolisation is being arranged. Which is the most appropriate reversal?

- A. FFP 15 mL/kg alone
- B. Vitamin K 10 mg orally
- C. Recombinant factor VIIa
- D. Idarucizumab 5 g
- E. Four-factor PCC 25–50 U/kg plus vitamin K 5–10 mg IV

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** For bleeding trauma patients on VKA the European guideline recommends early PCC with 5–10 mg IV vitamin K (1A). PCC corrects INR within minutes; vitamin K sustains the effect. A mechanical valve does not change the need for reversal in life-threatening bleeding.

**Why not the others:**
- A – FFP is slower, needs large volumes and corrects INR incompletely.
- B – oral vitamin K takes hours and is insufficient alone.
- C – rFVIIa is not recommended for VKA reversal.
- D – idarucizumab reverses dabigatran only.

**Ref:** Rossaint R, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

**8.** Regarding tranexamic acid pharmacology:

## W23 · D3 – Thoracic and abdominal trauma

**1.** A 27-year-old man with a stab wound to the left chest has a chest drain inserted; 1,700 mL of blood drains immediately and he remains hypotensive despite transfusion. What is the next step?

- A. Clamp the drain to tamponade the bleeding
- B. Thoracotomy
- C. Insert a second chest drain
- D. CT angiography of the chest
- E. Observe drainage for the next 4 hours

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** An initial drainage of ≥ 1,500 mL, ongoing loss > 200 mL/h for 2–4 h, or persistent transfusion requirement indicates massive haemothorax requiring surgical control.

**Why not the others:**
- A – clamping does not stop bleeding and risks tension haemothorax.
- C – a second drain does not address the source.
- D – CT in an unstable patient delays haemorrhage control.
- E – observation is appropriate only when initial loss is small and the patient is stable.

**Ref:** ATLS 11th ed., ACS 2025; Rossaint R, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

- B. It is recommended in blunt trauma patients without signs of life. — T / F
- C. Cross-clamping of the descending aorta is one of its aims. — T / F
- D. Survival after blunt trauma is similar to that after penetrating thoracic trauma. — T / F

**11.** A 19-year-old man is found unconscious after an opioid overdose, having lain on his left arm for an estimated 14 hours. CK is 38,000 U/L, creatinine 210 µmol/L, K⁺ 6.8 mmol/L with peaked T waves. What is the immediate priority?

- A. Forearm fasciotomy
- B. IV calcium and insulin–glucose, followed by urgent renal replacement therapy if refractory
- C. Sodium bicarbonate infusion to a urine pH > 6.5 as the only treatment
- D. Furosemide 80 mg IV
- E. Repeat potassium measurement in 4 hours

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Hyperkalaemia with ECG changes is immediately life-threatening: membrane stabilisation with calcium, shift with insulin–glucose (± salbutamol), then removal – in rhabdomyolysis with AKI, potassium is continuously released and early dialysis is often needed.

**Why not the others:**
- A – compartment syndrome must be assessed, but hyperkalaemia with ECG changes comes first.
- C – bicarbonate alone is insufficient and unproven.
- D – furosemide is ineffective in oliguric AKI and may worsen volume depletion.
- E – delay risks cardiac arrest.

**Ref:** Bosch X, N Engl J Med 2009.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Outline the medical management of a crush victim from entrapment to the ICU.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Pre-extrication isotonic saline ~ 1 L/h (500 mL/h if > 2 h), no K⁺-containing fluids; anticipate hyperkalaemic arrest on release (ECG, calcium, insulin–glucose, binders).
- After extrication 3–6 L in the first 6 h, then titrate to UO ~ 200–300 mL/h; catheterise; monitor for overload, especially if anuric.
- Labs: K⁺, CK, creatinine, Ca²⁺, phosphate, pH; dipstick (blood without RBC).
- Bicarbonate/mannitol optional, unproven; treat hypocalcaemia only if symptomatic/hyperkalaemic.
- Early RRT for refractory K⁺, acidosis, overload; triage dialysis resources in disasters.
- Fasciotomy only for established compartment syndrome; wound care, tetanus, antibiotics for open wounds.

</details>

**O2. (open)** How should an ICU prepare for and run a mass casualty incident?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

- Surge: cancel electives, reverse triage, expand ICU into PACU/theatres, ventilators, oxygen, drugs, blood products.
