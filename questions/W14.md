## W14 · D1 – Review: sepsis + infections

**1.** A 61-year-old with pneumonia had a baseline SOFA score of 0; his SOFA is now 3. After adequate fluids he needs noradrenaline to keep MAP ≥ 65 mmHg, and his lactate is 1.6 mmol/L. According to Sepsis-3, he has:

- A. Septic shock, because the SOFA score rose by ≥ 2 points
- B. Sepsis, but not septic shock, because lactate is not > 2 mmol/L
- C. Severe sepsis
- D. Septic shock, because a vasopressor alone is sufficient for the diagnosis
- E. SIRS without sepsis, because qSOFA has not been reported

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Sepsis-3 defines sepsis as infection with an acute SOFA rise of ≥ 2 points, which he has. Septic shock additionally requires **both** a vasopressor to keep MAP ≥ 65 mmHg **and** lactate > 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids; with lactate 1.6 mmol/L he does not qualify.

**Why not the others:**
- A – ΔSOFA ≥ 2 defines sepsis, not septic shock.
- C – "Severe sepsis" was abandoned by Sepsis-3 in 2016.
- D – a vasopressor alone is not enough; the lactate criterion must also be met.
- E – qSOFA is a prognostic prompt, not a diagnostic criterion, and SIRS is no longer used to define sepsis.

**EDIC trap:** shock = vasopressor **and** lactate > 2 – both, after fluids.

**Ref:** Singer M et al., Sepsis-3, JAMA 2016. Review: [W01/D1](#W01-D1).

</details>

**2.** Regarding sepsis screening and SOFA-2:

- A. SOFA-2 (JAMA 2025) scores six organ systems, including hemostasis. — T / F
- B. SOFA-2 includes a gastrointestinal dysfunction component. — T / F
- C. SSC 2026 prefers NEWS/NEWS2/MEWS or SIRS over qSOFA for screening. — T / F
- D. qSOFA ≥ 2 in a patient with infection defines sepsis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – SOFA-2 keeps six systems: brain, respiratory, cardiovascular, liver, kidney and hemostasis, with updated thresholds.
- B (False) – gastrointestinal and immune dysfunction were considered but not included in SOFA-2.
- C (True) – SSC 2026 prefers NEWS/NEWS2/MEWS or SIRS over qSOFA, which has poor sensitivity for screening.
- D (False) – qSOFA ≥ 2 only flags a higher risk of death; sepsis is defined by ΔSOFA ≥ 2.

**EDIC trap:** qSOFA is neither a definition nor a good screening tool.

**Ref:** Ranzani OT et al., SOFA-2, JAMA 2025; Prescott HC et al., SSC 2026, Intensive Care Med 2026. Review: [W01/D1](#W01-D1).

</details>

**3.** A 54-year-old on the ward has fever and a mildly raised respiratory rate. Blood pressure is normal and lactate is 1.4 mmol/L. Infection is possible, but the source is unclear. Per SSC 2026, the most appropriate approach is:

- A. Antibiotics only if qSOFA is ≥ 2
- B. Rapid, time-limited assessment; give antibiotics within 3 hours of first suspicion if concern for infection persists
- C. No antibiotics until blood culture results are available
- D. Broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour, because any suspected sepsis needs them within 1 hour
- E. Antibiotics within 6 hours, together with ICU admission

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** This is *possible* sepsis without shock. SSC 2026 suggests a rapid, time-limited assessment and antibiotics within 3 hours of first recognition if concern for infection persists (conditional). If infection becomes unlikely, antibiotics can be deferred while monitoring continues.

**Why not the others:**
- A – qSOFA is not used to trigger or withhold antibiotics.
- C – cultures should be drawn first, but treatment is never delayed until results are back.
- D – the 1-hour target applies to septic shock and to probable/definite sepsis, not to every possible case.
- E – 6 hours is too long, and ICU admission is not indicated in a stable patient.

**Ref:** Prescott HC et al., SSC 2026, Intensive Care Med 2026. Review: [W01/D2](#W01-D2).

</details>

**4.** Regarding fluid therapy in sepsis (SSC 2026):

- A. Albumin should not be used in patients with traumatic brain injury. — T / F
- B. Lactate should be followed serially, but fluids should not be given solely to normalise it. — T / F
- C. At least 30 mL/kg within 3 h is suggested for hypoperfusion/shock, but the volume should be individualised. — T / F
- D. Active de-resuscitation after stabilisation is a new element of the 2026 guideline. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – SAFE-TBI showed higher mortality with albumin in traumatic brain injury; albumin is not used routinely in sepsis and never in TBI.
- B (True) – measure lactate and follow it serially, but do not give fluid solely to normalise it.
- C (True) – ≥ 30 mL/kg crystalloid within 3 h is suggested for hypoperfusion/shock, then individualised by reassessment.
- D (True) – SSC 2026 adds active de-resuscitation (fluid removal) once the patient is stabilised.

**EDIC trap:** lactate kinetics are slow and partly type B – chasing a normal value causes fluid overload.

**Ref:** Prescott HC et al., SSC 2026, Intensive Care Med 2026; SAFE-TBI investigators, N Engl J Med 2007. Review: [W01/D3](#W01-D3), [W01/D2](#W01-D2).

</details>

**5.** A 66-year-old with septic shock has an adequate MAP on noradrenaline. Lactate stays high and echocardiography shows severe LV systolic dysfunction with low cardiac output. Which statement is correct?

- A. Dobutamine (or adrenaline) may be added; levosimendan is not suggested
- B. Switching noradrenaline to dopamine is preferred because it has inotropic effects
- C. Levosimendan is the inotrope of choice because LeoPARDS showed lower mortality
- D. Raising the MAP target to 85 mmHg is the next step
- E. Inotropes are contraindicated in septic shock

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** With adequate MAP but persistent hypoperfusion and documented cardiac dysfunction, SSC suggests adding dobutamine to noradrenaline or switching to adrenaline. Levosimendan is not suggested because LeoPARDS showed no benefit and more tachyarrhythmias.

**Why not the others:**
- B – dopamine is not recommended; SOAP II showed more arrhythmias than with noradrenaline.
- C – LeoPARDS was negative for organ dysfunction and mortality.
- D – raising MAP to 80–85 mmHg (SEPSISPAM) did not improve survival and does not address low cardiac output.
- E – inotropes are indicated when cardiac dysfunction causes hypoperfusion.

**Ref:** Prescott HC et al., SSC 2026, Intensive Care Med 2026; Gordon AC et al., LeoPARDS, N Engl J Med 2016; De Backer D et al., SOAP II, N Engl J Med 2010. Review: [W01/D4](#W01-D4).

</details>

**6.** Regarding corticosteroids in septic shock:

- A. Hydrocortisone 200 mg/day is indicated when noradrenaline/adrenaline ≥ 0.25 µg/kg/min has been needed for ≥ 4 h. — T / F
- B. An ACTH stimulation test should be used to select patients for hydrocortisone. — T / F
- C. In APROCCHSS, hydrocortisone was given without fludrocortisone. — T / F
- D. In ADRENAL, hydrocortisone did not change mortality but led to faster resolution of shock. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – IV hydrocortisone 200 mg/day is suggested for persistent need of noradrenaline/adrenaline ≥ 0.25 µg/kg/min for ≥ 4 h.
- B (False) – the ACTH stimulation test is not used to select patients.
- C (False) – APROCCHSS combined hydrocortisone **with** fludrocortisone 50 µg and lowered 90-day mortality (43.0 vs 49.1 %).
- D (True) – ADRENAL showed no difference in 90-day mortality but faster shock reversal and shorter ventilation.

**EDIC trap:** ADRENAL (hydrocortisone alone) negative for mortality, APROCCHSS (plus fludrocortisone) positive.

**Ref:** Venkatesh B et al., ADRENAL, N Engl J Med 2018; Annane D et al., APROCCHSS, N Engl J Med 2018; Prescott HC et al., SSC 2026, Intensive Care Med 2026. Review: [W01/D5](#W01-D5).

</details>

**7.** A 24-year-old trauma patient with septic shock from pneumonia has a measured creatinine clearance of 180 mL/min/1.73 m². He is on standard-dose meropenem. The main concern is:

- A. No concern; high clearance only matters for aminoglycosides
- B. Meropenem is concentration-dependent, so a single daily dose is preferable
- C. The loading dose should be halved
- D. Neurotoxicity due to accumulation
- E. Underdosing due to augmented renal clearance; consider prolonged infusion and TDM

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Augmented renal clearance (> 130 mL/min/1.73 m²) is common in young trauma and septic patients. Together with a larger Vd and hypoalbuminaemia, it puts renally cleared hydrophilic drugs such as meropenem at risk of underdosing. β-lactams are time-dependent (fT>MIC), so give a loading bolus, then prolonged or continuous infusion, guided by TDM.

**Why not the others:**
- A – ARC matters most for renally cleared β-lactams and vancomycin.
- B – meropenem is time-dependent, so once-daily dosing would leave long sub-MIC periods.
- C – the loading dose depends on Vd, not on clearance, and should not be reduced.
- D – accumulation is a risk in renal failure, not in ARC.

**Ref:** Abdul-Aziz MH et al., ESICM/ESCMID position paper, Intensive Care Med 2020. Review: [W02/D1](#W02-D1).

</details>

**8.** Regarding PK/PD of antimicrobials in critical illness:

- A. The loading dose does not depend on renal function. — T / F
- B. The efficacy of β-lactams depends on the time the free concentration stays above the MIC. — T / F
- C. Aminoglycosides are concentration-dependent (Cmax/MIC). — T / F
- D. The current vancomycin target is an AUC/MIC of ≥ 400 rather than a trough of 15–20 mg/L. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – the loading dose is determined by the volume of distribution, so it is not reduced in renal failure.
- B (True) – β-lactams are time-dependent; the target is the fraction of the dosing interval with free concentration above the MIC (fT>MIC).
- C (True) – aminoglycosides are concentration-dependent (Cmax/MIC ≈ 8–10), hence once-daily high doses.
- D (True) – the 2020 consensus recommends AUC/MIC ≥ 400 (≈ 400–600), because the trough is a poor surrogate for AUC and high troughs raise nephrotoxicity.

**Ref:** Rybak MJ et al., ASHP/IDSA/PIDS/SIDP vancomycin consensus, Am J Health Syst Pharm 2020; Abdul-Aziz MH et al., Intensive Care Med 2020. Review: [W02/D1](#W02-D1).

</details>

**9.** A stable patient on day 4 has *E. coli* bacteraemia from a urinary source. He is now afebrile and the isolate is susceptible. Based on BALANCE, the total duration of therapy can be:

- A. 14 days, because bacteraemia always requires 2 weeks
- B. 5 days
- C. 10 days
- D. 7 days
- E. 3 days

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** BALANCE randomised 3608 patients with bloodstream infection, mostly Gram-negative and many in ICU. Seven days was non-inferior to 14 days (90-day mortality 14.5 vs 16.1 %). This stable patient with source control and a susceptible isolate fits the trial.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 14 days is no longer required for uncomplicated Gram-negative bacteraemia.
- B – 5 days was not tested in BALANCE.
- C – 10 days is not the duration supported by the trial.
- E – 3 days is too short for bacteraemia.

**EDIC trap:** BALANCE excluded *S. aureus* bacteraemia and candidaemia.

**Ref:** Daneman N et al., BALANCE, N Engl J Med 2025. Review: [W02/D2](#W02-D2).

</details>

**10.** Regarding de-escalation and duration of therapy:

- A. iDIAPASON demonstrated that 8 days is non-inferior to 15 days for *Pseudomonas* VAP. — T / F
- B. De-escalation means narrowing the spectrum or reducing the number of antimicrobials. — T / F
- C. De-escalation is synonymous with shortening the duration of therapy. — T / F
- D. In PneumA, 8 days of therapy for VAP gave higher mortality than 15 days. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – iDIAPASON did **not** demonstrate non-inferiority of 8 days for *Pseudomonas* VAP; recurrences were more frequent with the short course.
- B (True) – de-escalation means narrowing the spectrum or stopping unnecessary agents once cultures are known.
- C (False) – shortening duration is a separate stewardship decision.
- D (False) – in PneumA, mortality was similar with 8 and 15 days, but recurrences with non-fermenting Gram-negative bacilli were more frequent with 8 days.

**Ref:** Chastre J et al., PneumA, JAMA 2003; Bouglé A et al., iDIAPASON, Lancet Respir Med 2022; Tabah A et al., ESICM/ESCMID position statement, Intensive Care Med 2020. Review: [W02/D2](#W02-D2), [W02/D5](#W02-D5).

**11.** A ventilated patient has bacteraemia with *Enterobacter cloacae* (a chromosomal AmpC producer) that tests susceptible to third-generation cephalosporins. The preferred definitive agent (IDSA 2024) is:

- A. Aztreonam
- B. Cefepime
- C. Ceftazidime-avibactam
- D. Colistin monotherapy
- E. Ceftriaxone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** *Enterobacter cloacae*, *K. aerogenes* and *C. freundii* carry inducible chromosomal AmpC. Third-generation cephalosporins can select derepressed mutants during therapy, even if the isolate tests susceptible. IDSA 2024 therefore suggests cefepime for invasive infections, as it is stable to AmpC.

**Why not the others:**
- A – aztreonam is hydrolysed by AmpC and is not a preferred agent.
- C – ceftazidime-avibactam is reserved for carbapenem-resistant organisms.
- D – colistin monotherapy is never preferred when better options exist.
- E – ceftriaxone risks AmpC derepression and treatment failure in invasive infection.

**EDIC trap:** cefepime is fine for AmpC, but not for ESBL (use a carbapenem).

**Ref:** Tamma PD et al., IDSA 2024 Guidance, Clin Infect Dis 2024. Review: [W02/D3](#W02-D3).

</details>

**12.** Regarding multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria:

- A. Sulbactam has intrinsic activity against *Acinetobacter baumannii* through PBP binding. — T / F
- B. Durlobactam protects sulbactam from the OXA β-lactamases of *Acinetobacter*. — T / F
- C. Combination therapy is routinely preferred when the strain is susceptible to one of the new β-lactam agents. — T / F
- D. Ceftolozane-tazobactam is an option for pneumonia caused by DTR *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – sulbactam has intrinsic activity against *A. baumannii* by binding PBP1 and PBP3.
- B (True) – durlobactam inhibits class A, C and D (OXA) β-lactamases and restores sulbactam activity (ATTACK trial).
- C (False) – when the strain is susceptible to a new β-lactam agent, it is used as **monotherapy**.
- D (True) – ceftolozane-tazobactam is a preferred option for DTR *P. aeruginosa*, alongside ceftazidime-avibactam and imipenem-relebactam.

**Ref:** Tamma PD et al., IDSA 2024 Guidance, Clin Infect Dis 2024; Paul M et al., ESCMID guidelines, Clin Microbiol Infect 2022; Kaye KS et al., ATTACK, Lancet Infect Dis 2023. Review: [W02/D3](#W02-D3).

</details>

**13.** Which trial showed higher 30-day mortality with piperacillin-tazobactam than with meropenem (12.3 vs 3.7 %) for bloodstream infection caused by ceftriaxone-resistant *E. coli*/*Klebsiella*?

- A. AIDA
- B. ReSTORE
- C. CREDIBLE-CR
- D. MERINO
- E. BALANCE

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** MERINO randomised patients with ceftriaxone-resistant *E. coli* or *Klebsiella* bacteraemia to piperacillin-tazobactam or meropenem. Thirty-day mortality was 12.3 vs 3.7 %, so non-inferiority was not shown. This is why a carbapenem is the definitive therapy for ESBL bacteraemia.

**Why not the others:**
- A – AIDA compared colistin with colistin plus meropenem for carbapenem-resistant Gram-negatives.
- B – ReSTORE tested rezafungin vs caspofungin in candidaemia.
- C – CREDIBLE-CR tested cefiderocol vs best available therapy.
- E – BALANCE compared 7 vs 14 days of therapy for bacteraemia.

**Ref:** Harris PNA et al., MERINO, JAMA 2018. Review: [W02/D3](#W02-D3).

</details>

**14.** Regarding candidaemia:

- A. An echinocandin is first-line, with step-down to fluconazole after 5–7 days in a stable patient with negative cultures and a susceptible isolate. — T / F
- B. *Candida* grown from the tracheal aspirate of a ventilated patient should usually be treated. — T / F
- C. Once-weekly rezafungin was non-inferior to caspofungin. — T / F
- D. Treatment lasts 14 days from the first negative blood culture. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – an echinocandin is first-line; step down to fluconazole after 5–7 days if the patient is stable, cultures are negative and the isolate is susceptible.

**15.** A ventilated COVID-19 patient has a new infiltrate that no other cause explains. Which finding satisfies the mycological criterion for *probable* CAPA (ECMM/ISHAM 2020)?

- A. Serum galactomannan 0.3
- B. A raised procalcitonin
- C. Presence of a classical host factor (e.g. neutropenia)
- D. BAL galactomannan index 1.2
- E. *Candida albicans* in the tracheal aspirate

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** ECMM/ISHAM criteria for probable CAPA require a compatible clinical picture plus a mycological criterion, such as BAL galactomannan ≥ 1.0. Serum GM > 0.5, positive BAL culture or PCR also count. Treat with voriconazole or isavuconazole.

**Why not the others:**
- A – serum GM must be > 0.5; 0.3 is below the threshold.
- B – procalcitonin is not a mycological criterion.
- C – classical host factors are not required for CAPA, and a host factor is not a mycological criterion.
- E – *Candida* in the tracheal aspirate is colonisation and says nothing about *Aspergillus*.

**Ref:** Koehler P et al., ECMM/ISHAM consensus, Lancet Infect Dis 2021. Review: [W02/D4](#W02-D4).

</details>

**17.** A patient with a CVC has fever. Paired blood cultures are drawn simultaneously from the catheter and a peripheral vein. Both grow *S. epidermidis*; the catheter sample turns positive 2.5 h earlier. This finding:

- A. Is only meaningful if the catheter tip grows < 15 CFU
- B. Excludes CRBSI because coagulase-negative staphylococci are always contaminants
- C. Requires a guidewire exchange of the catheter to eradicate the biofilm
- D. Fulfils the CLABSI definition only if the catheter has been in place < 48 h
- E. Supports CRBSI (differential time to positivity ≥ 2 h)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** When paired cultures are taken at the same time, a catheter sample that turns positive ≥ 2 h before the peripheral sample (differential time to positivity) supports CRBSI. Both samples grew the same CoNS, so contamination is unlikely.

**Why not the others:**
- A – a tip culture supports CRBSI at ≥ 15 CFU (semi-quantitative), not < 15 CFU.
- B – CoNS in one of two bottles is often a contaminant, but here both samples are positive.
- C – a guidewire exchange does not eradicate the biofilm; the catheter is removed when indicated.
- D – CLABSI requires the line to be in place > 2 days, not < 48 h.

**Ref:** Mermel LA et al., IDSA CRBSI guideline, Clin Infect Dis 2009. Review: [W03/D1](#W03-D1).

</details>

**19.** A patient has *S. aureus* bacteraemia from a short-term CVC. He has been afebrile since 48 h after starting cefazolin. The best plan is:

- A. Remove the catheter and treat for 5–7 days
- B. Keep the catheter with antibiotic lock therapy
- C. Keep the catheter and treat for 7 days
- D. Remove the catheter, perform TEE and treat for ≥ 14 days
- E. Exchange the catheter over a guidewire and treat for 7 days

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** *S. aureus* CRBSI is never treated conservatively. Remove the catheter, perform echocardiography (TEE) to exclude endocarditis, and give at least 14 days of IV therapy from the first negative culture. Longer therapy is needed if bacteraemia persists or there are metastatic foci.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 5–7 days is too short for *S. aureus*.
- B – catheter salvage with lock therapy is not acceptable for *S. aureus*.
- C – keeping the catheter risks persistent bacteraemia and relapse.
- E – a guidewire exchange leaves the infected tract and biofilm in place.

**EDIC trap:** BALANCE (7 days) excluded *S. aureus*.

**Ref:** Mermel LA et al., IDSA CRBSI guideline, Clin Infect Dis 2009. Review: [W03/D1](#W03-D1).

**20.** Regarding bacterial meningitis:

- B. CT before lumbar puncture is indicated when GCS is < 10. — T / F
- C. In de Gans, dexamethasone reduced mortality in pneumococcal meningitis from 34 % to 14 %. — T / F

**21.** A patient with pneumococcal meningitis caused by a strain with reduced ceftriaxone susceptibility receives ceftriaxone, vancomycin and dexamethasone. Which statement is correct?

- A. Dexamethasone increases vancomycin penetration into CSF
- B. Adding amoxicillin is required to cover the resistant pneumococcus
- C. Dexamethasone should be stopped because it inactivates vancomycin
- D. Vancomycin should be replaced by gentamicin
- E. Dexamethasone reduces vancomycin penetration into CSF; rifampicin is sometimes added, but dexamethasone is not omitted

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Dexamethasone reduces meningeal inflammation, so less vancomycin crosses the blood–brain barrier. Some guidelines add rifampicin for resistant strains. Dexamethasone is still given, because its mortality benefit in pneumococcal meningitis is proven.

**Why not the others:**
- A – dexamethasone reduces, not increases, vancomycin penetration.
- B – amoxicillin is added for *Listeria* (age > 50, immunosuppression), not for resistant pneumococci.
- C – dexamethasone does not inactivate vancomycin.
- D – gentamicin penetrates CSF poorly and has no role against pneumococci.

**Ref:** van de Beek D et al., ESCMID guideline, Clin Microbiol Infect 2016. Review: [W03/D2](#W03-D2).

**22.** Regarding necrotising soft-tissue infection (NSTI):

- A. An LRINEC score < 6 reliably excludes NSTI. — T / F
- B. A planned second-look operation within 24 h is standard. — T / F
- C. Hyperbaric oxygen is recommended as an adjunct. — T / F
- D. In INSTINCT, IVIG improved physical function. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – LRINEC ≥ 6 has a sensitivity of only about 68 %, so a low score cannot exclude NSTI; only surgical exploration does.

**EDIC trap:** consider IVIG only for streptococcal toxic shock syndrome.

**1.** A patient has SaO₂ 98 % and mixed venous saturation (SvO₂) 58 %. The approximate oxygen extraction ratio (O₂ER) is:

- B. 69 %
- C. 41 %
- D. 20 %

**Why not the others:**
- A – 59 % would need an SvO₂ of about 40 %.
- B – 69 % would need an SvO₂ of about 30 %.
- D – 20 % is lower than the normal resting extraction.

- B – Bronchodilators do not relieve tube obstruction or displacement.
