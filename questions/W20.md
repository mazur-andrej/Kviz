## W20 · D1 – Upper GI bleeding: non-variceal and variceal haemorrhage

**1.** A 54-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis (Child–Pugh C, 11 points) is admitted with haematemesis. After terlipressin, ceftriaxone and band ligation of an actively bleeding oesophageal varix 6 hours ago, he is haemodynamically stable. What is the best next step?

- A. Repeat endoscopy at 24 hours to confirm haemostasis
- B. Continue terlipressin for 5 days and start carvedilol at discharge
- C. Insert a Sengstaken–Blakemore tube prophylactically for 24 hours
- D. Placement of a covered TIPS within 72 hours
- E. Start propranolol immediately while continuing terlipressin

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Child–Pugh C 10–13 points (or Child B > 7 with active bleeding) identifies high risk of treatment failure and death. Pre-emptive covered TIPS within 72 h (ideally < 24 h) improved 1-year survival (86 vs 61 %) in García-Pagán's trial and is recommended by Baveno VII/VIII.

**Why not the others:**
- A – routine second-look endoscopy is not recommended.
- B – drugs + EVL alone are inferior to p-TIPS in this high-risk group.
- C – balloon tamponade is reserved as a temporary bridge for refractory bleeding (SEMS preferred).
- E – NSBB are started after the vasoactive drug is stopped, and do not replace p-TIPS.

**EDIC trap:** high bilirubin, HE or ACLF are not absolute contraindications to p-TIPS (Baveno VIII).

**Ref:** García-Pagán JC, N Engl J Med 2010; Baveno VIII, J Hepatol 2026.

</details>

**2.** Regarding red cell transfusion in acute upper GI bleeding:

- A. In the Villanueva trial, a restrictive threshold of 7 g/dL improved 6-week survival compared with 9 g/dL. — T / F
- B. A liberal transfusion strategy lowers the hepatic venous pressure gradient in cirrhosis. — T / F
- C. ESGE recommends a post-transfusion target of at least 10 g/dL in patients with acute coronary syndrome. — T / F
- D. Fresh frozen plasma should be given to cirrhotic patients with an INR > 1.5 before variceal ligation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – survival 95 vs 91 % (HR 0.55), with less rebleeding and fewer adverse events.
- B (False) – liberal transfusion **increased** HVPG in the first days, promoting rebleeding.
- C (True) – with cardiovascular disease ESGE transfuses at ≤ 8 g/dL with a target ≥ 10 g/dL.
- D (False) – INR does not reflect bleeding risk in cirrhosis; FFP is not recommended (Baveno VIII).

**Ref:** Villanueva C, N Engl J Med 2013; Gralnek IM, Endoscopy 2021; Baveno VIII, J Hepatol 2026.

</details>

**3.** A 32-year-old woman presents to the emergency department after one episode of melaena. HR 82/min, BP 128/76 mmHg, Hb 13.6 g/dL, urea 5.0 mmol/L, no syncope, no comorbidities. Her Glasgow–Blatchford score is 1. What is the most appropriate management?

- A. ICU admission and endoscopy within 6 hours
- B. Outpatient management with outpatient endoscopy
- C. Admission and IV pantoprazole infusion for 72 hours
- D. CT angiography
- E. Transfusion of 1 unit of red cells

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** A Glasgow–Blatchford score ≤ 1 identifies very low risk of needing intervention or of death; ESGE strongly recommends outpatient management without urgent inpatient endoscopy.

**Why not the others:**
- A – emergent endoscopy is not recommended even in high-risk NVUGIB.
- C – high-dose PPI infusion is for high-risk stigmata after endoscopic therapy.
- D – CTA is for active bleeding not localised at endoscopy.
- E – Hb 13.6 g/dL is far above any transfusion threshold.

**Ref:** Gralnek IM, Endoscopy 2021.

</details>

**4.** A 61-year-old man with known cirrhosis presents with haematemesis, HR 118/min and BP 92/55 mmHg. All of the following are recommended in the first hours EXCEPT:

- A. Terlipressin or somatostatin started before endoscopy
- B. Intravenous ceftriaxone
- C. Erythromycin 250 mg IV before endoscopy
- D. Lactulose to prevent hepatic encephalopathy
- E. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV followed by an infusion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** HALT-IT (n = 12,009, many with liver disease and suspected variceal bleeding) showed no reduction in death due to bleeding (4 % in both arms) and more venous thromboembolism with TXA. Baveno VIII and ESGE recommend against it.

**Why not the others:**
- A – vasoactive drugs should be started as soon as variceal bleeding is suspected.
- B – antibiotic prophylaxis reduces infection, rebleeding and mortality.
- C – erythromycin 30–90 min before endoscopy improves visualisation (new Baveno VIII statement).
- D – lactulose is recommended to prevent or treat overt HE.

**Ref:** HALT-IT, Lancet 2020; Baveno VIII, J Hepatol 2026.

</details>

**5.** A 72-year-old man has a 1.5-cm duodenal ulcer at endoscopy. Regarding endoscopic findings and therapy:

- A. A non-bleeding visible vessel (Forrest IIa) should be treated endoscopically. — T / F
- B. Adrenaline injection alone is adequate therapy for an actively spurting ulcer. — T / F
- C. A clean-based ulcer (Forrest III) does not need endoscopic therapy. — T / F
- D. Routine second-look endoscopy at 24 h is not recommended after successful haemostasis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – Forrest IIa carries ~40–50 % rebleeding risk; thermal, mechanical or sclerosant therapy ± adrenaline is recommended.
- B (False) – adrenaline must be combined with a second modality (thermal or clips).
- C (True) – Forrest IIc and III have low rebleeding risk.
- D (True) – second look is reserved for clinical evidence of rebleeding.

**Ref:** Gralnek IM, Endoscopy 2021.

</details>

**6.** A 66-year-old woman with melaena has a Glasgow–Blatchford score of 13. After 2 L of crystalloid and 2 units of red cells, HR is 88/min and BP 118/70 mmHg. When should endoscopy be performed?

- A. Within 24 hours of presentation
- B. Immediately, within 2 hours, irrespective of haemodynamics
- C. Within 6 hours, because urgent endoscopy reduces mortality in high-risk patients
- D. After 48 hours of PPI infusion
- E. Only if bleeding recurs

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** ESGE recommends early endoscopy (≤ 24 h) after haemodynamic resuscitation. In Lau's trial (GBS ≥ 12), endoscopy ≤ 6 h did not reduce 30-day mortality compared with 6–24 h (8.9 vs 6.6 %).

**Why not the others:**
- B – endoscopy before resuscitation is unsafe; she is now stable.
- C – urgent endoscopy did not improve mortality or rebleeding.
- D – delaying beyond 24 h increases risk; PPI does not replace endoscopy.
- E – all patients with GBS > 1 need inpatient endoscopy.

**Ref:** Lau JYW, N Engl J Med 2020; Gralnek IM, Endoscopy 2021.

</details>

**7.** Regarding the HALT-IT trial of tranexamic acid in gastrointestinal bleeding:

- A. It showed a 30 % reduction in death due to bleeding at 5 days. — T / F
- B. The rate of venous thromboembolic events was higher with tranexamic acid. — T / F
- C. It enrolled fewer than 1,000 patients. — T / F
- D. Benefit was confined to patients with variceal bleeding. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – death due to bleeding was 4 % in both groups (RR 0.99).
- B (True) – VTE 0.8 vs 0.4 % (RR 1.85); seizures were also more frequent.
- C (False) – 12,009 patients were randomised.
- D (False) – no subgroup benefited, including suspected variceal bleeding.

**Ref:** HALT-IT Trial Collaborators, Lancet 2020.

</details>

**8.** A 49-year-old man with cirrhosis continues to bleed from oesophageal varices despite terlipressin and two attempts at band ligation. He is intubated; the TIPS team is unavailable until tomorrow. What is the preferred bridging measure?

- A. Fresh frozen plasma 15 mL/kg
- B. Recombinant factor VIIa
- C. A covered self-expanding oesophageal metal stent
- D. Emergency surgical portocaval shunt
- E. Increase terlipressin and wait for TIPS

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Baveno VIII prefers a covered self-expanding metal stent over balloon tamponade as a bridge to TIPS or transplantation in refractory oesophageal variceal bleeding. In Escorsell's RCT the stent controlled bleeding without rebleeding more often (66 vs 20 %) with fewer serious adverse events.

**Why not the others:**
- A – FFP does not correct the coagulopathy of cirrhosis and expands volume.
- B – rFVIIa is not recommended.
- D – surgical shunts carry very high mortality and have been replaced by TIPS.
- E – ongoing bleeding requires a mechanical bridge; escalation of drugs alone fails.

**Ref:** Escorsell À, Hepatology 2016; Baveno VIII, J Hepatol 2026.

</details>

**9.** A 79-year-old woman on apixaban for atrial fibrillation presents with haematemesis, HR 124/min and BP 84/50 mmHg despite initial fluids. What is the most appropriate approach to her anticoagulation?

- A. Continue apixaban and perform endoscopy when anti-Xa activity is undetectable
- B. Withhold apixaban, resuscitate and consider andexanet alfa or PCC, without delaying endoscopy
- C. Give vitamin K 10 mg IV and FFP 4 units
- D. Give idarucizumab 5 g IV
- E. Delay endoscopy for 48 hours until the drug has been eliminated

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** ESGE recommends withholding DOACs, and in severe ongoing bleeding considering a reversal agent (andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors) or IV PCC; endoscopy should not be delayed by antithrombotic use.

**Why not the others:**
- A – continuing anticoagulation during life-threatening bleeding is inappropriate.
- C – vitamin K and FFP do not reverse factor Xa inhibitors.
- D – idarucizumab reverses dabigatran only.
- E – timing of endoscopy should not depend on anticoagulant clearance.

**Ref:** Gralnek IM, Endoscopy 2021.

</details>

**10.** Regarding pharmacological therapy in acute variceal bleeding according to Baveno VIII:

- A. Vasoactive drugs should be started only after endoscopy has confirmed variceal bleeding. — T / F
- B. A third-generation cephalosporin is generally preferred for antibiotic prophylaxis. — T / F
- C. After successful band ligation, a vasoactive course as short as 24 hours can be considered if a non-selective beta-blocker is started. — T / F

- C (True) – new in Baveno VIII; standard duration remains 2–5 days.

</details>

**11.** A 58-year-old man has a gastric ulcer with a spurting vessel (Forrest Ia) treated with adrenaline injection and clips. Which PPI regimen is recommended afterwards?

- A. Oral omeprazole 20 mg once daily
- B. No PPI is needed after successful endoscopic therapy
- C. IV pantoprazole 40 mg once daily for 24 hours

- E. High-dose PPI: 80 mg IV bolus then 8 mg/h for 72 hours (or high-dose intermittent dosing)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** After endoscopic haemostasis of high-risk stigmata, ESGE recommends high-dose PPI for 72 h, preferably 80 mg bolus + 8 mg/h, or high-dose intermittent IV/oral twice daily, then twice-daily oral PPI.

**Why not the others:**
- A – standard-dose once daily is insufficient in the first 72 h.
- B – PPI reduces rebleeding after endoscopic therapy.
- C – dose and duration are inadequate.
- D – H₂ blockers are inferior to PPIs.

**Ref:** Gralnek IM, Endoscopy 2021.

</details>

## W20 · D2 – Acute pancreatitis: severity, fluids (WATERFALL), nutrition, necrosis

**1.** A 46-year-old man with alcohol-related acute pancreatitis develops hypoxaemia (PaO₂/FiO₂ 220) on day 1, which resolves within 36 hours. CT on day 4 shows no necrosis or collections. According to the revised Atlanta classification, his pancreatitis is:

- A. Mild
- B. Severe
- C. Moderately severe
- D. Critical
- E. Cannot be classified until day 7

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Transient organ failure (modified Marshall ≥ 2, resolving < 48 h) defines moderately severe acute pancreatitis, even without local complications.

**Why not the others:**
- A – mild requires no organ failure at all.
- B – severe requires persistent organ failure > 48 h.
- D – "critical" is a category of the determinant-based classification (persistent OF + infected necrosis), not Atlanta.
- E – classification is dynamic and possible once the 48-h window has passed.

**Ref:** Banks PA, Gut 2013.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the WATERFALL trial:

- A. Both arms received Lactated Ringer's solution. — T / F
- B. The aggressive arm received a 20 mL/kg bolus followed by 3 mL/kg/h. — T / F
- C. Aggressive resuscitation reduced progression to moderately severe or severe pancreatitis. — T / F
- D. The trial was stopped early because of more fluid overload in the aggressive arm. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – LR in both arms.
- B (True) – vs 1.5 mL/kg/h (10 mL/kg bolus only if hypovolaemic) in the moderate arm.
- C (False) – 22.1 vs 17.3 % (aRR 1.30, NS) – numerically worse.
- D (True) – fluid overload 20.5 vs 6.3 %; stopped at the first interim analysis for harm.

**Ref:** de-Madaria E, N Engl J Med 2022.

</details>

**3.** A 38-year-old woman is admitted with mild gallstone pancreatitis (no organ failure). Her pain is controlled with opioids on day 2 and she is hungry. Lipase is still 4 × ULN. What is the best nutritional plan?

- A. Start a low-fat solid oral diet now
- B. Keep nil by mouth until lipase normalises
- C. Start clear liquids only for 48 hours
- D. Insert a nasojejunal tube and start enteral feeding
- E. Start parenteral nutrition to rest the pancreas

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** In mild AP, early oral feeding within 24–48 h as tolerated is recommended, starting with a low-fat solid diet; normalisation of lipase is not required.

**Why not the others:**
- B – "resting the pancreas" increases infection and length of stay.
- C – stepwise liquid diets offer no advantage over solid low-fat diet.
- D – tube feeding is for patients who cannot eat (usually severe AP).
- E – PN is reserved for failure of enteral nutrition.

**Ref:** Tenner S, Am J Gastroenterol 2024; Párniczky A, Pancreatology 2025.

</details>

**4.** A 57-year-old man with severe necrotising pancreatitis is in the ICU on day 5. All of the following are appropriate EXCEPT:

- A. Measuring intra-abdominal pressure via the bladder
- B. Enteral nutrition via a nasogastric tube
- C. Opioid analgesia
- D. Goal-directed fluid therapy with Lactated Ringer's
- E. Prophylactic meropenem to prevent infection of the necrosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Prophylactic antibiotics do not reduce infected necrosis or mortality in severe or necrotising AP and are recommended against (ACG 2024, IAP 2025). Antibiotics are given for documented or strongly suspected infection.

**Why not the others:**
- A – IAH is common in severe AP; IAP should be monitored when risk factors are present.
- B – NG feeding is as safe as NJ in severe AP.
- C – opioids are safe and effective.
- D – moderate, goal-directed LR is recommended.

**Ref:** Tenner S, Am J Gastroenterol 2024; Párniczky A, Pancreatology 2025.

</details>

**5.** Regarding local complications of acute pancreatitis (revised Atlanta):

- A. A pseudocyst can be diagnosed 10 days after onset if a fluid collection is present. — T / F
- B. Walled-off necrosis develops more than 4 weeks after onset of necrotising pancreatitis. — T / F
- C. Gas within a necrotic collection on CT suggests infection. — T / F
- D. An acute necrotic collection by definition contains no solid material. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – pseudocysts are encapsulated collections > 4 weeks after interstitial AP; before that it is an APFC.
- B (True) – WON = mature encapsulated necrosis, usually > 4 weeks.
- C (True) – gas (in the absence of recent intervention) is a sign of infected necrosis.
- D (False) – ANC contains fluid and necrotic (solid) tissue.

**Ref:** Banks PA, Gut 2013.

</details>

**6.** A 63-year-old woman with necrotising pancreatitis develops fever and rising CRP on day 14. CT shows gas in a 10-cm heterogeneous acute necrotic collection. She is haemodynamically stable on room air. What is the best initial management?

- A. Immediate percutaneous drainage within 24 hours
- B. Open surgical necrosectomy
- C. CT-guided fine-needle aspiration before any treatment
- D. Broad-spectrum antibiotics with penetration into necrosis, postponing drainage until the collection is walled off
- E. Urgent ERCP with sphincterotomy

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** POINTER showed that postponing intervention until walled-off necrosis (with antibiotics meanwhile) was not inferior to immediate drainage, required fewer interventions, and 39 % of patients needed no intervention. Step-up intervention follows if she fails to improve.

**Why not the others:**
- A – immediate drainage offered no benefit and more procedures.
- B – primary open necrosectomy has more major complications and death (PANTER 69 vs 40 %).
- C – routine FNA is not recommended; gas on CT already indicates infection.
- E – ERCP is not a treatment for infected necrosis.

**Ref:** Boxhoorn L, N Engl J Med 2021; van Santvoort HC, N Engl J Med 2010.

</details>

**7.** Regarding the evidence for interventions in acute pancreatitis:

- A. In PANTER, a step-up approach reduced major complications or death compared with primary open necrosectomy. — T / F
- B. In TENSION, an endoscopic step-up approach caused fewer pancreatic fistulae than a surgical step-up approach. — T / F
- C. In PYTHON, early nasoenteric feeding within 24 h did not reduce major infection or death compared with an on-demand oral diet. — T / F
- D. In APEC, urgent ERCP did not improve outcome in predicted severe gallstone pancreatitis without cholangitis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – 40 vs 69 %; 35 % needed drainage only.
- B (True) – 5 vs 32 %, shorter stay; primary endpoint not different (43 vs 45 %).
- C (True) – 30 vs 27 %.
- D (True) – 38 vs 44 %, NS.

**Ref:** van Santvoort HC, N Engl J Med 2010; van Brunschot S, Lancet 2018; Bakker OJ, N Engl J Med 2014; Schepens NJ, Lancet 2020.

</details>

**8.** A 52-year-old man with gallstone pancreatitis has fever 39.2 °C, rigors, jaundice (bilirubin 85 µmol/L) and a dilated common bile duct. What is the most appropriate intervention?

- A. Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy within 24 hours
- B. ERCP with sphincterotomy within 24 hours
- C. ERCP after 2 weeks of antibiotics
- D. Percutaneous drainage of the peripancreatic fluid
- E. MRCP followed by conservative management

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Acute cholangitis complicating gallstone pancreatitis is an indication for urgent ERCP (< 24 h) with biliary drainage, in addition to antibiotics.

**Why not the others:**
- A – cholecystectomy does not drain the infected bile duct and is deferred.
- C – delayed drainage in cholangitis increases mortality.
- D – the source is biliary, not a collection.
- E – MRCP delays treatment; conservative care is for patients without cholangitis (APEC).

**Ref:** Tenner S, Am J Gastroenterol 2024; Schepens NJ, Lancet 2020.

</details>

**9.** A 41-year-old man with poorly controlled diabetes presents with acute pancreatitis. Serum is lipaemic; triglycerides are 28 mmol/L. Glucose is 16 mmol/L; there is no organ failure. Which initial treatment is most appropriate to lower triglycerides?

- A. Oral fenofibrate only
- B. Prophylactic imipenem
- C. Unfractionated heparin bolus to activate lipoprotein lipase
- D. Urgent ERCP
- E. Intravenous insulin infusion with glucose as needed

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** IV insulin activates lipoprotein lipase and lowers triglycerides rapidly; glucose is added to avoid hypoglycaemia. Plasmapheresis is reserved for selected severe cases.

**Why not the others:**
- A – oral agents are for long-term prevention, not acute lowering.
- B – prophylactic antibiotics are not indicated.
- C – heparin causes only transient lipolysis followed by depletion of lipoprotein lipase, and risks haemorrhagic pancreatitis.
- D – there is no biliary indication.

**Ref:** Párniczky A, Pancreatology 2025; Tenner S, Am J Gastroenterol 2024.

</details>

**10.** Regarding diagnosis and severity assessment in acute pancreatitis:

- A. A lipase of 20 × ULN predicts severe disease better than a lipase of 4 × ULN. — T / F
- B. Contrast-enhanced CT is recommended within 12 hours of admission in all patients. — T / F
- C. Persistent SIRS for more than 48 hours is associated with increased mortality. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – enzyme level does not correlate with severity.

**Ref:** Banks PA, Gut 2013; Tenner S, Am J Gastroenterol 2024.

</details>

**11.** A 60-year-old woman with mild biliary pancreatitis has recovered by day 4 and is tolerating a normal diet. What is the best management of her gallbladder?

- A. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy during the same admission
- B. Cholecystectomy after 3 months
- C. No intervention unless symptoms recur
- D. Ursodeoxycholic acid long term
- E. Elective ERCP and sphincterotomy before discharge in all patients

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Same-admission cholecystectomy in mild biliary pancreatitis reduces readmission for gallstone-related complications or death (PONCHO: 5 vs 17 %).

**Why not the others:**
- B – interval cholecystectomy exposes the patient to recurrent pancreatitis, cholangitis and colic.
- C – recurrence risk is high without cholecystectomy.
- D – ursodeoxycholic acid does not prevent recurrence.
- E – ERCP is for persistent CBD stones or cholangitis; sphincterotomy alone is an option only for patients unfit for surgery.

**Ref:** da Costa DW, Lancet 2015; Tenner S, Am J Gastroenterol 2024.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Describe your approach to fluid resuscitation in a patient with predicted severe acute pancreatitis, and justify it with trial evidence.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Early hypovolaemia from capillary leak, but excess fluids cause fluid overload, IAH/ACS, ARDS.

## W20 · D3 – Acute liver failure: aetiology, King's criteria, neuroprotection, plasma exchange

**1.** A 23-year-old woman is admitted 3 days after a staggered paracetamol overdose. After 3 L of crystalloid her arterial pH is 7.24, lactate 4.2 mmol/L, INR 4.8, creatinine 190 µmol/L and she has grade II encephalopathy. Which statement is correct?

- A. She does not meet King's College Criteria because INR is < 6.5
- B. She meets King's College Criteria for emergency liver transplantation
- C. NAC is no longer indicated because more than 24 h have passed
- D. Transplant criteria cannot be applied in staggered overdoses
- E. She should first receive FFP to correct the INR before reassessment

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** In paracetamol ALF, an arterial pH < 7.30 after adequate fluid resuscitation is sufficient on its own (the lactate modification also applies: > 3.0 mmol/L after resuscitation).

**Why not the others:**
- A – the INR/creatinine/HE triad is an alternative criterion, not a requirement.
- C – NAC is given in established paracetamol ALF regardless of the time since ingestion.
- D – KCC are applied to staggered overdoses (nomograms are not).
- E – FFP obscures the INR as a prognostic marker and is not indicated without bleeding.

**Ref:** O'Grady JG, Gastroenterology 1989; EASL, J Hepatol 2017.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the classification of acute liver failure (O'Grady):

- A. Hyperacute liver failure has an interval of ≤ 7 days between jaundice and encephalopathy. — T / F
- B. Cerebral oedema is most frequent in subacute liver failure. — T / F
- C. Paracetamol typically causes hyperacute liver failure. — T / F
- D. Subacute liver failure has the best transplant-free survival. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – acute 8–28 days, subacute 29 days–12 weeks.
- B (False) – cerebral oedema is most common in hyperacute ALF.
- C (True) – also HAV and HEV.
- D (False) – subacute ALF has the worst spontaneous survival; hyperacute the best.

**Ref:** EASL, J Hepatol 2017.

</details>

- A. Hypertonic saline to a sodium of 145–150 mmol/L and early continuous renal replacement therapy
- B. Lactulose via nasogastric tube
- C. Cooling to 33 °C
- D. Prophylactic hyperventilation to PaCO₂ 3.5 kPa
- E. Dexamethasone 4 mg IV every 6 hours
