## W27 · D1 – Review: neuro

**1.** A 34-year-old with severe traumatic brain injury has an ICP of 26 mmHg for 20 minutes. He is sedated, intubated, head up 30°, PaCO₂ 38 mmHg, temperature 37.2 °C and CPP 66 mmHg. Per SIBICC, the most appropriate next step is:

- A. Cooling to 33 °C
- B. Hyperventilation to PaCO₂ 28 mmHg
- C. A bolus of hypertonic saline or mannitol
- D. Methylprednisolone 2 g IV
- E. Barbiturate coma

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** ICP > 22 mmHg persists despite tier 0–1 measures. The next step is tier 1–2: intermittent osmotherapy boluses (hypertonic saline or mannitol), CSF drainage if an EVD is in place, and mild hypocapnia to 35–38 mmHg. Barbiturates, decompressive craniectomy and mild hypothermia are reserved for tier 3.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Eurotherm showed that hypothermia for ICP control worsens outcome; 35–36 °C is at most a tier-3 option.
- B – SIBICC does not allow PaCO₂ < 30 mmHg; 32–35 mmHg is a tier-2 step only.
- D – Steroids are contraindicated in TBI (CRASH: higher mortality).
- E – Barbiturate coma is tier 3, for refractory intracranial hypertension.

**EDIC trap:** Steroids, continuous mannitol, lumbar drains and PaCO₂ < 30 mmHg are on the SIBICC "do not" list.

**Ref:** Hawryluk GWJ et al., SIBICC, Intensive Care Med 2019; Carney N et al., BTF 4th edition, Neurosurgery 2017. Review: [W15/D1](#W15-D1).

</details>

**2.** Regarding trials in traumatic brain injury:

- A. DECRA: early bifrontal decompressive craniectomy for diffuse TBI led to worse functional outcome at 6 months. — T / F
- B. RESCUEicp: late decompressive craniectomy for refractory ICP reduced mortality but increased vegetative state and severe disability. — T / F
- C. Eurotherm: hypothermia (32–35 °C) for ICP > 20 mmHg improved functional outcome. — T / F
- D. CRASH-3: tranexamic acid within 3 h reduced head-injury death mainly in mild-to-moderate TBI. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – In DECRA, early bifrontal DC lowered ICP and ICU stay but worsened GOS-E at 6 months.
- B (True) – In RESCUEicp, mortality was about halved (26.9 vs 48.9 %), at the cost of more survivors in a vegetative state or with severe disability.
- C (False) – Eurotherm was stopped early because of a worse outcome with hypothermia.
- D (True) – In CRASH-3, the benefit was seen in mild–moderate TBI (GCS 9–15) treated early; there was no benefit in severe TBI with unreactive pupils.

**Ref:** Cooper DJ et al., DECRA, N Engl J Med 2011; Hutchinson PJ et al., RESCUEicp, N Engl J Med 2016; Andrews PJD et al., Eurotherm3235, N Engl J Med 2015; CRASH-3 collaborators, Lancet 2019. Review: [W15/D1](#W15-D1).

</details>

**3.** A 48-year-old woman with aneurysmal SAH (WFNS II, aneurysm coiled on day 1) becomes confused on day 6 and has a new left pronator drift. She is on oral nimodipine, Na⁺ is 137 mmol/L and CT shows no rebleeding or hydrocephalus. The most appropriate next step is:

- A. Fluid restriction and furosemide to avoid cerebral oedema
- B. Prophylactic triple-H therapy: hypervolaemia, haemodilution and hypertension
- C. Start intravenous magnesium to a serum level of 2 mmol/L
- D. Ensure euvolaemia and try induced hypertension, with angioplasty or intra-arterial vasodilators if there is no response
- E. Stop nimodipine, because it causes hypotension

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** A new focal deficit between days 3 and 14, with rebleeding, hydrocephalus, seizures and hyponatraemia excluded, is delayed cerebral ischaemia. Management is euvolaemia plus a cautious trial of induced hypertension. Endovascular rescue (angioplasty or intra-arterial vasodilators) is used if the deficit does not respond.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Hypovolaemia worsens DCI; fluid restriction is harmful after SAH.
- B – Hypervolaemia and haemodilution (triple-H) are no longer used: they cause complications without benefit.
- C – Magnesium did not improve outcome (MASH-2).
- E – Nimodipine improves outcome. If it causes hypotension, give smaller, more frequent doses instead of stopping it.

**Ref:** Treggiari MM et al., NCS SAH guideline, Neurocrit Care 2023; Hoh BL et al., AHA/ASA SAH guideline, Stroke 2023. Review: [W15/D2](#W15-D2).

</details>

**4.** Regarding aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage:

- A. Nimodipine improves functional outcome without clearly reducing angiographic vasospasm. — T / F
- B. In ULTRA, early short-course tranexamic acid improved functional outcome. — T / F
- C. Hyponatraemia after SAH should be treated with fluid restriction. — T / F
- D. Clazosentan reduced angiographic vasospasm without improving functional outcome. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – Nimodipine 60 mg orally every 4 h for 21 days improves outcome, probably through neuroprotection rather than an effect on large-vessel spasm.
- B (False) – ULTRA showed less rebleeding but no better functional outcome with TXA.
- C (False) – Hyponatraemia after SAH is often cerebral salt wasting. Fluid restriction causes hypovolaemia and DCI; give isotonic or hypertonic saline and consider fludrocortisone.
- D (True) – CONSCIOUS and REACT showed that less vasospasm did not translate into better outcome.

**EDIC trap:** Vasospasm and DCI are not the same thing – drugs that reduce spasm do not necessarily improve outcome.

**Ref:** Post R et al., ULTRA, Lancet 2021; Macdonald RL et al., CONSCIOUS-3, Lancet Neurol 2012; Hoh BL et al., AHA/ASA SAH guideline, Stroke 2023. Review: [W15/D2](#W15-D2).

</details>

**5.** A 76-year-old on apixaban for atrial fibrillation presents 3 hours after symptom onset with a 25 mL lobar intracerebral haemorrhage; GCS is 13. His last dose was 4 hours ago. The most appropriate haemostatic treatment is:

- A. Platelet transfusion
- B. 4-factor PCC 50 IU/kg or andexanet alfa
- C. Idarucizumab 5 g IV
- D. Fresh frozen plasma 15 mL/kg
- E. Vitamin K 10 mg IV alone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Apixaban is a factor Xa inhibitor. Options are andexanet alfa (ANNEXA-I: better haemostatic efficacy, more thrombotic events; US marketing ended in 2025) or 4F-PCC 50 IU/kg where andexanet is unavailable. Treatment should be given as quickly as possible, together with SBP lowering to ~140 mmHg.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Platelets do not reverse an anticoagulant, and in antiplatelet-associated ICH they worsened outcome (PATCH).
- C – Idarucizumab reverses dabigatran only.
- D – FFP is slow, needs a large volume and does not reliably reverse FXa inhibitors.
- E – Vitamin K is used for VKA reversal and has no effect on apixaban.

**Ref:** Connolly SJ et al., ANNEXA-I, N Engl J Med 2024; Greenberg SM et al., AHA/ASA ICH guideline, Stroke 2022. Review: [W15/D3](#W15-D3).

</details>

**6.** A 62-year-old with a 20 mL deep (basal ganglia) ICH arrives 2 hours after onset with BP 214/108 mmHg and GCS 14. He is not anticoagulated. The best blood pressure strategy is:

- A. No lowering unless SBP exceeds 220 mmHg for longer than 6 hours
- B. Lower SBP to < 110 mmHg as quickly as possible
- C. Lower SBP by no more than 10 % in the first 24 hours
- D. Labetalol only if there are signs of end-organ damage outside the brain
- E. Lower SBP to ~140 mmHg within 1 hour, keeping it in 130–150 mmHg and avoiding < 130 mmHg

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** In mild–moderate ICH with SBP 150–220 mmHg, early, smooth lowering to ~140 mmHg is safe and may reduce haematoma growth. INTERACT3 showed that a care bundle (BP, glucose, temperature, anticoagulant reversal) improves functional outcome. ATACH-2 found no benefit from 110–139 mmHg and more renal adverse events, so SBP should not fall below 130 mmHg.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Untreated severe hypertension favours haematoma expansion.
- B – Very intensive lowering (ATACH-2) gave no benefit and more AKI.
- C – The "≤ 10–15 %" rule applies to hypertensive emergencies without ICH and to ischaemic stroke not receiving thrombolysis, not to ICH.
- D – Raised BP in ICH is treated for the sake of the brain itself.

**Ref:** Qureshi AI et al., ATACH-2, N Engl J Med 2016; Ma L et al., INTERACT3, Lancet 2023; Greenberg SM et al., AHA/ASA ICH guideline, Stroke 2022. Review: [W15/D3](#W15-D3).

</details>

**7.** Regarding intravenous thrombolysis and thrombectomy for acute ischaemic stroke:

- A. Tenecteplase is given as a single bolus of 0.25 mg/kg (maximum 25 mg). — T / F
- B. BP must be below 185/110 mmHg before thrombolysis. — T / F
- C. Patients with unknown onset (wake-up stroke) may be thrombolysed if MRI shows a DWI–FLAIR mismatch. — T / F
- D. After successful thrombectomy, an SBP target < 140 mmHg improves functional outcome compared with < 180 mmHg. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – TNK 0.25 mg/kg as a single bolus (max 25 mg) is at least as effective as alteplase and easier to give.
- B (True) – BP < 185/110 before thrombolysis, then < 180/105 for 24 h.
- C (True) – WAKE-UP: a DWI-positive, FLAIR-negative lesion suggests onset < 4.5 h; perfusion mismatch (EXTEND) is an alternative.

**Ref:** Thomalla G et al., WAKE-UP, N Engl J Med 2018; Nam HS et al., OPTIMAL-BP, JAMA 2023; Berge E et al., ESO IVT guideline, Eur Stroke J 2021. Review: [W15/D4](#W15-D4).

**8.** A 72-year-old woman was last seen well 14 hours ago and now has right hemiparesis and aphasia (NIHSS 18). CT angiography shows an occlusion of the left M1 segment; CT perfusion shows a 20 mL core with a large penumbra. The best management is:

- A. Mechanical thrombectomy
- B. IV alteplase alone, without thrombectomy
- C. Aspirin and admission to the stroke unit; she is outside every time window
- D. Decompressive hemicraniectomy now
- E. Dual antiplatelet therapy for 21 days

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** In large-vessel occlusion presenting 6–24 h after last known well, a clinical–imaging mismatch (small core, salvageable penumbra) selects patients for thrombectomy. DAWN (NNT ~2.8) and DEFUSE 3 showed a large functional benefit.

**Why not the others:**
- B – At 14 h with an LVO, thrombolysis alone is not standard; thrombectomy is the effective treatment.
- C – The late time window was opened by DAWN and DEFUSE 3.
- D – Hemicraniectomy is for malignant MCA infarction with space-occupying oedema, not as the first treatment.
- E – DAPT is for minor stroke or TIA (NIHSS ≤ 3), not for LVO.

**Ref:** Nogueira RG et al., DAWN, N Engl J Med 2018; Albers GW et al., DEFUSE 3, N Engl J Med 2018. Review: [W15/D4](#W15-D4).

</details>

**9.** Regarding malignant middle cerebral artery infarction, all of the following are true **EXCEPT**:

- A. Decompressive hemicraniectomy within 48 hours roughly halves mortality in patients ≤ 60 years.
- B. Clinical deterioration usually occurs within the first 2–5 days.
- C. In patients older than 60, hemicraniectomy increases the proportion who survive without disability (mRS 0–2).
- D. Osmotherapy may be used as a bridge but does not replace surgery.
- E. The patient's wishes about survival with disability must be discussed before surgery.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In DESTINY II (age > 60), hemicraniectomy improved survival, but nearly all survivors had moderately severe or severe disability (mRS 4–5). No patient reached mRS 0–2.

**Why not the others:**
- A – True: the pooled DECIMAL, DESTINY and HAMLET analysis showed an absolute mortality reduction of about 50 %.
- B – True: space-occupying oedema peaks at days 2–5.
- D – True: osmotherapy only buys time.
- E – True: the decision depends on the acceptable level of disability, so shared decision-making is essential.

**Ref:** Jüttler E et al., DESTINY II, N Engl J Med 2014; Vahedi K et al., Lancet Neurol 2007. Review: [W15/D4](#W15-D4).

</details>

**10.** Regarding convulsive status epilepticus:

- A. In ESETT, levetiracetam, fosphenytoin and valproate were similarly effective in benzodiazepine-refractory status. — T / F
- B. In RAMPART, prehospital IM midazolam was at least as effective as IV lorazepam. — T / F
- C. In HYBERNATUS, therapeutic hypothermia (32–34 °C) improved functional outcome. — T / F
- D. A second-line antiseizure drug should be given only once 30 minutes of seizure activity have passed. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – Seizure cessation was ~45–47 % in all three arms.
- B (True) – RAMPART: IM midazolam stopped seizures before arrival more often (73 vs 63 %), largely because it was given faster.
- C (False) – HYBERNATUS: hypothermia did not improve 90-day outcome, although it reduced progression to EEG-confirmed SE.
- D (False) – If seizures continue after an adequate benzodiazepine dose (~10 min), a second-line ASM is given immediately. t2 = 30 min is the point at which long-term damage becomes likely.

**Ref:** Kapur J et al., ESETT, N Engl J Med 2019; Silbergleit R et al., RAMPART, N Engl J Med 2012; Legriel S et al., HYBERNATUS, N Engl J Med 2016. Review: [W15/D5](#W15-D5).

**11.** A 30-year-old (70 kg) has had a generalised tonic–clonic seizure for 7 minutes in the emergency department. He has IV access and is oxygenated. The most appropriate first drug is:

- A. Levetiracetam 60 mg/kg IV
- B. Lorazepam 4 mg IV, repeated once if seizures continue
- C. Propofol 2 mg/kg IV and intubation
- D. Diazepam 2 mg IV
- E. Phenobarbital 20 mg/kg IV

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Benzodiazepines are first-line and must be given in full dose: lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV (usually 4 mg, repeated once), or midazolam 10 mg IM if there is no IV access. Underdosing is the most frequent error and contributes to refractoriness, which develops as GABA-A receptors are internalised.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Levetiracetam is a second-line drug given after benzodiazepines.
- C – Anaesthetic drugs are for refractory SE.
- D – 2 mg diazepam is a typical underdose; the adult dose is ~10 mg IV (0.15–0.2 mg/kg).
- E – Phenobarbital is a second-line option; it is not used first when benzodiazepines are available.

**Ref:** Glauser T et al., AES guideline, Epilepsy Curr 2016; Silbergleit R et al., RAMPART, N Engl J Med 2012. Review: [W15/D5](#W15-D5).

</details>

- A. Light sedation (RASS −2 to 0) is recommended for most ventilated adults. — T / F
- B. A CPOT score ≥ 5 is needed before pain can be diagnosed. — T / F
- C. Heart rate and blood pressure are valid stand-alone tools for pain assessment. — T / F
- D. In SPICE III, early dexmedetomidine reduced 90-day mortality. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**13.** A 68-year-old ventilated for pneumonia is calm (RASS −1) but is CAM-ICU positive on day 4. He receives midazolam boluses at night. The most appropriate management is:

- A. Look for and treat precipitating causes, stop the benzodiazepine, and use non-pharmacological measures (sleep, orientation, mobilisation)
- B. Haloperidol 2.5 mg IV three times daily until CAM-ICU becomes negative
- C. Increase sedation to RASS −3
- D. Start quetiapine to shorten the duration of delirium
- E. No action – hypoactive delirium is harmless

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** This is hypoactive delirium, the most frequent subtype. It is an independent predictor of mortality and long-term cognitive impairment. Treatment means finding the cause (sepsis, hypoxaemia, metabolic disorders, drugs), removing deliriogenic drugs (especially benzodiazepines) and using a non-pharmacological bundle.

**Why not the others:**
- B – Haloperidol did not increase days alive without delirium (AID-ICU, MIND-USA).
- C – Deeper sedation adds to the risk of delirium.
- D – Quetiapine (and other antipsychotics) have no proven effect on delirium duration; PADIS 2025 makes no recommendation.
- E – Hypoactive delirium is associated with worse outcome than hyperactive delirium.

**Ref:** Andersen-Ranberg NC et al., AID-ICU, N Engl J Med 2022; Girard TD et al., MIND-USA, N Engl J Med 2018. Review: [W16/D2](#W16-D2).

</details>

**14.** Regarding the assessment of delirium in the ICU, all of the following are true **EXCEPT**:

- A. Inattention is the cardinal feature in CAM-ICU.
- B. An ICDSC score of ≥ 4 out of 8 indicates delirium.
- C. Delirium is associated with long-term cognitive impairment (BRAIN-ICU).
- D. CAM-ICU can be validly used at RASS −4.
- E. Benzodiazepines are a modifiable risk factor.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** CAM-ICU and ICDSC can only be performed when the patient responds to voice (RASS ≥ −3). At RASS −4/−5 the patient is comatose and cannot be assessed for delirium.

**Why not the others:**
- A – True: feature 2 (inattention, SAVEAHAART with > 2 errors) is mandatory.
- B – True: the ICDSC threshold is ≥ 4.
- C – True: BRAIN-ICU linked longer delirium to worse global cognition at 3 and 12 months.
- E – True: benzodiazepines are the best-known deliriogenic drugs.

**Ref:** Ely EW et al., JAMA 2001; Pandharipande PP et al., BRAIN-ICU, N Engl J Med 2013. Review: [W16/D2](#W16-D2).

**15.** Regarding Guillain–Barré syndrome in the ICU:

- A. IVIg 2 g/kg over 5 days and plasma exchange are equally effective. — T / F
- B. Adding IV methylprednisolone to IVIg improves functional outcome. — T / F
- C. In SID-GBS, a second IVIg course in patients with poor prognosis improved outcome. — T / F
- D. Suxamethonium should be avoided because it can cause severe hyperkalaemia. — T / F

**16.** A 45-year-old with Guillain–Barré syndrome (day 5) has VC 17 mL/kg (falling from 28 mL/kg yesterday), MIP −24 cmH₂O, a weak cough and difficulty swallowing. ABG: pH 7.42, PaCO₂ 5.1 kPa, PaO₂ 11 kPa on room air. The best next step is:

- A. Wait until PaCO₂ rises before intervening
- B. NIV with high pressure support
- C. Repeat the measurements in 12 hours
- D. Start pyridostigmine
- E. Elective intubation in the ICU

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** The 20/30/40 rule (VC < 20 mL/kg, MIP weaker than −30 cmH₂O, MEP < 40 cmH₂O), a > 30 % fall in VC and bulbar dysfunction all point to impending respiratory failure. The blood gas is a late marker. Elective intubation is safer than an emergency.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Normal gases do not exclude impending failure; hypercapnia appears late.
- B – NIV is relatively contraindicated with bulbar dysfunction (aspiration) and in progressive GBS.
- C – With a rapid decline, waiting is dangerous.
- D – Pyridostigmine is for myasthenia gravis, not GBS.

**Ref:** Lawn ND et al., Arch Neurol 2001; van Doorn PA et al., EAN/PNS GBS guideline, Eur J Neurol 2023. Review: [W16/D3](#W16-D3).

**17.** A 58-year-old woman with known myasthenia gravis is treated for urosepsis with IV gentamicin. Twenty-four hours later she has ptosis, dysphagia and a VC of 15 mL/kg, and she is intubated. The most appropriate specific therapy is:

- A. Double the pyridostigmine dose
- B. High-dose methylprednisolone as monotherapy, started before any other treatment
- C. Plasma exchange or IVIg, and stop the gentamicin
- D. Edrophonium (Tensilon) test before any treatment
- E. Rituximab as first-line treatment for the crisis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** This is myasthenic crisis triggered by infection and an aminoglycoside. Rapid immunotherapy (PLEX or IVIg) is needed, together with removal of the trigger. Steroids are added later, carefully, because they can cause early worsening. Pyridostigmine is usually stopped while the patient is ventilated (secretions).

**Why not the others:**
- A – Higher cholinesterase inhibitor doses risk cholinergic crisis and secretions.
- B – Starting high-dose steroids alone can cause transient worsening (days 5–10).
- D – The edrophonium test is obsolete and dangerous (bradycardia).
- E – Rituximab is long-term therapy (MuSK-positive MG), not crisis treatment.

**Ref:** Narayanaswami P et al., International consensus guidance for MG, Neurology 2021. Review: [W16/D3](#W16-D3).

- A. Core temperature must be ≥ 36 °C. — T / F
- B. In the apnoea test, PaCO₂ must reach ≥ 60 mmHg and be ≥ 20 mmHg above baseline (US 2023 guideline). — T / F
- C. Triple-flexion responses of the legs exclude brain death. — T / F
- D. An ancillary test (e.g. CT angiography) is required in every patient. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**19.** A 40-year-old brain-dead organ donor has urine output of 450 mL/h for the last 3 hours, Na⁺ 158 mmol/L and urine osmolality 110 mOsm/kg. MAP is 70 mmHg on noradrenaline 0.1 µg/kg/min. The most appropriate treatment is:

- A. Furosemide
- B. Desmopressin, with hypotonic fluid to replace losses
- C. 3 % saline
- D. Levothyroxine as a high-dose infusion
- E. Fluid restriction

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Dilute polyuria with hypernatraemia after brain death is central diabetes insipidus (AVP deficiency). Desmopressin (or a vasopressin infusion, which also helps the circulation) reduces losses. **Why not the others:**

- C – Hypertonic saline would worsen hypernatraemia.
- D – Routine levothyroxine did not increase the number of hearts transplanted (Dhar 2023).

**21.** In a patient with TBI, the ICP waveform shows a P2 peak higher than P1. This indicates:

- A. Reduced intracranial compliance
- B. Normal compliance
- C. A malfunctioning catheter (damping)
- D. Lundberg B waves
- E. Hyperventilation-induced cerebral vasoconstriction

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Normally P1 (percussion wave, arterial pulsation) is the highest peak. When P2 (tidal wave, reflecting brain compliance) exceeds P1, the intracranial pressure–volume curve is on its steep part. Even a small increase in volume will cause a large rise in ICP.

**Why not the others:**
- B – Normal compliance gives P1 > P2 > P3.
- C – Damping flattens all peaks; it does not reverse them.
- D – B waves are rhythmic oscillations of ICP at 0.5–2 per minute, not a change in pulse waveform.
- E – Hypocapnia reduces cerebral blood volume and usually improves compliance.

**Ref:** Le Roux P et al., Consensus on multimodality monitoring, Neurocrit Care 2014. Review: [W16/D5](#W16-D5).

**22.** A 25-year-old with a complete C5 spinal cord injury after a diving accident has BP 78/40 mmHg, HR 44/min and warm extremities. FAST and CT exclude bleeding. After volume, the most appropriate haemodynamic strategy is:

**1.** A 70 kg man with septic shock had a baseline creatinine of 80 µmol/L. Two days later it is 185 µmol/L (2.3 × baseline). Urine output has been 0.25 mL/kg/h for the last 26 hours. His KDIGO AKI stage is:

- A. Stage 1, by creatinine
- B. Stage 2, by creatinine
- C. Stage 2, by urine output
- D. Stage 3, by urine output
- E. Not AKI yet, because creatinine is < 354 µmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** KDIGO stages AKI by the worse of the two criteria. Creatinine 2.0–2.9 × baseline is stage 2, but urine output < 0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥ 24 h (or anuria ≥ 12 h) is stage 3. The patient is therefore stage 3.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Stage 1 is 1.5–1.9 × baseline or a rise ≥ 26.5 µmol/L; he is above that.
- B – Creatinine alone would give stage 2, but the UO criterion is worse.
- C – Stage 2 by UO is < 0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥ 12 h; he has < 0.3 for ≥ 24 h.
- E – Creatinine ≥ 354 µmol/L is only one of the stage-3 criteria, not a condition for AKI.

**EDIC trap:** Always stage by the worst criterion – creatinine or urine output.

**Ref:** KDIGO Clinical Practice Guideline for AKI, Kidney Int Suppl 2012. Review: [W18/D1](#W18-D1).

**4.** A 66-year-old with septic shock has KDIGO stage 3 AKI. K⁺ is 5.6 mmol/L, pH 7.29, urea 28 mmol/L, PaO₂/FiO₂ 260 without signs of fluid overload. He is improving on noradrenaline. The best strategy for kidney replacement therapy is:

- A. Watchful waiting, with KRT only if an urgent indication develops
- B. Start KRT immediately (accelerated strategy) to reduce mortality
- C. Start KRT once urea reaches 20 mmol/L
- D. High-dose furosemide to avoid KRT

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**6.** A 28-year-old with severe TBI (ICP monitored, upper limit 22 mmHg) develops anuric AKI from rhabdomyolysis. The most appropriate KRT modality is:

- A. Intermittent haemodialysis with a high blood flow
- B. Peritoneal dialysis
- C. Continuous KRT (CVVH/CVVHDF)
- D. Sustained low-efficiency dialysis as the only option
- E. KRT is contraindicated with raised ICP

**Why C:** Continuous KRT removes urea slowly and avoids rapid osmotic shifts. With IHD, those shifts cause water to move into the brain (dialysis disequilibrium) and raise ICP. KDIGO suggests CRRT for patients with acute brain injury or raised ICP, and for haemodynamically unstable patients.

**Why not the others:**
- A – IHD causes fast osmotic shifts and is risky with raised ICP.
- B – PD is inefficient in hypercatabolic AKI and raises intra-abdominal pressure.
- D – SLED is a possible hybrid, but CRRT is preferred in raised ICP.
- E – KRT is not contraindicated; the modality must be chosen carefully.

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

**28.** A 71-year-old has an acute pulmonary embolism. BP is 128/76 mmHg, HR 104/min and SpO₂ 93 % on 2 L/min. CT shows an RV/LV ratio of 1.3, troponin is raised and the sPESI is 1. He has no contraindication to anticoagulation. The most appropriate management is:

- D. Inferior vena cava filter
- E. Surgical embolectomy

**C.**

**29.** A 74-year-old on warfarin (INR 4.6) has a 30 mL intracerebral haemorrhage. All of the following are appropriate **EXCEPT**:

**1.** A 28-year-old motorcyclist arrives with spurting arterial bleeding from a mid-thigh wound, gurgling breathing, GCS 10 and a deformed left femur. According to the ATLS 11th edition, the first action is:

- A. Jaw thrust and suction of the airway
- B. Rapid sequence intubation

- D. Control of the exsanguinating bleeding with a tourniquet or direct pressure
- E. Application of a femoral traction splint

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

- A – Airway comes immediately after "x", not before, in exsanguinating external bleeding.
- B – Intubating a patient who is still bleeding out risks peri-intubation arrest.
- C – Bleeding control comes before fluids, and crystalloid should be minimised (damage-control resuscitation).
- E – Splinting helps but is part of circulation/exposure, after the exsanguinating bleed is controlled.

**2.** Regarding damage-control resuscitation (European trauma guideline 2023):

- A. In bleeding trauma without brain injury, a target systolic BP of 80–90 mmHg is recommended until major bleeding is controlled. — T / F
- B. In severe TBI with haemorrhagic shock, a MAP ≥ 80 mmHg is recommended. — T / F

**3.** A 45-year-old with a pelvic fracture and ongoing bleeding reaches your hospital 4 hours after injury, transferred from a rural clinic. He has not received tranexamic acid. Regarding TXA, the best statement is:

- A. Do not start TXA now: CRASH-2 found that giving it more than 3 hours after injury increased death from bleeding
- B. Give TXA 1 g now, because benefit is independent of timing
- C. Give TXA 4 g as a single bolus to compensate for the delay
- D. Give TXA only if fibrinogen is below 1 g/L
- E. Give TXA only if ROTEM shows hyperfibrinolysis, whatever the time

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** In CRASH-2, TXA (1 g over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 h) reduced death from bleeding when given within 1 h (RR 0.68) or 1–3 h (RR 0.79). Given after 3 h, it increased death from bleeding (RR 1.44). The European guideline recommends TXA as early as possible and within 3 h, and advises against starting it later.

**Why not the others:**
- B – Benefit falls by about 10 % for every 15 minutes of delay and reverses after 3 h.
- C – No such regimen exists.
- D – The indication is based on bleeding risk and time, not on fibrinogen.
- E – Waiting for viscoelastic evidence of hyperfibrinolysis is not required within 3 h. After 3 h there is no evidence of benefit.

**4.** Regarding trials in haemorrhagic trauma:

**5.** A 34-year-old is in haemorrhagic shock after blunt trauma and has received 8 units of red cells and 6 units of plasma. He is still bleeding. ROTEM shows FIBTEM A5 6 mm with a normal EXTEM CT, and fibrinogen is 1.1 g/L. The most appropriate step is:

- A. Recombinant activated factor VII
- B. Fibrinogen concentrate (3–4 g) or cryoprecipitate
- C. Four-factor PCC 50 IU/kg
- D. More FFP only
- E. Platelet transfusion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Low FIBTEM amplitude and fibrinogen < 1.5 g/L with ongoing bleeding call for fibrinogen replacement (3–4 g of concentrate, or 15–20 units of cryoprecipitate). Fibrinogen is the first factor to fall in major haemorrhage. The CT is normal, so thrombin generation is adequate.

- A – rFVIIa is not recommended as first line; it is considered only for refractory bleeding after correcting fibrinogen, platelets, pH, temperature and calcium.

**EDIC trap:** CRYOSTAT-2 found that *empirical* early high-dose cryoprecipitate for all bleeding trauma patients did not reduce mortality – targeted replacement is still recommended.

**6.** A 22-year-old has a stab wound to the right chest. BP is 88/50 mmHg. A chest drain immediately produces 1,600 mL of blood, followed by 250 mL/h. The next step is:

**8.** A 60-year-old woman struck by a car has an open-book pelvic fracture. BP is 80/45 mmHg despite 2 units of red cells, and FAST is negative. The most appropriate immediate measure is:

- A. CT of the whole body before any intervention
- B. Laparotomy
- C. External fixation in the ICU after 24 hours
- D. Binder placed at the iliac crests
- E. Pelvic binder at the level of the greater trochanters, followed by angioembolisation or preperitoneal packing

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** A pelvic binder placed over the greater trochanters reduces pelvic volume and tamponades venous bleeding. With ongoing instability and a negative FAST, bleeding is likely retroperitoneal. The next steps are angioembolisation (arterial bleeding) or preperitoneal packing with external fixation, depending on local resources.

**Why not the others:**

**9.** An 80 kg man has a 40 % TBSA flame burn. He arrives 2 hours after the burn, having received 1,000 mL of Ringer's lactate before hospital. Using the Parkland formula (4 mL/kg/%TBSA), the infusion rate for the next 6 hours should be about:

- A. 530 mL/h
- B. 1,600 mL/h
- C. 1,067 mL/h
- D. 900 mL/h
- E. 400 mL/h

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** 24-h volume = 4 × 80 × 40 = 12,800 mL. Half of it (6,400 mL) is given in the first 8 hours from the time of the burn, not from arrival. 6,400 − 1,000 already given = 5,400 mL over the remaining 6 h = 900 mL/h. The rate is then titrated to a urine output of 0.5 mL/kg/h (~40 mL/h).

**Why not the others:**
- A – This spreads the first-half volume over 12 hours instead of the 6 hours left.
- B – This gives the whole first half in 4 hours.
- C – This ignores the 1,000 mL already given.
- E – This is the rate for the second 16 hours (6,400 mL/16 h).

**EDIC trap:** Formulas give only a starting rate. "Fluid creep" (over-resuscitation) causes compartment syndromes, so titrate to urine output and haemodynamics. Many centres start at 2–3 mL/kg/%TBSA.

**Ref:** ISBI Practice Guidelines Committee, Burns 2016; Pham TN et al., ABA consensus, J Burn Care Res 2008. Review: [W23/D4](#W23-D4).

**10.** A 50-year-old is rescued from a house fire. He has soot in the nostrils, GCS 12 and lactate 11 mmol/L. Regarding his management:

- A. Standard pulse oximetry overestimates arterial oxygen saturation in carbon monoxide poisoning. — T / F
- B. The half-life of carboxyhaemoglobin falls from about 4–5 hours on room air to about 75 minutes on 100 % oxygen. — T / F
- C. Hydroxocobalamin 5 g IV is the antidote of choice for suspected cyanide poisoning from smoke inhalation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**11.** A 30-year-old was trapped by a machine for 3 hours. His left calf is tense and painful on passive stretch. BP is 110/70 mmHg (diastolic 70 mmHg) and anterior compartment pressure is 45 mmHg. The most appropriate step is:

- A. Elevate the leg above heart level and reassess in 6 hours
- B. Urgent fasciotomy of all four compartments
- C. Mannitol infusion to reduce compartment oedema
- D. Apply a compression bandage
- E. Wait for a CK above 10,000 U/L before deciding

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Compartment perfusion (ΔP) = diastolic pressure − compartment pressure = 70 − 45 = 25 mmHg. A ΔP < 30 mmHg with clinical signs means acute compartment syndrome and urgent four-compartment fasciotomy.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Elevation lowers arterial inflow and worsens ischaemia; keep the limb at heart level.
- C – Mannitol is not a treatment for compartment syndrome.
- D – Compression raises compartment pressure further.
- E – CK reflects muscle damage; waiting for it delays treatment.

**EDIC trap:** In mass-casualty crush injury, compartment pressure measurement is often unavailable – clinical diagnosis is enough. After prolonged entrapment, prepare for reperfusion hyperkalaemia on release.

**13.** A 42-year-old on sertraline started tramadol 2 days ago for back pain. She is agitated, with temperature 38.9 °C, HR 128/min, diaphoresis, tremor, hyperreflexia and inducible ankle clonus, worse in the legs. The most likely diagnosis and first treatment are:

**18.** A 23-year-old took an unknown amount of amitriptyline. GCS is 8, BP 82/50 mmHg and HR 130/min. ECG shows QRS 150 ms and a terminal R wave in aVR. She has a generalised seizure. Besides airway management and a benzodiazepine, the most important treatment is:
