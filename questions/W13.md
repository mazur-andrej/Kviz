## W13 · D1 – Ventilation modes: VCV, PCV, PRVC, PSV, SIMV, APRV, NAVA, PAV

**1.** A patient on volume-controlled ventilation with constant inspiratory flow of 60 L/min, VT 500 mL and PEEP 5 cmH₂O has a respiratory system compliance of 25 mL/cmH₂O and an airway resistance of 10 cmH₂O/L/s. The expected peak and plateau pressures are:

- A. Ppeak 25, Pplat 20 cmH₂O
- B. Ppeak 45, Pplat 35 cmH₂O
- C. Ppeak 35, Pplat 25 cmH₂O
- D. Ppeak 35, Pplat 30 cmH₂O
- E. Ppeak 30, Pplat 25 cmH₂O

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In constant-flow VCV, Pplat = PEEP + VT/C = 5 + 500/25 = 25 cmH₂O. The resistive pressure is flow × resistance: 60 L/min = 1 L/s, so 1 × 10 = 10 cmH₂O, and Ppeak = 25 + 10 = 35 cmH₂O.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Forgets the resistive component (Ppeak = Pplat) and gives a plateau 5 cmH₂O too low.
- B – Overestimates both values; 35 cmH₂O would need a compliance of about 17 mL/cmH₂O.
- D – The peak is right but the plateau is too high; that would need a compliance of 20 mL/cmH₂O.
- E – The plateau is right but the resistive pressure is halved (as if flow were 0.5 L/s).

**EDIC trap:** Convert L/min to L/s before multiplying by resistance.

**Ref:** Hess DR. Respiratory mechanics in mechanically ventilated patients. *Respir Care* 2014;59:1773–1794.

</details>

**2.** Regarding pressure support ventilation (PSV):

- A. In a patient with COPD, increasing the cycle-off threshold (% of peak flow) shortens inspiration and can reduce delayed cycling. — T / F
- B. A backup respiratory rate is delivered only if the patient stops triggering (apnoea backup). — T / F
- C. Breaths are patient-triggered and flow-cycled. — T / F
- D. Ineffective triggering is associated with excessive levels of pressure support. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – A higher cycle-off % (e.g. 25 → 40–50 %) ends inspiration earlier. That suits COPD, where the long τ slows flow decay and causes delayed cycling.
- B (True) – PSV has no mandatory rate. The apnoea backup only fires after a set period with no triggering.
- C (True) – Breaths are patient-triggered, pressure-limited and flow-cycled. Each one ends when inspiratory flow falls to the set % of peak flow.
- D (True) – Excessive PS gives large VT and short expiration, which raises PEEPi. Efforts then fail to trigger (Thille 2006/2008).

**EDIC trap:** Ineffective efforts call for less support, not more.

**Ref:** Thille AW, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2006;32:1515–1522; Thille AW, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2008;34:1477–1486.

</details>

**3.** In the trial by Esteban et al. (NEJM 1995) comparing four weaning methods, which approach was associated with the slowest weaning?

- A. Non-invasive ventilation after early extubation
- B. Synchronised intermittent mandatory ventilation
- C. Once-daily spontaneous breathing trial
- D. Multiple daily spontaneous breathing trials
- E. Pressure support with gradual reduction

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Esteban et al. randomised 130 difficult-to-wean patients who had failed a 2-h SBT. IMV was the slowest method, with a median of 5 days. A once-daily SBT weaned patients 2.83 times faster than IMV and 2.05 times faster than PSV.

**Why not the others:**
- A – NIV after early extubation was not one of the four arms (IMV, PSV, once-daily SBT, multiple daily SBTs).
- C – The once-daily T-piece trial was the fastest, together with multiple trials.
- D – Multiple daily SBTs were as effective as a once-daily SBT and faster than IMV.
- E – Gradual PS reduction (median 4 days) was faster than IMV but slower than SBTs.

**EDIC trap:** SIMV is the slowest weaning method. Breath-by-breath switches in support stop the muscles from adapting.

**Ref:** Esteban A, et al. *N Engl J Med* 1995;332:345–350.

</details>

**4.** A patient is ventilated in pressure control with an inspiratory pressure of 15 cmH₂O above PEEP; respiratory system compliance is 40 mL/cmH₂O and inspiratory time is long enough for pressure equilibration.

- A. A fall in compliance in this mode results primarily in a fall in tidal volume. — T / F
- B. When fully passive, the expected VT is about 400 mL. — T / F
- C. The displayed airway pressure rises to 25 cmH₂O when the patient makes this effort. — T / F
- D. If the patient generates a muscle pressure of 10 cmH₂O, VT may approach 1000 mL. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – In PCV, pressure is the fixed variable, so a fall in compliance lowers VT (≈ ΔPinsp × C). Set a low-VT/low-VE alarm.
- B (False) – When passive, VT ≈ 15 × 40 = 600 mL, provided Ti allows pressure to equilibrate (≥ 3τ).
- C (False) – The ventilator keeps airway pressure at the set level (15 above PEEP). Patient effort raises flow and VT but not the displayed P_aw.
- D (True) – Transpulmonary driving pressure ≈ Pinsp + P_mus = 25 cmH₂O, so VT ≈ 25 × 40 = 1000 mL. This is hidden volutrauma.

**EDIC trap:** In pressure modes, a "safe" airway pressure does not mean safe lung stress. Watch VT.

**Ref:** Brochard L, Slutsky A, Pesenti A. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2017;195:438–442.

</details>

**5.** In pressure-regulated volume control (PRVC), a patient with high respiratory drive starts to generate large inspiratory efforts. The most likely ventilator response is:

- A. It increases the backup rate
- B. It decreases inspiratory pressure because the measured VT exceeds the target
- C. It cycles off on Edi decrease
- D. It increases inspiratory pressure to maintain target VT
- E. It switches automatically to volume control with constant flow

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** PRVC adjusts the inspiratory pressure breath by breath to reach a target VT. When strong patient effort makes the measured VT exceed the target, the algorithm lowers inspiratory pressure. As a result, the more the patient works, the less support they get, and work of breathing rises.

**Why not the others:**
- A – PRVC does not change the set backup rate in response to effort.
- C – Cycling on a fall in Edi belongs to NAVA, not PRVC.
- D – Pressure is increased only when VT falls below target (e.g. falling compliance), not when effort is high.
- E – PRVC does not switch automatically to constant-flow volume control.

**EDIC trap:** In adaptive volume-targeted modes, high drive can "wean" support inappropriately. Look for falling Pinsp with rising effort.

**Ref:** Chatburn RL, El-Khatib M, Mireles-Cabodevila E. *Respir Care* 2014;59:1747–1763.

</details>

**6.** Regarding neurally adjusted ventilatory assist (NAVA):

- A. The trial by Demoule et al. showed a higher asynchrony index with NAVA than with PSV. — T / F
- B. The diaphragmatic electrical activity is recorded with surface electrodes on the chest wall. — T / F
- C. The delivered pressure is proportional to Edi multiplied by the NAVA level. — T / F
- D. In the multicentre trial by Demoule et al., NAVA significantly increased the proportion of patients remaining in a partial ventilatory mode at 48 h. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – NAVA lowered the asynchrony index compared with PSV (14.7 vs 26.7 %).
- B (False) – Edi is recorded by electrodes on a dedicated oesophageal (nasogastric) catheter placed at the level of the crural diaphragm.
- C (True) – Pressure delivered = NAVA level (cmH₂O/µV) × Edi (µV). Assist is proportional to the neural output and is triggered and cycled by Edi.
- D (False) – The primary outcome (patients still in a partial mode at 48 h) did not differ: 67.2 vs 63.3 %, not significant.

**EDIC trap:** NAVA improves synchrony, but no RCT has shown a better outcome.

**Ref:** Demoule A, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2016;42:1723–1732.

</details>

**7.** Which statement about airway pressure release ventilation (APRV) is correct?

- A. It is recommended as first-line ventilation in all ARDS patients
- B. It is a flow-cycled, patient-triggered pressure mode without spontaneous breathing
- C. In the trial by Zhou et al., APRV significantly reduced ICU mortality
- D. CO₂ removal occurs mainly during brief releases from a high continuous pressure
- E. Its tidal volume during releases is fixed by the ventilator

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** APRV keeps a high continuous pressure (P-high) for oxygenation and recruitment. Brief releases to P-low (T-low) remove CO₂, and spontaneous breathing is allowed throughout the cycle. In Zhou 2017, APRV increased ventilator-free days (19 vs 2). ICU mortality fell from 34.3 to 19.7 %, which was not significant (p = 0.053).

**Why not the others:**
- A – No current ARDS guideline (ESICM 2023, ATS 2024) recommends APRV first-line.
- B – APRV is time-cycled between two pressure levels, and spontaneous breathing is essential to it.
- C – The mortality difference was not statistically significant.
- E – Release volume depends on compliance, resistance, P-high − P-low and T-low. It is not fixed.

**EDIC trap:** Zhou was a small single-centre trial. More ventilator-free days does not prove a mortality benefit.

**Ref:** Zhou Y, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2017;43:1648–1659.

</details>

**8.** Regarding proportional assist ventilation (PAV+):

- A. PAV+ estimates resistance and compliance automatically using short inspiratory pauses. — T / F
- B. Support decreases as patient effort increases. — T / F
- C. Runaway can occur if the delivered assistance exceeds the actual respiratory load. — T / F
- D. In the study by Xirouchaki et al., PAV+ had a higher failure rate than pressure support. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – PAV+ uses brief (~300 ms) end-inspiratory pauses to estimate compliance and resistance automatically.
- B (False) – Support is proportional to effort: the ventilator amplifies P_mus by the set gain (% assist), so support rises with effort.
- C (True) – If the calculated load is too high (e.g. resistance overestimated or a leak), assistance can outlast effort ("runaway").
- D (False) – PAV+ failed less often than PSV (11.1 vs 22.0 %) and had fewer asynchronies.

**EDIC trap:** In PAV and NAVA the patient controls the pattern. In PSV the clinician sets the pressure.

**Ref:** Xirouchaki N, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2008;34:2026–2034.

</details>

**9.** On volume-controlled ventilation with constant flow, the pressure–time curve of a patient with strong inspiratory effort shows a concave "scooped" inspiratory limb. The most likely problem and solution are:

- A. Endotracheal tube obstruction – suction
- B. Pneumothorax – chest drain
- C. Delayed cycling – increase cycle-off threshold
- D. Auto-triggering – make trigger less sensitive
- E. Flow starvation – increase inspiratory flow or switch to a pressure-targeted mode

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** With constant-flow VCV, the ventilator delivers a fixed flow whatever the patient wants. When effort is strong, P_mus pulls airway pressure down during inspiration, so the pressure trace becomes concave ("scooped"). Treat it by raising peak flow, using a decelerating ramp, or switching to a pressure-targeted mode that lets flow follow demand.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Tube obstruction raises Ppeak, with a wider Ppeak–Pplat gap. It does not scoop the curve.
- B – Pneumothorax raises Ppeak and Pplat (lower compliance).
- C – Delayed cycling shows as an end-inspiratory pressure spike in PSV.
- D – Auto-triggering gives breaths with no patient effort, not a concave curve.

**EDIC trap:** "Scooped" pressure means flow starvation. "End-inspiratory spike" means delayed cycling.

**Ref:** Georgopoulos D, Prinianakis G, Kondili E. *Intensive Care Med* 2006;32:34–47.

</details>

**10.** Regarding classification of ventilator breaths:

- A. In volume control, airway pressure depends on respiratory system compliance and resistance. — T / F
- B. A breath that is time-triggered is a mandatory breath. — T / F
- C. In PSV, the breath ends when a set inspiratory time has elapsed. — T / F
- D. A ventilator can control either volume/flow or pressure during a given inspiration, but not both simultaneously. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – In VCV, flow and volume are fixed, so P_aw = PEEP + VT/C + flow × R depends on the patient's mechanics.
- B (True) – A breath started by the ventilator's timer is mandatory, by Chatburn's taxonomy.
- C (False) – PSV is flow-cycled: the breath ends when flow falls to the set % of peak. Time-cycling is only a backup.
- D (True) – Equation of motion: during one inspiration the ventilator can control only volume/flow or pressure, never both.

**EDIC trap:** Trigger (what starts the breath), target (what limits it) and cycle (what ends it) are separate variables.

**Ref:** Chatburn RL, El-Khatib M, Mireles-Cabodevila E. *Respir Care* 2014;59:1747–1763.

</details>

**11.** A patient with ARDS is on APRV (P-high 28 cmH₂O, T-high 5 s, P-low 0 cmH₂O, T-low 0.8 s). On the flow–time curve the expiratory flow during each release peaks at 60 L/min and decays to 20 L/min before pressure returns to P-high. According to the approach described in BJA Education (2020), the most appropriate adjustment is:

- A. Add pressure support of 10 cmH₂O to spontaneous breaths
- B. Shorten T-low (in 0.1-s steps) until the release ends at about 45 L/min
- C. Lengthen T-low to 1.2 s so that expiratory flow reaches zero
- D. Shorten T-high to 2 s to increase the number of releases
- E. Increase P-low to 10 cmH₂O and leave T-low unchanged

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** In APRV, T-low is set so that expiratory flow is stopped at about 75 % of peak expiratory flow (0.75 × 60 = 45 L/min). This keeps some intended auto-PEEP and avoids derecruitment. Flow falling to 20 L/min (33 % of peak) means T-low is too long, so shorten it in 0.1-s steps.

**Why not the others:**
- A – PS added to spontaneous breaths at P-high can overdistend recruited lung (high transpulmonary pressure).
- C – Letting expiratory flow reach zero removes end-expiratory "splinting" and promotes cyclic collapse.
- D – A shorter T-high increases releases (and MV) but does not fix the end-release derecruitment.
- E – APRV keeps P-low at 0 and controls end-expiratory volume through T-low.

**EDIC trap:** In APRV, end-expiratory lung volume is set by time (T-low), not by P-low.

**Ref:** Swindin J, Sampson C, Howatson A. *BJA Educ* 2020;20:80–88.

</details>

**12.** Regarding airway pressure release ventilation (APRV):

- A. Severe bronchospasm is a relative contraindication because a short T-low cannot adequately empty slow lung units. — T / F
- B. Spontaneous inspiratory efforts taken at P-high may generate high transpulmonary pressures, particularly in heterogeneous lungs. — T / F
- C. APRV is recommended in preference to prone positioning for severe ARDS. — T / F
- D. In the trial by Zhou et al. (2017), patients on APRV were less often successfully extubated than those on low tidal volume ventilation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – Slow units (long τ) cannot empty during a short T-low. That causes gas trapping, hypercapnia and haemodynamic compromise.
- B (True) – Spontaneous breaths at P-high add negative pleural pressure to a high airway pressure. Transpulmonary pressure can become injurious, especially in heterogeneous lungs.
- C (False) – Prone positioning has mortality benefit (PROSEVA) and should come first. APRV is a rescue option when proning is contraindicated or fails.
- D (False) – APRV patients were extubated successfully more often (66.2 vs 38.8 %).

**EDIC trap:** APRV is not evidence-based first-line therapy for ARDS. Current ARDS guidelines do not recommend it routinely.

**Ref:** Zhou Y, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2017;43:1648–1659; Swindin J, et al. *BJA Educ* 2020;20:80–88.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Compare the evidence for SIMV, PSV and a daily spontaneous breathing trial as weaning strategies, and explain the physiological reason SIMV performs poorly.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Esteban (NEJM 1995): 546 patients, 130 randomised after failing a 2-h SBT.

## W13 · D2 – Waveforms, loops and respiratory mechanics: compliance, resistance, τ, pauses, oesophageal pressure

**1.** A sedated, paralysed patient on volume control (constant flow 60 L/min, VT 450 mL, PEEP 8 cmH₂O, total PEEP 8 cmH₂O) has Ppeak 38 cmH₂O and Pplat 26 cmH₂O. Which pair of values is correct?

- A. Static compliance 25 mL/cmH₂O; airway resistance 12 cmH₂O/L/s
- B. Static compliance 17 mL/cmH₂O; airway resistance 12 cmH₂O/L/s
- C. Static compliance 25 mL/cmH₂O; airway resistance 0.2 cmH₂O/L/s
- D. Static compliance 15 mL/cmH₂O; airway resistance 26 cmH₂O/L/s
- E. Static compliance 30 mL/cmH₂O; airway resistance 6 cmH₂O/L/s

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Static compliance uses the plateau pressure: C_stat = VT/(Pplat − total PEEP) = 450/(26 − 8) = 25 mL/cmH₂O. Resistance is (Ppeak − Pplat)/flow. With 60 L/min = 1 L/s, that gives 12/1 = 12 cmH₂O/L/s.

**Why not the others:**
- B – 17 mL/cmH₂O comes from dividing VT by Pplat alone (450/26), ignoring PEEP.
- C – 0.2 comes from failing to convert flow to L/s (12/60).
- D – 15 mL/cmH₂O is dynamic compliance, 450/(38 − 8). Resistance 26 is the plateau pressure, not a resistance.
- E – Neither value fits the measured pressures.

**EDIC trap:** Use total PEEP (set + intrinsic) in the denominator. Here they are equal.

**Ref:** Hess DR. *Respir Care* 2014;59:1773–1794.

</details>

**2.** A ventilated patient's peak pressure rises suddenly from 28 to 42 cmH₂O while the plateau pressure stays at 22 cmH₂O. Possible causes include:

- A. Tension pneumothorax — T / F
- B. Endobronchial (right main bronchus) intubation — T / F
- C. Kinked or bitten endotracheal tube — T / F
- D. Abdominal distension — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – Tension pneumothorax lowers respiratory system compliance and raises both Ppeak and Pplat.
- B (False) – Endobronchial intubation ventilates one lung, so compliance falls and Pplat rises.
- C (True) – A kinked or bitten tube adds resistance. Only the resistive part (Ppeak − Pplat) widens, and Pplat stays unchanged. Secretions and bronchospasm behave the same way.
- D (False) – Abdominal distension stiffens the chest wall, lowering compliance and raising Pplat.

**EDIC trap:** Ppeak up with Pplat unchanged points to resistance. Ppeak and Pplat up together point to compliance.

**Ref:** Hess DR. *Respir Care* 2014;59:1773–1794.

</details>

**3.** A patient has airway resistance of 20 cmH₂O/L/s and respiratory system compliance of 50 mL/cmH₂O. Approximately how long an expiratory time is needed to exhale about 95 % of the tidal volume?

- A. 0.3 s
- B. 3.0 s
- C. 2.0 s
- D. 5.0 s
- E. 1.0 s

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** τ = R × C = 20 cmH₂O/L/s × 0.05 L/cmH₂O = 1.0 s. Passive exhalation is exponential: about 63 % of VT is exhaled after 1τ, 86 % after 2τ, 95 % after 3τ and 98–99 % after 5τ. So about 3 s is needed for 95 %.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 0.3 s is about 0.3τ, so only about 26 % would be exhaled.
- C – 2.0 s = 2τ gives about 86 %.
- D – 5.0 s = 5τ gives about 99 %, more than asked.
- E – 1.0 s is one time constant, about 63 %.

**EDIC trap:** Convert compliance to L/cmH₂O (50 mL = 0.05 L) before multiplying.

**Ref:** Hess DR. *Respir Care* 2014;59:1773–1794.

</details>

**4.** Regarding ventilator waveforms:

- A. A "scooped-out" expiratory limb of the flow–volume loop suggests restrictive lung disease. — T / F
- B. Saw-tooth oscillations on the flow trace may indicate secretions or water in the circuit. — T / F
- C. Expiratory flow that returns to zero before the next breath suggests dynamic hyperinflation. — T / F
- D. A volume–time curve whose expiratory limb does not return to baseline suggests a leak. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – A concave, "scooped-out" expiratory limb of the flow–volume loop is typical of airflow obstruction (asthma, COPD), not restriction.
- B (True) – Saw-tooth oscillations on flow and pressure tracings suggest secretions or condensate (water) in the circuit, and call for suction or draining.
- C (False) – Expiratory flow that reaches zero before the next breath means complete emptying. Dynamic hyperinflation shows as flow that has not returned to zero.
- D (True) – When the expiratory volume does not return to baseline, less volume came back than went in. That is a leak (cuff, circuit, bronchopleural fistula).

**EDIC trap:** Expiratory flow not reaching zero points to auto-PEEP. Volume not reaching zero points to a leak.

**Ref:** Georgopoulos D, Prinianakis G, Kondili E. *Intensive Care Med* 2006;32:34–47.

</details>

**5.** Dynamic compliance is lower than static compliance in a patient because:

- A. Dynamic compliance uses plateau pressure in the denominator
- B. Dynamic compliance includes the pressure needed to overcome airway resistance
- C. Static compliance is measured during spontaneous breathing
- D. Dynamic compliance excludes PEEP
- E. Static compliance includes the chest wall but dynamic compliance does not

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Dynamic compliance = VT/(Ppeak − PEEP). Ppeak includes the pressure needed to drive flow through the airways and tube (flow × R). The denominator is therefore larger and C_dyn is lower than C_stat. The gap widens as resistance increases.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Plateau pressure is used for static, not dynamic, compliance.
- C – Static compliance needs a passive patient with an end-inspiratory hold. Effort makes it invalid.
- D – Both calculations subtract PEEP (ideally total PEEP).
- E – Both values describe the whole respiratory system (lung + chest wall) unless oesophageal pressure is measured.

**EDIC trap:** A falling C_dyn with a stable C_stat means a resistance problem, not stiffer lungs.

**Ref:** Hess DR. *Respir Care* 2014;59:1773–1794.

</details>

**6.** Regarding oesophageal pressure measurement:

- A. The balloon should be positioned in the lower third of the oesophagus. — T / F
- B. During an occlusion test, a ratio ΔPes/ΔPaw close to 1 confirms adequate positioning. — T / F
- C. In EPVent-2, oesophageal pressure–guided PEEP reduced 28-day mortality. — T / F
- D. Transpulmonary pressure equals oesophageal pressure minus airway pressure. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – The balloon sits in the lower third of the oesophagus (about 35–40 cm from the nostril), where it best reflects pleural pressure next to the lung.
- B (True) – The occlusion (Baydur) test checks position: during an occluded effort, ΔPes/ΔPaw should be about 0.8–1.2.
- C (False) – EPVent-2 (200 patients) found no difference in the composite of death and ventilator-free days. 28-day mortality was 32.4 vs 30.6 %.
- D (False) – Transpulmonary pressure is P_L = P_aw − P_es (airway minus pleural), not the reverse.

**EDIC trap:** P_es monitoring is physiologically useful but did not improve outcomes, so it is not routine.

**Ref:** Beitler JR, et al. *JAMA* 2019;321:846–857; Akoumianaki E, et al. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2014;189:520–531.

</details>

**7.** A patient with BMI 48 is ventilated with PEEP 10 cmH₂O. End-expiratory oesophageal pressure is 16 cmH₂O. The end-expiratory transpulmonary pressure and its implication are:

- A. +6 cmH₂O – adequate; no change
- B. +26 cmH₂O – overdistension; reduce PEEP
- C. −16 cmH₂O – pneumothorax likely
- D. −6 cmH₂O – dependent lung regions are likely collapsing; consider increasing PEEP
- E. 0 cmH₂O – oesophageal balloon is misplaced

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** End-expiratory transpulmonary pressure = PEEP − P_es,ee = 10 − 16 = −6 cmH₂O. A negative P_L,ee means pleural pressure exceeds alveolar pressure at end-expiration. Dependent lung is then likely to collapse, which is common in obesity with a heavy chest wall. Raising PEEP to make P_L,ee roughly 0 to +2 cmH₂O can be considered.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Swaps the sign (P_es − PEEP).
- B – Adds the pressures instead of subtracting them.
- C – Uses P_es alone. A negative P_L does not mean pneumothorax.
- E – 0 would need P_es = PEEP. A high P_es in obesity is expected and does not imply misplacement.

**EDIC trap:** Obesity raises pleural pressure. Normal PEEP may not keep dependent alveoli open.

**Ref:** Talmor D, et al. *N Engl J Med* 2008;359:2095–2104.

</details>

**8.** VT 500 mL, Pplat 30 cmH₂O, PEEP 10 cmH₂O; tidal swing in oesophageal pressure is 5 cmH₂O.

- A. Respiratory system elastance is 60 cmH₂O/L. — T / F
- B. Chest wall elastance is 20 cmH₂O/L. — T / F

**1.** A paralysed patient on volume control (constant flow 30 L/min, VT 400 mL, PEEP 10 cmH₂O, no intrinsic PEEP) has Ppeak 34 cmH₂O and Pplat 30 cmH₂O. Which is correct?

- A. Static compliance 20 mL/cmH₂O; resistance 4 cmH₂O/L/s; driving pressure 20 cmH₂O
- B. Static compliance 13.3 mL/cmH₂O; resistance 8 cmH₂O/L/s; driving pressure 30 cmH₂O
- C. Static compliance 20 mL/cmH₂O; resistance 8 cmH₂O/L/s; driving pressure 20 cmH₂O
- D. Static compliance 16.7 mL/cmH₂O; resistance 4 cmH₂O/L/s; driving pressure 24 cmH₂O
- E. Static compliance 40 mL/cmH₂O; resistance 0.13 cmH₂O/L/s; driving pressure 10 cmH₂O

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Driving pressure ΔP = Pplat − PEEP = 30 − 10 = 20 cmH₂O, so C_stat = 400/20 = 20 mL/cmH₂O. Flow 30 L/min = 0.5 L/s, so R = (Ppeak − Pplat)/flow = 4/0.5 = 8 cmH₂O/L/s.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Resistance takes flow as 1 L/s (4/1) instead of 0.5 L/s.
- B – Compliance is divided by Pplat alone (400/30), and ΔP is taken as 30.
- D – Uses Ppeak − PEEP (24), which gives dynamic compliance, and the wrong resistance.
- E – Flow is not converted (4/30), and the compliance and ΔP are off by a factor of 2.

**EDIC trap:** Always convert L/min to L/s, and use Pplat (not Ppeak) for static compliance.

**Ref:** Hess DR. *Respir Care* 2014;59:1773–1794.

</details>

**2.** Regarding ventilation modes:

- A. In PSV, breaths are patient-triggered, pressure-targeted and time-cycled. — T / F
- B. In pressure control, a patient's own inspiratory effort can substantially increase tidal volume without changing the displayed airway pressure. — T / F
- C. In PRVC, strong patient effort can lead the ventilator to reduce inspiratory pressure. — T / F
- D. SIMV was the fastest weaning method in the trial by Esteban et al. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – PSV is patient-triggered, pressure-targeted and flow-cycled at a set % of peak inspiratory flow, not time-cycled.
- B (True) – In PCV the ventilator holds airway pressure constant, so VT ≈ (Pinsp + P_mus) × C. Effort can greatly increase VT and lung stress without changing displayed pressure.
- C (True) – PRVC lowers inspiratory pressure when the measured VT exceeds the target. With strong effort, support falls as the patient works harder.
- D (False) – In Esteban 1995, SIMV was the slowest method. A once-daily SBT was 2.83 times faster.

**EDIC trap:** In pressure-targeted modes, monitor VT, not just pressure.

**Ref:** Esteban A, et al. *N Engl J Med* 1995;332:345–350; Brochard L, et al. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2017;195:438–442.

</details>

**3.** A patient on PSV has a resistance of 30 cmH₂O/L/s and compliance of 60 mL/cmH₂O. The time constant is:

- A. 2.0 s
- B. 1.8 s
- C. 18 s
- D. 0.18 s
- E. 0.5 s

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** τ = R × C = 30 cmH₂O/L/s × 0.06 L/cmH₂O = 1.8 s. About 95 % of VT is exhaled after 3τ (5.4 s). That is a long Te, typical of obstructive disease, with a high risk of PEEPi at normal rates.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 2.0 s matches no R × C product here.
- C – 18 s means compliance was taken as 0.6 L/cmH₂O (a units error).
- D – 0.18 s means compliance was taken as 0.006 L/cmH₂O.
- E – 0.5 s has no basis in these values.

**EDIC trap:** Convert mL/cmH₂O to L/cmH₂O before multiplying. τ comes out in seconds.

**Ref:** Hess DR. *Respir Care* 2014;59:1773–1794.

</details>

**4.** Concerning waveform interpretation:

- A. A pressure spike at the end of inspiration in PSV suggests premature cycling. — T / F
- B. The expiratory limb of the volume–time curve not returning to zero suggests a leak. — T / F
- C. A rise in Ppeak with a parallel rise in Pplat suggests increased airway resistance. — T / F
- D. A concave ("scooped") pressure–time curve during volume control inspiration indicates excessive inspiratory flow. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – An end-inspiratory pressure spike in PSV means delayed cycling: the patient exhales actively while the ventilator is still inflating. Premature cycling shows as an expiratory "bump" or double triggering.
- B (True) – An expiratory volume limb that does not return to zero means less gas came back than went in, which is a leak.
- C (False) – Ppeak and Pplat rising together point to reduced compliance (e.g. pneumothorax, intubation of one lung). Higher resistance widens only the Ppeak–Pplat gap.
- D (False) – A scooped pressure–time curve in VCV means flow starvation: set flow is too low for the patient's demand, not too high.

**EDIC trap:** In waveform questions, decide first whether the problem is in the trigger, flow or cycling phase.

**Ref:** Georgopoulos D, Prinianakis G, Kondili E. *Intensive Care Med* 2006;32:34–47.

</details>

**5.** Using the formula MP = 0.098 × RR × VT(L) × (Ppeak − ½ΔP), calculate mechanical power for VT 0.45 L, RR 20/min, Ppeak 28 cmH₂O, Pplat 22 cmH₂O, PEEP 8 cmH₂O:

- A. 30.9 J/min
- B. 12.3 J/min
- C. 24.7 J/min
- D. 9.3 J/min
- E. 18.5 J/min

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** ΔP = Pplat − PEEP = 22 − 8 = 14 cmH₂O, so Ppeak − ½ΔP = 28 − 7 = 21 cmH₂O. MP = 0.098 × 20 × 0.45 × 21 = 0.882 × 21 ≈ 18.5 J/min, just above the observational ~17 J/min threshold.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Uses a pressure term of about 35 (Ppeak + ½ΔP).
- B – Works out to a pressure term of about 14, i.e. ΔP alone.
- C – Uses Ppeak (28) without subtracting ½ΔP.
- D – Half the correct value (VT or RR halved).

**EDIC trap:** For VCV the formula uses Ppeak − ½ΔP. Do not plug in Pplat or Ppeak alone.

**Ref:** Gattinoni L, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2016;42:1567–1575.

</details>

**6.** Regarding patient–ventilator asynchrony:

- A. The asynchrony index is calculated as asynchronous events divided by the sum of ventilator cycles and ineffective efforts. — T / F
- B. An index above 10 % is considered severe. — T / F
- C. In Blanch et al. (ICM 2015), a high asynchrony index was associated with higher mortality. — T / F
- D. Ineffective efforts are best treated by increasing pressure support. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – AI = asynchronous events / (ventilator cycles + ineffective efforts) × 100.
- B (True) – Thille 2006 defined an AI above 10 % as severe asynchrony. It occurred in 24 % of patients.
- C (True) – Blanch 2015 (about 8.7 million breaths) linked an AI above 10 % to higher ICU and hospital mortality, though only as an association.
- D (False) – Ineffective efforts come mainly from over-assistance (large VT, PEEPi), so reduce PS/VT. External PEEP can help in COPD.

**EDIC trap:** The reflex "the patient is struggling, give more support" is wrong for ineffective efforts.

**Ref:** Thille AW, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2006;32:1515–1522; Blanch L, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2015;41:633–641.

</details>

**7.** A patient on PSV 8 cmH₂O, PEEP 5 cmH₂O (Ppeak 13 cmH₂O) has ΔPocc −21 cmH₂O. Which estimates are correct (Pmus ≈ −¾ × ΔPocc; ΔPL,dyn ≈ ΔPaw − ⅔ × ΔPocc)?

- A. Pmus ≈ 5 cmH₂O; ΔPL,dyn ≈ 8 cmH₂O
- B. Pmus ≈ 16 cmH₂O; ΔPL,dyn ≈ 22 cmH₂O
- C. Pmus ≈ 16 cmH₂O; ΔPL,dyn ≈ −6 cmH₂O
- D. Pmus ≈ 14 cmH₂O; ΔPL,dyn ≈ 13 cmH₂O
- E. Pmus ≈ 21 cmH₂O; ΔPL,dyn ≈ 29 cmH₂O

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** P_mus ≈ −¾ × ΔPocc = 0.75 × 21 ≈ 16 cmH₂O. ΔP_aw = 13 − 5 = 8, so ΔP_L,dyn ≈ 8 − ⅔ × (−21) = 8 + 14 = 22 cmH₂O. Both effort (P_mus above 10–15) and lung stress (ΔP_L,dyn above 15–20) are excessive. Consider more support or sedation, and treat the cause of high drive.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Ignores ΔPocc, using only the support values.
- C – Subtracts the effort term (8 − 14) instead of adding it.
- D – Uses ⅔ for P_mus and adds a wrong term for ΔP_L,dyn.
- E – Takes P_mus = |ΔPocc| and ΔP_L,dyn = ΔP_aw + |ΔPocc| without the coefficients.

**EDIC trap:** P_mus uses ¾. ΔP_L,dyn uses ⅔.

**Ref:** Bertoni M, et al. *Crit Care* 2019;23:346.

</details>

**8.** Regarding transpulmonary pressure:

- A. It is calculated as airway pressure plus oesophageal pressure. — T / F
- B. A positive end-expiratory transpulmonary pressure suggests alveolar collapse in dependent regions. — T / F
- C. Talmor et al. (NEJM 2008) found no improvement in oxygenation with oesophageal pressure–guided PEEP. — T / F
- D. Respiratory system elastance equals lung elastance minus chest wall elastance. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – Transpulmonary pressure is airway minus oesophageal (pleural) pressure: P_L = P_aw − P_es.
- B (False) – A negative end-expiratory P_L (pleural above alveolar pressure) suggests dependent collapse. A positive value means alveoli are held open.
- C (False) – Talmor (EPVent, 61 patients) improved oxygenation, with P/F 88 mmHg higher at 72 h, and compliance. It was not powered for mortality.
- D (False) – Elastances in series add: E_rs = E_L + E_cw. So E_L = E_rs − E_cw.

**EDIC trap:** In this question all four statements are false. "All false" is a valid K′ pattern.

**Ref:** Talmor D, et al. *N Engl J Med* 2008;359:2095–2104.

</details>

**9.** Which mechanism is NOT part of patient self-inflicted lung injury (P-SILI)?

- A. Increased pulmonary vascular transmural pressure from negative intrathoracic pressure
- B. Injury from excessive set PEEP in a paralysed patient
- C. Aggravation of injury in already damaged lungs ("two-hit")
- D. Pendelluft between lung regions
- E. Large swings in transpulmonary pressure from vigorous inspiratory effort

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** P-SILI is lung injury from the patient's own vigorous effort, and it needs spontaneous inspiratory activity. A paralysed patient makes no effort. Injury from excessive set PEEP is classic VILI (volutrauma/overdistension), not P-SILI.

**Why not the others:**
- A – Negative pleural swings raise pulmonary vascular transmural pressure and promote oedema, a P-SILI mechanism.
- C – Strong effort worsens injury in lungs that are already damaged ("two-hit"), which is part of the concept.
- D – Pendelluft (gas shifting to dependent regions) causes occult local overstretch.
- E – Large swings in transpulmonary pressure cause high global stress.

**EDIC trap:** The question is negatively phrased ("NOT"). Pick the option that involves no patient effort.

**Ref:** Brochard L, Slutsky A, Pesenti A. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2017;195:438–442.

</details>

**10.** A ventilated patient with severe asthma:

- A. Plateau pressure is a better indicator of alveolar pressure than peak pressure. — T / F
- B. Should be ventilated with a low respiratory rate and high inspiratory flow to prolong expiration. — T / F
- C. External PEEP is absolutely contraindicated. — T / F
- D. Should have the respiratory rate increased to normalise PaCO₂. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – Ppeak mostly reflects the high airway resistance. Pplat (end-inspiratory hold) better reflects alveolar pressure and hyperinflation.
- B (True) – A low RR (and MV) with high inspiratory flow shortens Ti and lengthens Te. This is the key to reducing dynamic hyperinflation.
- C (False) – External PEEP is not absolutely contraindicated. A cautious trial guided by Pplat/PEEP_tot can be considered, because the response is unpredictable (Caramez 2005).
- D (False) – Raising RR to normalise PaCO₂ shortens Te and worsens hyperinflation. Permissive hypercapnia is the standard approach.

**EDIC trap:** In severe asthma, aim for Pplat below 30 (ideally below 25) cmH₂O, not a normal PaCO₂.

**Ref:** Tuxen DV, Lane S. *Am Rev Respir Dis* 1987;136:872–879; Caramez MP, et al. *Crit Care Med* 2005;33:1519–1528.

</details>

**11.** In Thille et al. (ICM 2008), reducing pressure support in patients with frequent ineffective triggering:

- A. Had no effect on tidal volume
- B. Increased ineffective triggering
- C. Reduced ineffective triggering, often to zero, with a fall in tidal volume to about 6 mL/kg PBW
- D. Required switching to NAVA
- E. Caused severe hypercapnic acidosis in most patients

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Thille 2008 lowered PS (mean 20 → 13 cmH₂O) in patients with frequent ineffective triggering. Ineffective triggering fell from 45 % to 0 %, and VT fell from 10.2 to 5.9 mL/kg PBW. Respiratory effort per breath did not rise significantly, and breathing became more efficient.

**Why not the others:**
- A – VT fell substantially.
- B – Ineffective triggering decreased, often to zero.
- D – No mode change was needed; the PS adjustment alone worked.
- E – There was no clinically relevant hypercapnic acidosis.

**EDIC trap:** Large VT on PSV in COPD points to over-assistance and ineffective efforts.

**Ref:** Thille AW, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2008;34:1477–1486.

**12.** Regarding P0.1 and respiratory drive:

- A. Ventilator estimates of P0.1 without true occlusion may overestimate the reference value. — T / F
- B. A P0.1 of 0.5 cmH₂O may indicate over-assistance or oversedation. — T / F
- C. P0.1 is measured 100 ms after the start of an occluded inspiratory effort. — T / F
- D. A P0.1 of 5 cmH₂O suggests potentially excessive inspiratory effort. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – Ventilator estimates of P0.1 without a true occlusion may underestimate the reference value, not overestimate it.
- B (True) – A P0.1 of 1.0 cmH₂O or less (here 0.5) suggests low drive or effort, from over-assistance or oversedation.
- C (True) – P0.1 is the airway pressure drop 100 ms after the start of an occluded inspiratory effort.
- D (True) – A P0.1 above 3.5 cmH₂O detects potentially excessive effort (Telias 2020).

**EDIC trap:** Low P0.1 (≤ 1) suggests over-assistance. High P0.1 (> 3.5) suggests high drive and possible P-SILI.

**Ref:** Telias I, et al. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2020;201:1086–1098.

**13.** Which trial compared early APRV with low tidal volume ventilation in ARDS and found more ventilator-free days with APRV?

- A. ARMA
- B. Xirouchaki et al., ICM 2008
- C. Zhou et al., ICM 2017
- D. Demoule et al., ICM 2016
- E. EPVent-2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Zhou et al. randomised 138 ARDS patients to early APRV or low-VT ventilation. APRV gave more ventilator-free days at day 28 (median 19 vs 2). ICU mortality was 19.7 vs 34.3 %, which was not significant.

**Why not the others:**
- A – ARMA (ARDSNet 2000) compared 6 vs 12 mL/kg VT.
- B – Xirouchaki compared PAV+ with pressure support.
- D – Demoule compared NAVA with pressure support.
- E – EPVent-2 compared oesophageal pressure–guided PEEP with an empirical high PEEP/FiO₂ table.

**EDIC trap:** Match each trial to its intervention. EDIC often tests trial acronyms.

**Ref:** Zhou Y, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2017;43:1648–1659.

**14.** Regarding auto-PEEP:

- A. Pepe and Marini described it as "occult" because it is not seen on the manometer during normal ventilator operation. — T / F
- B. It increases the work required to trigger the ventilator. — T / F
- C. Using set PEEP instead of total PEEP to calculate compliance underestimates compliance. — T / F
- D. It is detected by an end-expiratory occlusion manoeuvre. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – Pepe & Marini named auto-PEEP "occult" because the expiratory valve is open to the set PEEP, so the manometer does not show it.
- B (True) – The patient must first overcome PEEPi before the trigger is reached, which increases triggering work and ineffective efforts.
- C (True) – Using set PEEP instead of total PEEP inflates the denominator (Pplat − PEEP), so compliance is underestimated.
- D (True) – An end-expiratory occlusion (hold) in a passive patient reveals total PEEP. PEEPi = PEEP_tot − set PEEP.

**EDIC trap:** Unexplained hypotension, ineffective efforts or "low compliance" in COPD and asthma all suggest checking for PEEPi.

**Ref:** Pepe PE, Marini JJ. *Am Rev Respir Dis* 1982;126:166–170.

</details>

**15.** In the study by Serpa Neto et al. (ICM 2018), mechanical power:

- A. Showed no relation with mortality
- B. Was associated with in-hospital mortality per 5 J/min increase, with risk rising above about 17 J/min
- C. Was relevant only when tidal volume exceeded 10 mL/kg PBW
- D. Was a randomised intervention target
- E. Was calculated only in patients on ECMO

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** In MIMIC-III and eICU (more than 8,000 patients), each 5 J/min increase in mechanical power raised the odds of in-hospital death (OR ≈ 1.06). Risk rose consistently above about 17 J/min, and the association held even with low VT and low ΔP.

**Why not the others:**
- A – A clear association was found.
- C – The association was present even with low (protective) VT.
- D – This was an observational analysis, and MP has not been tested as a randomised target.
- E – It was calculated in general ventilated ICU patients, not ECMO patients.

**EDIC trap:** MP adds information beyond VT and ΔP, but it remains an observational marker.

**Ref:** Serpa Neto A, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2018;44:1914–1922.

</details>
