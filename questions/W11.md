## W11 · D1 – VV-ECMO in ARDS: CESAR, EOLIA and the Bayesian reanalysis

**1.** Which of the following was an inclusion criterion of the EOLIA trial (despite optimised ventilation)?

- A. PaO₂/FiO₂ < 80 mmHg for > 6 hours
- B. Murray lung injury score ≥ 2.5
- C. Mechanical ventilation for more than 7 days
- D. PaO₂/FiO₂ < 150 mmHg for > 12 hours
- E. pH < 7.35 with PaCO₂ > 50 mmHg for > 1 hour

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** EOLIA criteria: P/F < 50 for > 3 h, P/F < 80 for > 6 h, or pH < 7.25 with PaCO₂ ≥ 60 for > 6 h. MV > 7 days was an exclusion criterion; the Murray score belongs to CESAR (≥ 3).

</details>

**2.** Regarding the CESAR trial:

- A. Patients were randomised to referral to an ECMO centre or to conventional management. — T / F
- B. All patients in the intervention arm received ECMO. — T / F
- C. The control arm followed a mandatory lung-protective ventilation protocol. — T / F
- D. The primary outcome was 28-day mortality. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.** B: only ~75 % (68/90) received ECMO. C: no protocol; lung-protective ventilation 70 vs 93 %. D: death or severe disability at 6 months.

</details>

**3.** In EOLIA, 60-day mortality was 35 % with ECMO vs 46 % in the control group (RR 0.76; 95 % CI 0.55–1.04). The most accurate interpretation is:

- A. ECMO is harmful in severe ARDS
- B. The trial proved equivalence of ECMO and conventional ventilation
- C. The trial was stopped early because of excess bleeding
- D. ECMO significantly reduces mortality in severe ARDS
- E. The primary outcome was not statistically significant, and crossover to rescue ECMO in 28 % of controls diluted the comparison

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.** The trial was stopped for futility (not for harm). "Non-significant" does not mean equivalence.

</details>

**4.** Regarding the Bayesian reanalysis of EOLIA (Goligher, JAMA 2018):

- A. The probability of a large absolute risk reduction of ≥ 20 % was high. — T / F
- B. It was a pre-specified primary analysis of EOLIA. — T / F
- C. With most of the priors used, the posterior probability of any mortality benefit (RR < 1) was below 50 %. — T / F
- D. Even with a strongly sceptical prior, the probability of benefit exceeded 85 %. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.** D: 88 %. C: P(RR < 1) was high across all priors, about 88–99 %. A: only 0–4 %. B: post hoc.

</details>

**5.** A patient on VV-ECMO for severe ARDS has adequate oxygenation but PaCO₂ is rising. The most direct way to increase CO₂ removal is to:

- A. Start inhaled nitric oxide
- B. Increase the sweep gas flow through the oxygenator
- C. Increase the ventilator FiO₂
- D. Increase PEEP to 20 cmH₂O
- E. Reduce the extracorporeal blood flow

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** CO₂ elimination on ECMO is driven mainly by gas flow through the oxygenator (sweep); oxygenation mainly by blood flow.

</details>

**6.** Regarding the physiology of VV-ECMO:

- A. Recirculation increases the effective oxygen delivery of the circuit. — T / F
- B. Arterial oxygenation depends largely on extracorporeal blood flow relative to the patient's cardiac output. — T / F
- C. It provides direct circulatory support to a failing left ventricle. — T / F
- D. In the presence of severe cardiogenic shock, VA configuration is usually needed instead. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – T.** A: recirculated (already oxygenated) blood is drained back into the circuit – effective oxygen delivery falls. C: VV replaces the lungs, not the heart.

</details>

**7.** According to the 2023 ESICM ARDS guidelines, VV-ECMO in severe ARDS not due to COVID-19:

- A. Is suggested only after 14 days of mechanical ventilation
- B. Is recommended against because EOLIA was negative
- C. Has no recommendation because there are no RCTs
- D. Is recommended for patients meeting EOLIA criteria, with moderate certainty of evidence
- E. Is recommended for all patients with PaO₂/FiO₂ < 150 mmHg

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.** Strong recommendation, moderate certainty; meta-analysis of CESAR + EOLIA RR 0.72.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.** C: 46 vs 28 %. D: 27 vs 16 %. A: 0 vs 5 %. B: 35 vs 58 %.

</details>

**9.** Which ventilator strategy corresponds to the ECMO-arm protocol of EOLIA?

- A. Tidal volume 8 mL/kg PBW, PEEP 5 cmH₂O, FiO₂ 1.0
- B. Plateau pressure ≤ 35 cmH₂O, PEEP 0, RR 35/min
- C. Plateau pressure ≤ 24 cmH₂O, PEEP ≥ 10 cmH₂O, RR 10–30/min, FiO₂ 0.3–0.5

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Ultra-protective ventilation – "lung rest".

</details>

**10.** Regarding the selection of patients for VV-ECMO:

- A. ECMO should be considered before prone positioning has been tried. — T / F
- B. Early referral to an experienced ECMO centre is a key lesson from CESAR. — T / F
- C. In EOLIA, patients ventilated for more than 7 days were excluded. — T / F
- D. ECMO is a treatment that reverses the underlying lung injury. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.** A: prone positioning and lung-protective ventilation should be optimised first. D: ECMO buys time and allows "lung rest"; it does not treat ARDS.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Explain why CESAR and EOLIA, despite both being cited in support of VV-ECMO, actually tested different questions, and why EOLIA is formally "negative".

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- CESAR randomised to **referral to an ECMO centre** vs conventional care in the referring hospital – it tested a referral strategy, not ECMO itself.
- In CESAR only ~75 % (68/90) of the intervention arm received ECMO, and the control arm had no ventilation protocol (lung-protective ventilation 70 vs 93 %).
- EOLIA randomised to **early ECMO** vs conventional management, with rescue ECMO allowed: 28 % of controls crossed over.
- EOLIA 60-day mortality 35 vs 46 %, RR 0.76 (0.55–1.04), p = 0.09 – primary endpoint not met; stopped for futility.
- It was powered for an unrealistic 20 % absolute mortality reduction; the observed ARR was ~11 %, and crossover diluted the ITT effect.
- The post hoc Bayesian reanalysis showed an 88–99 % probability of some benefit, but uncertainty about its size.
- ESICM 2023 meta-analysis (CESAR + EOLIA) RR 0.72 (0.57–0.91) underpins a strong recommendation with moderate certainty for patients meeting EOLIA criteria.

</details>

**O2. (open)** A patient with severe non-COVID ARDS has had PaO₂/FiO₂ < 80 mmHg for 7 hours despite optimised ventilation. Outline your approach to VV-ECMO and to ventilation once ECMO is running.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Confirm that lung-protective ventilation, PEEP optimisation, prone positioning and NMB have been used; ECMO is part of a stepwise algorithm.
- The patient meets an EOLIA criterion (P/F < 80 mmHg for > 6 h); check exclusions (MV > 7 days, indication for VA-ECMO, irreversible disease, contraindication to anticoagulation).
- Refer early to an experienced ECMO centre (lesson from CESAR); ESICM 2023 recommends VV-ECMO in this setting.
- Typical cannulation: femoral drainage, internal jugular return; oxygenation driven by blood flow, CO₂ removal by sweep gas.
- Ultra-protective ventilation: Pplat ≤ 24 cmH₂O, PEEP ≥ 10 cmH₂O, RR 10–30/min, FiO₂ 0.3–0.5; targets PaO₂ 65–90 mmHg or SaO₂ > 90 %, PaCO₂ < 45 mmHg.
- Heparin anticoagulation (aPTT 40–55 s or anti-Xa 0.2–0.3 IU/mL); monitor for bleeding, thrombocytopenia, circuit thrombosis, haemolysis and recirculation.
- Wean by reducing circuit FiO₂, then sweep gas, with an off-sweep trial.

</details>


## W11 · D2 – Oxygenation targets: HOT-ICU, ICU-ROX, LOCO2, UK-ROX and the harm of hyperoxia

**1.** In the HOT-ICU trial of adults with acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, a PaO₂ target of 8 kPa compared with 12 kPa:

- A. Increased 90-day mortality
- B. Was only tested in patients with COPD
- C. Increased the incidence of mesenteric ischaemia
- D. Did not significantly change 90-day mortality
- E. Reduced 90-day mortality

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.** 42.9 vs 42.4 %, HR 1.02. Serious ischaemic events did not differ either.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the LOCO2 trial:

- A. It enrolled patients with ARDS. — T / F
- B. Mesenteric ischaemia occurred more often in the liberal arm. — T / F
- C. The conservative arm targeted PaO₂ 90–105 mmHg. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.** B: 5 vs 0 % in the conservative arm. C: conservative = PaO₂ 55–70 mmHg (SpO₂ 88–92 %); 90–105 mmHg was the liberal arm.

</details>

**3.** The IOTA meta-analysis (Lancet 2018) concluded that in acutely ill adults:

- A. Liberal oxygen reduces mortality in sepsis
- B. Liberal oxygen therapy is associated with increased in-hospital and 30-day mortality
- C. Conservative oxygen increases myocardial infarction
- D. Oxygen should be titrated to SpO₂ 100 %
- E. There is no relationship between SpO₂ and mortality

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** RR 1.21 (in-hospital) and 1.14 (30-day). Risk increased with higher SpO₂.

</details>

**4.** Regarding ICU-ROX:

- A. The conservative arm used an upper SpO₂ limit of 97 %. — T / F
- B. It enrolled mechanically ventilated adults in Australia and New Zealand. — T / F
- C. Subgroup findings (e.g. hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy) should be considered hypothesis-generating. — T / F
- D. Conservative oxygen significantly increased ventilator-free days. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.** D: 21.3 vs 22.1, NS.

</details>

**5.** A 60-year-old ventilated patient with pneumonia has SpO₂ 100 % on FiO₂ 0.7, and PaO₂ is 22 kPa. The most appropriate action is:

- A. Titrate FiO₂ down, aiming for SpO₂ around 92–96 %
- B. Target PaO₂ of 6 kPa to minimise oxidative stress

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Hyperoxia has no benefit (IOTA). An abrupt fall in FiO₂ or an extremely low target is not safe (LOCO2).

</details>

**9.** A patient with ARDS is ventilated with FiO₂ 0.6. Based on LOCO2, which oxygenation target should be avoided?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** In LOCO2 this conservative target was associated with higher 90-day mortality and mesenteric ischaemia.

</details>

**10.** Regarding pulse oximetry and oxygen targets:

- A. Pulse oximetry tends to underestimate arterial saturation, particularly in patients with darker skin. — T / F
- B. In hypercapnic COPD, an SpO₂ target of 88–92 % is commonly used. — T / F
- C. Occult hypoxaemia is more relevant when low SpO₂ targets are used. — T / F
- D. Arterial blood gas analysis is unnecessary when SpO₂ is in range. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.** A: it may overestimate saturation (occult hypoxaemia), especially in darker skin.

</details>

**O1. (open)** IOTA showed harm from liberal oxygen, yet HOT-ICU, ICU-ROX and UK-ROX found no mortality benefit from conservative targets. Explain this apparent discrepancy.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- IOTA (25 RCTs, 16 037 patients) showed harm from liberal O₂: in-hospital RR 1.21, 30-day RR 1.14, with risk rising with higher SpO₂.
- Harm from hyperoxia is not the same as benefit from a low target.
- IOTA included very liberal strategies (high FiO₂ regardless of saturation); ICU trials compared "low-normal" vs "high-normal" oxygenation.
- Small separation between arms: relatively low O₂ in the ICU-ROX control group; only ~2 % difference in mean SpO₂ in UK-ROX.
- Heterogeneous populations may hide opposite subgroup effects (ICU-ROX signals for HIE vs sepsis, not confirmed in UK-ROX).
- Different target metrics (SpO₂ vs PaO₂), with SpO₂ targets affected by oximetry inaccuracy.
- Conclusion: within the usual range the target probably matters little; the extremes are harmful.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Outline your oxygen-titration strategy for (a) a ventilated patient with ARDS and (b) a patient with hypercapnic COPD, including the role of pulse oximetry.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- General corridor: SpO₂ ~92–96 % or PaO₂ ~8–12 kPa (60–90 mmHg); avoid both extremes.
- Actively titrate FiO₂ down; SpO₂ persistently 98–100 % on FiO₂ > 0.4 means unnecessary FiO₂.
- ARDS: do not go below PaO₂ ~55–60 mmHg / SpO₂ ~88 % – in LOCO2 the 55–70 mmHg target was associated with higher 90-day mortality (44.4 vs 30.4 %) and mesenteric ischaemia (5 vs 0 %).
- Hypercapnic COPD: SpO₂ 88–92 % to avoid oxygen-induced hypercapnia (V/Q worsening, Haldane effect).
- Pulse oximetry may overestimate SaO₂ (occult hypoxaemia), especially with darker skin pigmentation and poor perfusion – more relevant at low targets.
- Verify with arterial blood gas analysis when in doubt.

</details>

**10.** Regarding the HACOR score:

**O1. (open)** A patient with pneumonia has been on HFNO (FiO₂ 0.6, 60 L/min) for 12 hours with SpO₂ 93 % and RR 32/min. Calculate the ROX index, interpret it, and outline your next steps.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- ROX = (SpO₂/FiO₂)/RR = (93/0.6)/32 = 155/32 ≈ 4.84.
- At 12 h this is above the high-risk threshold (< 3.85) but below the 4.88 threshold associated with a lower risk of intubation – an intermediate, "grey zone" value.
- Continue HFNO with close reassessment of ROX over time (predictive power increases with time).
- Look for signs of excessive respiratory drive/effort (P-SILI concept: large transpulmonary pressure swings, pendelluft, oedema).
- Do not delay intubation if the trajectory worsens – failure of non-invasive support with late intubation is associated with worse outcome.
- ROX is validated for HFNO in pneumonia; for NIV, use HACOR (> 5 after 1 h predicts failure).

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why NIV is not routinely recommended in de novo acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, whereas it is strongly recommended in COPD exacerbation with acidosis.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- ERS/ATS 2017: NIV in hypercapnic COPD exacerbation (pH ≤ 7.35) – strong recommendation, high certainty; cardiogenic oedema – strong, moderate; de novo ARF – no recommendation.
- FLORALI: face-mask NIV had the highest 90-day mortality (28 % vs 12 % HFNO; HR 2.50 vs HFNO) and fewest ventilator-free days.
- Large tidal volumes on NIV (expired V_T > 9.5 mL/kg PBW) predict failure (Carteaux 2016).
- P-SILI concept: high drive → large transpulmonary swings, pendelluft, increased transmural vascular pressure and oedema.
- Delayed intubation after NIV failure worsens outcome (HACOR cohort: lower mortality with intubation < 12 h).
- Helmet NIV may perform better (higher sustained PEEP; Patel, HENIVOT), but evidence is limited/hypothesis-generating.
- ESICM 2023: HFNO over conventional O₂ (strong, moderate certainty); HFNO vs NIV – no recommendation.

</details>
