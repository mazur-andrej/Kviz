## W11 · D1 – VV-ECMO in ARDS: CESAR, EOLIA and the Bayesian reanalysis

**1.** Which of the following was an inclusion criterion of the EOLIA trial (despite optimised ventilation)?

- A. PaO₂/FiO₂ < 80 mmHg for > 6 hours
- B. Murray lung injury score ≥ 2.5
- C. Mechanical ventilation for more than 7 days
- D. PaO₂/FiO₂ < 150 mmHg for > 12 hours
- E. pH < 7.35 with PaCO₂ > 50 mmHg for > 1 hour

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** EOLIA enrolled very severe ARDS despite optimised ventilation if one of three criteria was met: PaO₂/FiO₂ < 50 mmHg for > 3 h, PaO₂/FiO₂ < 80 mmHg for > 6 h, or pH < 7.25 with PaCO₂ ≥ 60 mmHg for > 6 h. These criteria are now the threshold for VV-ECMO in the ESICM 2023 guideline.

**Why not the others:**

- B – A Murray score (≥ 3, not 2.5) was the CESAR entry criterion, not EOLIA's.
- C – MV > 7 days was an exclusion criterion; patients had to be intubated < 7 days.
- D – P/F < 150 mmHg defines moderate–severe ARDS (prone positioning threshold), far above ECMO thresholds.
- E – The hypercapnic criterion was pH < 7.25 with PaCO₂ ≥ 60 mmHg for > 6 h, not a mild 1-h acidosis.

**EDIC trap:** Murray score = CESAR; P/F and pH/PaCO₂ cut-offs = EOLIA.

**Ref:** Combes A, et al. EOLIA. *N Engl J Med* 2018;378:1965–1975. Grasselli G, et al. ESICM ARDS guidelines. *Intensive Care Med* 2023;49:727–759.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the CESAR trial:

- A. Patients were randomised to referral to an ECMO centre or to conventional management. — T / F
- B. All patients in the intervention arm received ECMO. — T / F
- C. The control arm followed a mandatory lung-protective ventilation protocol. — T / F
- D. The primary outcome was 28-day mortality. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – CESAR randomised 180 UK adults to transfer to an ECMO centre (Glenfield) for consideration of ECMO vs conventional management in the referring hospital.
- B (False) – Only ~75 % (68/90) of the intervention arm received ECMO; 5 died before or during transfer and 17 improved on conventional care in the centre.
- C (False) – The control arm had no mandated protocol; lung-protective ventilation was used in 70 % of controls vs 93 % in the referral arm.
- D (False) – The primary outcome was death or severe disability at 6 months (37 % vs 53 %, RR 0.69).

**EDIC trap:** CESAR proved the benefit of referral to an ECMO centre, not of ECMO itself.

**Ref:** Peek GJ, et al. CESAR. *Lancet* 2009;374:1351–1363.

</details>

**3.** In EOLIA, 60-day mortality was 35 % with ECMO vs 46 % in the control group (RR 0.76; 95 % CI 0.55–1.04). The most accurate interpretation is:

- A. ECMO is harmful in severe ARDS
- B. The trial proved equivalence of ECMO and conventional ventilation
- C. The trial was stopped early because of excess bleeding
- D. ECMO significantly reduces mortality in severe ARDS
- E. The primary outcome was not statistically significant, and crossover to rescue ECMO in 28 % of controls diluted the comparison

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** The 95 % CI of RR 0.76 (0.55–1.04) crosses 1, so the primary outcome was not significant (p = 0.09). Moreover, 28 % of controls received rescue ECMO (with 57 % mortality), which dilutes the intention-to-treat difference; the trial was also powered for an unrealistic 20 % absolute reduction.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Mortality was numerically lower with ECMO; no signal of harm on mortality.
- B – A non-significant superiority trial does not demonstrate equivalence.
- C – The DSMB stopped EOLIA for futility, not for bleeding.
- D – The difference did not reach statistical significance, so a significant reduction cannot be claimed.

**EDIC trap:** "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" – EOLIA compared early ECMO vs conventional care with rescue ECMO.

**Ref:** Combes A, et al. EOLIA. *N Engl J Med* 2018;378:1965–1975.

</details>

**4.** Regarding the Bayesian reanalysis of EOLIA (Goligher, JAMA 2018):

- A. The probability of a large absolute risk reduction of ≥ 20 % was high. — T / F
- B. It was a pre-specified primary analysis of EOLIA. — T / F
- C. With most of the priors used, the posterior probability of any mortality benefit (RR < 1) was below 50 %. — T / F
- D. Even with a strongly sceptical prior, the probability of benefit exceeded 85 %. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – The posterior probability of ARR ≥ 20 % (the effect EOLIA was powered for) was only 0–4 %, showing the trial assumed an unrealistic effect.
- B (False) – It was a post hoc Bayesian reanalysis, not a pre-specified analysis of EOLIA.
- C (False) – P(RR < 1) was high across all priors, about 88–99 %.
- D (True) – Even with a strongly sceptical prior the posterior probability of any mortality benefit was 88 % (posterior RR 0.88).

**EDIC trap:** a high probability of *some* benefit is not the same as certainty of a *large* benefit (P(RR < 0.67) 0–48 %).

**Ref:** Goligher EC, et al. Posterior probability of mortality benefit in EOLIA. *JAMA* 2018;320:2251–2259.

</details>

**5.** A patient on VV-ECMO for severe ARDS has adequate oxygenation but PaCO₂ is rising. The most direct way to increase CO₂ removal is to:

- A. Start inhaled nitric oxide
- B. Increase the sweep gas flow through the oxygenator
- C. Increase the ventilator FiO₂
- D. Increase PEEP to 20 cmH₂O
- E. Reduce the extracorporeal blood flow

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** On VV-ECMO, CO₂ removal is governed mainly by the sweep gas flow through the oxygenator, because CO₂ diffuses easily and the gradient is maintained by washing it out. Increasing sweep is the fastest and most direct lever; oxygenation, in contrast, depends mainly on extracorporeal blood flow relative to cardiac output.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Inhaled NO improves V/Q matching and oxygenation transiently but does not meaningfully increase CO₂ elimination.
- C – Ventilator FiO₂ affects oxygenation of native lung blood, not CO₂ clearance.
- D – PEEP 20 cmH₂O increases stress and may raise dead space; it is not a CO₂-removal strategy on ECMO.
- E – Reducing blood flow lowers circuit gas exchange and would worsen hypercapnia.

**EDIC trap:** blood flow → O₂; sweep gas → CO₂.

**Ref:** Tonna JE, et al. ELSO VV-ECMO guideline for adult respiratory failure. *ASAIO J* 2021;67:601–610.

</details>

**6.** Regarding the physiology of VV-ECMO:

- A. Recirculation increases the effective oxygen delivery of the circuit. — T / F
- B. Arterial oxygenation depends largely on extracorporeal blood flow relative to the patient's cardiac output. — T / F
- C. It provides direct circulatory support to a failing left ventricle. — T / F
- D. In the presence of severe cardiogenic shock, VA configuration is usually needed instead. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – Recirculated blood is already oxygenated and is drained back into the circuit, so effective oxygen delivery to the patient falls at the same flow; it is reduced by cannula distance and position.
- B (True) – Arterial oxygenation depends on the ratio of ECMO blood flow to cardiac output; with high cardiac output a greater fraction of venous blood bypasses the circuit.
- C (False) – VV-ECMO returns blood to the venous side; it replaces the lungs, not the heart, and gives no direct LV support.
- D (True) – Severe cardiogenic shock requires VA-ECMO (or a hybrid configuration); a VA indication was an EOLIA exclusion.

**EDIC trap:** improved oxygenation on VV may indirectly help the RV, but VV is not circulatory support.

**Ref:** Tonna JE, et al. ELSO VV-ECMO guideline. *ASAIO J* 2021;67:601–610. Combes A, et al. EOLIA. *N Engl J Med* 2018;378:1965–1975.

</details>

**7.** According to the 2023 ESICM ARDS guidelines, VV-ECMO in severe ARDS not due to COVID-19:

- A. Is suggested only after 14 days of mechanical ventilation
- B. Is recommended against because EOLIA was negative
- C. Has no recommendation because there are no RCTs
- D. Is recommended for patients meeting EOLIA criteria, with moderate certainty of evidence
- E. Is recommended for all patients with PaO₂/FiO₂ < 150 mmHg

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** ESICM 2023 recommends VV-ECMO for selected patients with severe non-COVID ARDS meeting EOLIA criteria (strong recommendation, moderate certainty). The recommendation rests on a meta-analysis of CESAR + EOLIA showing 60-day mortality RR 0.72 (0.57–0.91), delivered in experienced centres.

**Why not the others:**

- A – EOLIA excluded MV > 7 days; late ECMO after 14 days is not the recommendation.
- B – EOLIA was not significant, but pooled data with CESAR support benefit; there is no recommendation against.
- C – Two RCTs (CESAR, EOLIA) exist; the evidence gap concerns COVID-19 ARDS.
- E – P/F < 150 mmHg is the prone positioning threshold, not the ECMO threshold.

**EDIC trap:** "EOLIA was negative, so ECMO is not recommended" – false.

**Ref:** Grasselli G, et al. ESICM ARDS guidelines. *Intensive Care Med* 2023;49:727–759.

</details>

**8.** In the ECMO arm of EOLIA, compared with controls:

- A. Ischaemic stroke was less frequent. — T / F
- B. Treatment failure (death, or crossover to ECMO in controls) was significantly lower. — T / F
- C. Bleeding requiring transfusion was more frequent. — T / F
- D. Severe thrombocytopenia was more frequent. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – Ischaemic stroke occurred in 0 % with ECMO vs 5 % in controls.
- B (True) – Treatment failure (death in ECMO arm; death or crossover in controls) was 35 % vs 58 %, RR 0.62 (0.47–0.82).
- C (True) – Bleeding requiring transfusion was more frequent with ECMO: 46 % vs 28 %.
- D (True) – Severe thrombocytopenia (< 20 000/mm³) was more frequent: 27 % vs 16 %.

**EDIC trap:** ECMO increased bleeding and thrombocytopenia, yet ischaemic stroke was less frequent – all four statements are true.

**Ref:** Combes A, et al. EOLIA. *N Engl J Med* 2018;378:1965–1975.

</details>

**9.** Which ventilator strategy corresponds to the ECMO-arm protocol of EOLIA?

- A. Tidal volume 8 mL/kg PBW, PEEP 5 cmH₂O, FiO₂ 1.0
- B. Plateau pressure ≤ 35 cmH₂O, PEEP 0, RR 35/min
- C. Plateau pressure ≤ 24 cmH₂O, PEEP ≥ 10 cmH₂O, RR 10–30/min, FiO₂ 0.3–0.5
- D. High-frequency oscillatory ventilation
- E. Immediate extubation once ECMO is running

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The EOLIA ECMO arm used ultra-protective "lung rest" ventilation: plateau pressure ≤ 24 cmH₂O, PEEP ≥ 10 cmH₂O, RR 10–30/min and FiO₂ 0.3–0.5. Gas exchange is handled by the circuit, so the ventilator mainly keeps the lung open with minimal driving pressure.

**Why not the others:**

- A – 8 mL/kg with PEEP 5 and FiO₂ 1.0 is neither lung-protective nor oxygen-sparing.
- B – Pplat 35 cmH₂O and PEEP 0 would cause barotrauma and atelectrauma.
- D – HFOV was not part of the protocol and is not recommended in ARDS (OSCILLATE).
- E – Awake/extubated ECMO is used only in selected patients; it was not the EOLIA protocol.

**Ref:** Combes A, et al. EOLIA. *N Engl J Med* 2018;378:1965–1975. Tonna JE, et al. ELSO VV-ECMO guideline. *ASAIO J* 2021;67:601–610.

</details>

**10.** Regarding the selection of patients for VV-ECMO:

- A. ECMO should be considered before prone positioning has been tried. — T / F
- B. Early referral to an experienced ECMO centre is a key lesson from CESAR. — T / F
- C. In EOLIA, patients ventilated for more than 7 days were excluded. — T / F
- D. ECMO is a treatment that reverses the underlying lung injury. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – ECMO is the last step of a stepwise algorithm; lung-protective ventilation, PEEP optimisation, prone positioning and NMB should be applied first (most EOLIA patients were proned before randomisation).
- B (True) – CESAR showed the benefit of timely referral to an experienced ECMO centre.
- C (True) – Patients ventilated > 7 days were excluded from EOLIA.
- D (False) – ECMO does not treat ARDS; it buys time and allows lung rest, limiting VILI while the lung heals.

**EDIC trap:** ECMO is a bridge (to recovery or transplant), not a therapy for the lung injury.

**Ref:** Peek GJ, et al. CESAR. *Lancet* 2009;374:1351–1363. Grasselli G, et al. ESICM ARDS guidelines. *Intensive Care Med* 2023;49:727–759.

</details>

**O2. (open)** A patient with severe non-COVID ARDS has had PaO₂/FiO₂ < 80 mmHg for 7 hours despite optimised ventilation. Outline your approach to VV-ECMO and to ventilation once ECMO is running.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Confirm that lung-protective ventilation, PEEP optimisation, prone positioning and NMB have been used; ECMO is part of a stepwise algorithm.
- The patient meets an EOLIA criterion (P/F < 80 mmHg for > 6 h); check exclusions (MV > 7 days, indication for VA-ECMO, irreversible disease, contraindication to anticoagulation).
- Refer early to an experienced ECMO centre (lesson from CESAR); ESICM 2023 recommends VV-ECMO in this setting.
- Typical cannulation: femoral drainage, internal jugular return; oxygenation driven by blood flow, CO₂ removal by sweep gas.
- Ultra-protective ventilation: Pplat ≤ 24 cmH₂O, PEEP ≥ 10 cmH₂O, RR 10–30/min, FiO₂ 0.3–0.5; targets PaO₂ 65–90 mmHg or SaO₂ > 90 %, PaCO₂ < 45 mmHg.
- Heparin anticoagulation (aPTT 40–55 s or anti-Xa 0.2–0.3 IU/mL); monitor for bleeding, thrombocytopenia, circuit thrombosis, haemolysis and recirculation.
- Wean by reducing circuit FiO₂, then sweep gas, with an off-sweep trial.

</details>


## W11 · D2 – Oxygenation targets: HOT-ICU, ICU-ROX, LOCO2, UK-ROX and the harm of hyperoxia

**1.** In the HOT-ICU trial of adults with acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, a PaO₂ target of 8 kPa compared with 12 kPa:

- A. Increased 90-day mortality
- B. Was only tested in patients with COPD
- C. Increased the incidence of mesenteric ischaemia
- D. Did not significantly change 90-day mortality
- E. Reduced 90-day mortality

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** HOT-ICU randomised 2928 adults with acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure (≥ 10 L/min O₂ or FiO₂ ≥ 0.5) to PaO₂ 8 vs 12 kPa. 90-day mortality was 42.9 vs 42.4 % (HR 1.02), and days alive without life support and serious ischaemic events did not differ either.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Mortality was virtually identical; no increase with the lower target.
- B – HOT-ICU enrolled a broad ICU population with acute hypoxaemic failure, not only COPD (chronic hypercapnia was not the focus).
- C – Mesenteric ischaemia was a LOCO2 signal in ARDS; ischaemic events in HOT-ICU did not differ.
- E – No mortality reduction was seen with the lower target.

**EDIC trap:** HOT-ICU used PaO₂ (not SpO₂) targets and included non-ventilated patients.

**Ref:** Schjørring OL, et al. HOT-ICU. *N Engl J Med* 2021;384:1301–1311.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the LOCO2 trial:

- A. It enrolled patients with ARDS. — T / F
- B. Mesenteric ischaemia occurred more often in the liberal arm. — T / F
- C. The conservative arm targeted PaO₂ 90–105 mmHg. — T / F
- D. It was stopped early because of safety concerns in the conservative arm. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – LOCO2 enrolled 205 French patients with ARDS (within 12 h of onset), the only major oxygen-target trial restricted to ARDS.
- B (False) – Mesenteric ischaemia occurred in the conservative arm (5 vs 0 %), not the liberal arm.
- C (False) – The conservative arm targeted PaO₂ 55–70 mmHg (SpO₂ 88–92 %); PaO₂ 90–105 mmHg (SpO₂ ≥ 96 %) was the liberal arm.
- D (True) – The DSMB stopped the trial early for safety concerns (and futility); 90-day mortality was 44.4 vs 30.4 % with the conservative target.

**EDIC trap:** LOCO2 is the trial that warns against too-low targets in ARDS.

**Ref:** Barrot L, et al. LOCO2. *N Engl J Med* 2020;382:999–1008.

</details>

**3.** The IOTA meta-analysis (Lancet 2018) concluded that in acutely ill adults:

- A. Liberal oxygen reduces mortality in sepsis
- B. Liberal oxygen therapy is associated with increased in-hospital and 30-day mortality
- C. Conservative oxygen increases myocardial infarction
- D. Oxygen should be titrated to SpO₂ 100 %
- E. There is no relationship between SpO₂ and mortality

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** IOTA pooled 25 RCTs (16 037 acutely ill adults) and found that liberal oxygen increased in-hospital mortality (RR 1.21, 1.03–1.43) and 30-day mortality (RR 1.14, 1.01–1.28). Meta-regression showed risk rising with higher achieved SpO₂, with no benefit on morbidity.

**Why not the others:**

- A – No benefit of liberal oxygen was found in any subgroup, including sepsis.
- C – Conservative oxygen did not increase myocardial infarction or other morbidity.
- D – The authors suggested avoiding SpO₂ above ~94–96 %, not targeting 100 %.
- E – A dose–response relationship was found: the higher the SpO₂, the higher the mortality risk.

**EDIC trap:** IOTA shows harm of hyperoxia; it does not prove benefit of low targets.

**Ref:** Chu DK, et al. IOTA. *Lancet* 2018;391:1693–1705. Siemieniuk RAC, et al. Oxygen therapy for acutely ill medical patients: clinical practice guideline. *BMJ* 2018;363:k4169.

</details>

**4.** Regarding ICU-ROX:

- A. The conservative arm used an upper SpO₂ limit of 97 %. — T / F
- B. It enrolled mechanically ventilated adults in Australia and New Zealand. — T / F
- C. Subgroup findings (e.g. hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy) should be considered hypothesis-generating. — T / F
- D. Conservative oxygen significantly increased ventilator-free days. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – In the conservative arm, SpO₂ ≥ 97 % triggered FiO₂ reduction (aim for the lowest FiO₂, target SpO₂ 91–96 %); the usual-care arm had no upper limit.
- B (True) – 1000 ventilated adults in 21 ICUs in Australia and New Zealand.
- C (True) – Signals of benefit in HIE after cardiac arrest and possible harm in sepsis were post hoc and were not confirmed in UK-ROX.
- D (False) – Ventilator-free days did not differ (21.3 vs 22.1, NS), nor did 180-day mortality (35.7 vs 34.5 %).

**EDIC trap:** the ICU-ROX control arm was not very liberal, limiting separation between arms.

**Ref:** Mackle D, et al. ICU-ROX. *N Engl J Med* 2020;382:989–998.

</details>

**5.** A 60-year-old ventilated patient with pneumonia has SpO₂ 100 % on FiO₂ 0.7, and PaO₂ is 22 kPa. The most appropriate action is:

- A. Titrate FiO₂ down, aiming for SpO₂ around 92–96 %
- B. Target PaO₂ of 6 kPa to minimise oxidative stress
- C. Increase PEEP to further improve oxygenation
- D. Reduce FiO₂ to 0.21 immediately regardless of SpO₂
- E. Keep FiO₂ unchanged; hyperoxia is protective in pneumonia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** SpO₂ 100 % with PaO₂ 22 kPa on FiO₂ 0.7 is unnecessary hyperoxia, which carries harm (ROS, absorption atelectasis, vasoconstriction) without benefit. FiO₂ should be actively titrated down to a corridor of SpO₂ ~92–96 % (PaO₂ ~8–12 kPa).

**Why not the others:**

- B – PaO₂ 6 kPa is below the safe range; LOCO2 showed harm with very conservative targets.
- C – Oxygenation is already excessive; increasing PEEP adds stress without need.
- D – Abruptly dropping to room air risks hypoxaemia; titration must be stepwise and guided by SpO₂.
- E – Hyperoxia is not protective in pneumonia; IOTA showed increased mortality with liberal O₂.

**EDIC trap:** avoid both extremes – titrate, don't crash.

**Ref:** Chu DK, et al. IOTA. *Lancet* 2018;391:1693–1705. Schjørring OL, et al. HOT-ICU. *N Engl J Med* 2021;384:1301–1311.

</details>

**6.** Mechanisms by which hyperoxia may be harmful include:

- A. Systemic vasodilatation with increased cardiac output. — T / F
- B. Worsening hypercapnia in some patients with COPD. — T / F
- C. Absorption atelectasis. — T / F
- D. Generation of reactive oxygen species. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – Hyperoxia causes systemic (coronary, cerebral) vasoconstriction and a fall in cardiac output, not vasodilatation.
- B (True) – In COPD, hyperoxia releases hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (worsening V/Q) and, via the Haldane effect, reduces CO₂ carriage by haemoglobin, raising PaCO₂.
- C (True) – High FiO₂ washes out alveolar nitrogen; in low-V/Q units oxygen is absorbed and alveoli collapse.
- D (True) – Reactive oxygen species cause oxidative injury to the alveolar–capillary membrane with prolonged high FiO₂.

**EDIC trap:** oxygen-induced hypercapnia in COPD is mainly V/Q and Haldane, not just "loss of hypoxic drive".

**Ref:** Chu DK, et al. IOTA. *Lancet* 2018;391:1693–1705. O'Driscoll BR, et al. BTS guideline for oxygen use in adults. *Thorax* 2017;72(Suppl 1):ii1–ii90.

</details>

**7.** In the UK-ROX trial (JAMA 2025) of 16 500 mechanically ventilated adults, a conservative oxygen strategy targeting SpO₂ 88–92 %:

- A. Was stopped early because of mesenteric ischaemia
- B. Did not significantly change 90-day mortality compared with usual care
- C. Significantly increased 90-day mortality
- D. Significantly reduced 90-day mortality
- E. Was restricted to patients with ARDS

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** UK-ROX randomised 16 500 ventilated adults in 97 UK ICUs to a conservative target (SpO₂ 90 %, range 88–92 %) vs usual care. 90-day mortality was 35.4 vs 34.9 % (NS). Separation in achieved SpO₂ was modest (~93 vs ~95 %), which may have diluted any effect.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Early stopping for mesenteric ischaemia describes LOCO2, not UK-ROX; serious adverse events were rare.
- C – Mortality was not significantly increased.
- D – Mortality was not significantly reduced.
- E – UK-ROX was a pragmatic trial in all ventilated adults; LOCO2 was the ARDS trial.

**EDIC trap:** no ICU mega-trial shows that conservative oxygen reduces mortality.

**Ref:** Martin DS, et al. UK-ROX. *JAMA* 2025;334:398–408.

</details>

**8.** Which statements comparing oxygen-target trials are correct?

- A. All of HOT-ICU, ICU-ROX and UK-ROX showed lower mortality with conservative oxygen. — T / F
- B. HOT-ICU enrolled only invasively ventilated patients. — T / F
- C. LOCO2 is the only one of these trials restricted to ARDS. — T / F
- D. HOT-ICU used SpO₂-based targets. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – None showed a mortality difference: HOT-ICU 42.9 vs 42.4 %, ICU-ROX 35.7 vs 34.5 % (180 d), UK-ROX 35.4 vs 34.9 %.
- B (False) – HOT-ICU also enrolled non-ventilated patients on ≥ 10 L/min O₂ or FiO₂ ≥ 0.5.
- C (True) – LOCO2 was the only trial restricted to ARDS, and the only one with a harm signal for a low target.
- D (False) – HOT-ICU used PaO₂ targets (8 vs 12 kPa); ICU-ROX and UK-ROX used SpO₂.

**EDIC trap:** SpO₂ (ICU-ROX, UK-ROX) vs PaO₂ (HOT-ICU, LOCO2) targets – know which is which.

**Ref:** Schjørring OL, et al. HOT-ICU. *N Engl J Med* 2021;384:1301–1311. Barrot L, et al. LOCO2. *N Engl J Med* 2020;382:999–1008. Martin DS, et al. UK-ROX. *JAMA* 2025;334:398–408.

</details>

**9.** A patient with ARDS is ventilated with FiO₂ 0.6. Based on LOCO2, which oxygenation target should be avoided?

- A. SpO₂ 92–96 %
- B. PaO₂ 9–10 kPa
- C. Targeting PaO₂ 55–70 mmHg / SpO₂ 88–92 %
- D. PaO₂ 75–90 mmHg
- E. SpO₂ 94 %

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In LOCO2, a conservative target of PaO₂ 55–70 mmHg / SpO₂ 88–92 % in early ARDS was associated with higher 90-day mortality (44.4 vs 30.4 %) and five cases of mesenteric ischaemia, and the trial was stopped early. In ARDS, oxygenation should not be pushed below PaO₂ ~55–60 mmHg / SpO₂ ~88 %.

**Why not the others:**

- A – SpO₂ 92–96 % is the usual safe corridor.
- B – PaO₂ 9–10 kPa (~68–75 mmHg) lies within the 8–12 kPa range tested safely in HOT-ICU.
- D – PaO₂ 75–90 mmHg is a reasonable, non-hyperoxic target.
- E – SpO₂ 94 % is within the recommended corridor.

**Ref:** Barrot L, et al. LOCO2. *N Engl J Med* 2020;382:999–1008. Grasselli G, et al. ESICM ARDS guidelines. *Intensive Care Med* 2023;49:727–759.

</details>

**10.** Regarding pulse oximetry and oxygen targets:

- A. Pulse oximetry tends to underestimate arterial saturation, particularly in patients with darker skin. — T / F

**Why A:** ROX = (SpO₂/FiO₂)/RR = (92/0.7)/36 = 131.4/36 ≈ 3.65. At 12 h, a ROX < 3.85 identifies high risk of HFNO failure, so the patient should be reassessed for timely intubation to avoid harm from delayed intubation.

**Why not the others:**

- B – 5.1 is a miscalculation; ≥ 4.88 would indicate low risk.
- C – 47 results from not dividing by RR correctly (or using FiO₂ in %); ROX values are typically 2–10.
- D – 0.36 comes from inverting the formula.
- E – 4.9 is wrong; the correct value is below the 12-h threshold.

**EDIC trap:** ROX thresholds tighten with time (2.85 at 2 h, 3.47 at 6 h, 3.85 at 12 h).

**Ref:** Roca O, et al. ROX index. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2019;199:1368–1376.

**9.** A patient ventilated for 6 days for sepsis passes a 30-minute SBT on pressure support 8 cmH₂O. Which trial supports this SBT technique over a 2-hour T-piece trial?

**11.** A 45-year-old with an RSBI of 115 breaths/min/L at the start of an SBT. The most accurate interpretation is:

- A. Extubation will certainly succeed
- B. An RSBI > 105 mandates tracheostomy
- C. RSBI is calculated as tidal volume divided by respiratory rate
- D. An RSBI > 105 is associated with weaning failure; assess carefully and look for reversible causes
- E. RSBI is not related to weaning outcome

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** RSBI > 105 breaths/min/L was associated with weaning failure in Yang & Tobin; a value of 115 indicates rapid shallow breathing. It should prompt careful assessment and a search for reversible causes (secretions, fluid overload, weakness), but it does not replace clinical judgement or the SBT.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Even a low RSBI does not guarantee success (specificity 0.64); a high one suggests failure.
- B – No RSBI value mandates tracheostomy.
- C – RSBI = RR divided by V_T in litres, not the inverse.
- E – RSBI is related to outcome; this is the basis of its use.

**13.** Twelve hours after extubation, a patient develops respiratory distress without stridor (RR 35/min, accessory muscle use). NIV is started. Six hours later, there is no improvement. Based on the evidence, what is the main risk?

- A. Pneumothorax from HFNO
- B. Hyperoxia
- C. None; NIV is recommended for established postextubation failure
- D. Laryngeal oedema from NIV
- E. Delayed reintubation, associated with increased mortality

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** In established post-extubation respiratory failure, NIV did not reduce reintubation (48 % both arms) but increased ICU mortality (25 vs 14 %), likely because reintubation was delayed (12 h vs 2.5 h). With no improvement after 6 h, reintubation should not be postponed further.

**Why not the others:**

- A – The patient is on NIV, not HFNO, and pneumothorax is not the main concern.
- B – Hyperoxia is not the issue in a patient in respiratory distress.
- C – ERS/ATS 2017 suggests against NIV in established post-extubation failure.
- D – NIV does not cause laryngeal oedema, and there is no stridor.

**EDIC trap:** preventive NIV in high-risk patients – yes; rescue NIV for established failure – no.

**Ref:** Esteban A, et al. *N Engl J Med* 2004;350:2452–2460. Rochwerg B, et al. ERS/ATS NIV guideline. *Eur Respir J* 2017;50:1602426.
