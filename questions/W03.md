## W03 · D1 – CRBSI/CLABSI: definitions, prevention, treatment, catheter removal

**1.** A patient has a central venous catheter in situ. Paired blood cultures drawn simultaneously grow *S. epidermidis*: the catheter-drawn culture becomes positive 3 hours before the peripheral one. This finding:

- A. Proves endocarditis
- B. Supports catheter-related bloodstream infection without removing the catheter
- C. Is meaningless because the threshold is ≥ 6 hours
- D. Is diagnostic of contamination
- E. Is only interpretable if the catheter tip is cultured

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Differential time to positivity (DTP) – the catheter-drawn culture turning positive ≥ 2 h before the simultaneously drawn peripheral culture – indicates a higher bacterial load in the catheter and supports CRBSI without removing the line. Here the difference is 3 h, so the criterion is met. Equal blood volumes and sampling before antibiotics are prerequisites.

**Why not the others:**

- A – DTP localises the source to the catheter; it says nothing about valvular involvement (that needs echocardiography).
- C – The IDSA threshold is ≥ 2 h, not ≥ 6 h.
- D – Earlier catheter positivity points to true catheter infection, not contamination (although isolated CoNS in one bottle often is a contaminant).
- E – DTP was developed precisely to diagnose CRBSI with the catheter left in situ.

**EDIC trap:** DTP ≥ 2 h and the quantitative ratio ≥ 3:1 are the two "catheter-sparing" criteria.

**Ref:** Mermel, IDSA guideline, Clin Infect Dis 2009; Timsit, Intensive Care Med 2020.

</details>

**2.** Regarding CLABSI and CRBSI:

- A. CLABSI is primarily a surveillance definition. — T / F
- B. CLABSI tends to underestimate the true incidence of CRBSI. — T / F
- C. A semiquantitative tip culture of ≥ 15 CFU with the same organism in peripheral blood supports CRBSI. — T / F
- D. CLABSI requires growth from the catheter tip. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – CLABSI is a surveillance definition (CVC > 2 calendar days, BSI not secondary to another focus) used for benchmarking.
- B (False) – Because it attributes every primary BSI in a patient with a line to the line, without proof, CLABSI **overestimates** true CRBSI.
- C (True) – Roll-plate tip culture ≥ 15 CFU (or ≥ 10² CFU by sonication) with the same organism in peripheral blood is an IDSA CRBSI criterion.
- D (False) – CLABSI requires no tip culture; tip growth is a CRBSI (clinical) criterion.

**EDIC trap:** CLABSI = surveillance, CRBSI = microbiological proof – do not swap them.

**Ref:** Mermel, IDSA guideline, Clin Infect Dis 2009; Timsit, Intensive Care Med 2020.

</details>

**3.** In the CLEAN trial, skin antisepsis with chlorhexidine–alcohol compared with povidone iodine–alcohol:

- A. Increased catheter colonisation
- B. Was only effective when combined with skin scrubbing
- C. Reduced catheter-related bloodstream infection (HR about 0.2) at the cost of more severe skin reactions
- D. Showed no difference in catheter-related bloodstream infection
- E. Was tested only for arterial catheters

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In CLEAN (2349 patients, 11 French ICUs, 2×2 factorial), 2% chlorhexidine–alcohol reduced CRBSI from 1.32 to 0.28 per 1000 catheter-days (HR 0.21) compared with povidone iodine–alcohol. Severe skin reactions were more frequent with chlorhexidine (3% vs 1%), without systemic reactions.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Chlorhexidine–alcohol reduced, not increased, catheter colonisation.
- B – Skin scrubbing before antisepsis added no benefit; chlorhexidine was effective on its own.
- D – There was a large, significant reduction in CRBSI.
- E – Both central venous and arterial catheters were included.

**EDIC trap:** Guidelines recommend chlorhexidine **in alcohol** – not aqueous chlorhexidine and not povidone iodine.

**Ref:** Mimoz et al., CLEAN, Lancet 2015.

</details>

**4.** Components of the Keystone (Pronovost) central line insertion bundle include:

- A. Preferring the femoral site to reduce mechanical complications — T / F
- B. Full barrier precautions during insertion — T / F
- C. Hand hygiene — T / F
- D. Routine replacement of the catheter every 7 days — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – The bundle recommends **avoiding** the femoral site where possible, because of higher infection (and thrombosis) risk.
- B (True) – Maximal barrier precautions (cap, mask, sterile gown and gloves, large sterile drape) are a core element.
- C (True) – Hand hygiene is the first element of the five-item bundle.
- D (False) – Routine scheduled replacement is not recommended; the bundle element is **removal of unnecessary catheters** after daily review.

The fifth element is chlorhexidine skin antisepsis. Keystone (> 100 Michigan ICUs, before–after design) reduced the median CRBSI rate from 2.7 to 0 per 1000 catheter-days.

**EDIC trap:** Keystone was a before–after cohort, not an RCT.

**Ref:** Pronovost et al., Keystone, N Engl J Med 2006.

</details>

**5.** In 3SITES, which insertion site had the lowest rate of the composite of catheter-related bloodstream infection and symptomatic deep vein thrombosis?

- A. No difference between sites
- B. Femoral
- C. Peripherally inserted central catheter
- D. Subclavian
- E. Internal jugular

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** In 3SITES (3471 catheters, randomised site), the composite of CRBSI and symptomatic DVT was 1.5 (subclavian) vs 3.6 (jugular) vs 4.6 (femoral) per 1000 catheter-days. The price was more mechanical complications, mainly pneumothorax (2.1% vs 1.4% vs 0.7%).

**Why not the others:**

- A – There was a clear difference between sites, favouring subclavian.
- B – Femoral had the highest composite rate (4.6/1000 catheter-days).
- C – PICCs were not randomised in 3SITES.
- E – Jugular was intermediate (3.6/1000 catheter-days).

**EDIC trap:** Subclavian = fewest infections and thromboses but most pneumothoraces; choose it for expected prolonged catheterisation by an experienced operator.

**Ref:** Parienti et al., 3SITES, N Engl J Med 2015.

</details>

**6.** A short-term CVC should be removed in catheter-related bloodstream infection due to:

- A. Enterococci — T / F
- B. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* — T / F
- C. *Staphylococcus aureus* — T / F
- D. *Candida albicans* — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – Enterococcal CRBSI: remove a short-term catheter and treat for 7–14 days.
- B (True) – Gram-negative bacilli, including *Pseudomonas*, mandate removal of a short-term CVC; salvage is not attempted for *Pseudomonas*.
- C (True) – *S. aureus* (including MSSA) always requires removal, followed by ≥ 14 days of therapy and TEE.
- D (True) – *Candida* CRBSI requires removal in all cases, with 14 days of therapy from the first negative culture and fundoscopy.

Mycobacteria are the fifth organism on the IDSA "always remove" list for short-term catheters.

**EDIC trap:** Only CoNS (and some Gram-negatives in long-term catheters) allow salvage with lock therapy.

**Ref:** Mermel, IDSA guideline, Clin Infect Dis 2009.

</details>

**7.** A 58-year-old ICU patient has MSSA bacteraemia from a jugular CVC, which has been removed. Blood cultures are negative from day 2; TEE shows no vegetations and there is no metastatic focus. The most appropriate duration of IV therapy is:

- A. 6 weeks in all cases
- B. 7 days, as supported by BALANCE
- C. 5 days
- D. At least 14 days from the first negative blood culture
- E. Until CRP normalises

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Uncomplicated *S. aureus* CRBSI (catheter removed, blood cultures negative within 72 h, no vegetations on TEE, no metastatic focus) is treated for at least 14 days, counted from the first negative blood culture. TEE is required before fixing the duration.

**Why not the others:**

- A – 4–6 weeks is for complicated infection (endocarditis, septic thrombophlebitis, persistent bacteraemia), not all cases.
- B – BALANCE excluded *S. aureus*, so its 7-day result does not apply here.
- C – 5–7 days is the duration for CoNS after catheter removal, not *S. aureus*.
- E – Duration is fixed by microbiology and complications, not by CRP normalisation.

**EDIC trap:** *S. aureus* bacteraemia is never contamination and never a 7-day course.

**Ref:** Mermel, IDSA guideline, Clin Infect Dis 2009; BALANCE, N Engl J Med 2025.

</details>

**8.** Regarding the BALANCE trial:

- A. It compared 7 vs 14 days of antibiotics for bloodstream infection. — T / F
- B. 90-day mortality was significantly higher with 7 days than with 14 days. — T / F
- C. Fewer than 10 % of the patients were in the ICU. — T / F
- D. Patients with *S. aureus* bacteraemia were included. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – BALANCE randomised 3608 patients with bloodstream infection (74 hospitals, 7 countries) to 7 vs 14 days of antibiotics.
- B (False) – 90-day mortality was 14.5% vs 16.1%; 7 days was **non-inferior** (margin 4 percentage points).
- C (False) – About **55%** of patients were in the ICU, so the result applies to critically ill patients.
- D (False) – *S. aureus* bacteraemia and severe immunosuppression were **excluded**.

**EDIC trap:** Do not extrapolate BALANCE to *S. aureus*, *Candida* or complicated infections.

**Ref:** BALANCE Investigators (Daneman et al.), N Engl J Med 2025.

</details>

**9.** In the JAMA 2009 Dressing trial in critically ill adults, which finding is correct?

- A. Chlorhexidine-impregnated sponges increased catheter-related bloodstream infection
- B. The trial was a before–after study
- C. Changing dressings every 7 days was non-inferior to every 3 days
- D. Dressings should be changed every 24 hours
- E. Chlorhexidine sponges were applied only to arterial catheters

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In the 2×2 factorial Dressing trial (1636 patients, 7 French ICUs), changing dressings every 7 days was non-inferior to every 3 days for major catheter-related infection. Chlorhexidine-impregnated sponges reduced CRBSI from 1.3 to 0.40 per 1000 catheter-days (HR 0.24).

**Why not the others:**

- A – CHG sponges reduced, not increased, CRBSI.
- B – It was a multicentre randomised controlled trial, not a before–after study (that was Keystone).
- D – Daily changes are unnecessary; intact transparent dressings are changed every 7 days.
- E – Sponges were applied to both central venous and arterial catheters.

**EDIC trap:** Change dressings earlier only if soiled, wet or detached.

**Ref:** Timsit et al., Dressing trial, JAMA 2009; Timsit et al., Intensive Care Med 2018.

</details>

**10.** A patient with a long-term tunnelled haemodialysis catheter has uncomplicated CRBSI with coagulase-negative staphylococci and no tunnel infection. Regarding management:

- A. Septic shock would be an indication for catheter removal. — T / F
- B. Persisting positive blood cultures at 72 h despite appropriate therapy is an indication for removal. — T / F
- C. Catheter salvage with antibiotic lock therapy plus systemic antibiotics is an option. — T / F
- D. Linezolid is the recommended empirical agent. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – Severe sepsis/septic shock is an indication to remove the catheter, also for long-term devices.
- B (True) – Bacteraemia persisting ≥ 72 h despite appropriate antibiotics mandates removal.
- C (True) – For uncomplicated CoNS CRBSI in a long-term catheter, salvage with antibiotic lock therapy (≥ 1000× MIC) plus systemic antibiotics for 10–14 days is acceptable.
- D (False) – Empirical therapy is **vancomycin** (daptomycin if MRSA MIC ≥ 2); linezolid is not recommended empirically.

Other removal indications: tunnel infection, port abscess, suppurative thrombophlebitis, endocarditis.

**EDIC trap:** Salvage is not recommended for *S. aureus*, *Pseudomonas* or *Candida*.

**Ref:** Mermel, IDSA guideline, Clin Infect Dis 2009.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A septic ICU patient with a 6-day-old femoral CVC grows *S. aureus* in paired blood cultures. Outline your diagnostic and therapeutic management.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Confirm CRBSI: paired cultures (catheter + peripheral, equal volume, before antibiotics); DTP ≥ 2 h or ratio ≥ 3:1, or tip ≥ 15 CFU after removal.
- *S. aureus* is never contamination, even in one bottle.
- Remove the short-term CVC (always for *S. aureus*; septic shock is an additional urgent indication); no guidewire exchange – new site.
- Empirical vancomycin (daptomycin if MIC ≥ 2), not linezolid; add Gram-negative coverage given sepsis and femoral catheter; consider echinocandin if *Candida* risk factors.
- Follow-up blood cultures 2–4 days after starting therapy (several sets for *S. aureus*); day 1 = first negative culture.
- TEE before deciding duration.
- Uncomplicated: ≥ 14 days from first negative culture; complicated (endocarditis, septic thrombophlebitis, persistence ≥ 72 h): 4–6 weeks.
- 7-day course (BALANCE) does not apply – *S. aureus* was excluded.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why CLABSI rates and CRBSI rates differ, and list the evidence-based elements of CVC infection prevention with their supporting trials.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- CLABSI is a surveillance definition (CVC > 2 days, BSI not secondary to another focus) that does not require proof of catheter origin → overestimates true CRBSI.
- CRBSI requires microbiological proof (tip culture, DTP ≥ 2 h, quantitative ratio ≥ 3:1).
- Keystone bundle (before–after, NEJM 2006): hand hygiene, maximal barrier, chlorhexidine, avoid femoral, remove unnecessary lines → median 2.7 → 0/1000 catheter-days.
- CLEAN: chlorhexidine–alcohol vs povidone iodine–alcohol, HR 0.21 (more severe skin reactions).

</details>


## W03 · D2 – Bacterial meningitis and encephalitis

**1.** A 45-year-old man with fever, neck stiffness and confusion (GCS 13) has no focal deficit, no seizures and is not immunocompromised. The most appropriate next step is:

- A. Blood cultures, then lumbar puncture without prior CT, with antibiotics and dexamethasone started immediately
- B. CT head, then lumbar puncture, then antibiotics
- C. Lumbar puncture only after 24 h of observation
- D. MRI brain before any treatment
- E. Antibiotics only after CSF Gram stain is available

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** ESCMID 2016 restricts CT before LP to focal neurological deficit (excluding cranial nerve palsies), new-onset seizures, GCS < 10 or severe immunocompromise. This patient has none, so CT would only delay treatment. Take blood cultures, perform LP and give antibiotics plus dexamethasone without delay (within 1 h of arrival).

**Why not the others:**

- B – Routine CT before LP is not indicated here and postpones antibiotics.
- C – Observation for 24 h delays diagnosis and treatment of a lethal disease.
- D – MRI is not required before treatment and would cause major delay.
- E – Antibiotics must not wait for the Gram stain; delay worsens outcome.

**EDIC trap:** GCS 13 is not "severely impaired consciousness" – the ESCMID cut-off is GCS < 10.

**Ref:** van de Beek et al., ESCMID guideline, Clin Microbiol Infect 2016.

</details>

**2.** Regarding de Gans & van de Beek (NEJM 2002):

- A. The largest benefit was seen in pneumococcal meningitis. — T / F
- B. Mortality was reduced from 15 % to 7 %. — T / F
- C. Unfavourable outcome was reduced from 25 % to 15 %. — T / F
- D. Dexamethasone 10 mg every 6 h for 4 days was given before or with the first antibiotic dose. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – The benefit was driven by pneumococcal meningitis: unfavourable outcome 26% vs 52%, mortality 14% vs 34%; no significant effect in meningococcal disease.
- B (True) – Overall mortality fell from 15% to 7% (RR 0.48).
- C (True) – Unfavourable outcome (GOS 1–4) at 8 weeks fell from 25% to 15% (RR 0.59).
- D (True) – Dexamethasone 10 mg IV every 6 h for 4 days, first dose 15–20 min before or with the first antibiotic dose.

The trial was a double-blind RCT in 301 European adults.

**EDIC trap:** The timing (before/with antibiotics) matters because dexamethasone blunts the inflammatory burst triggered by bacterial lysis.

**Ref:** de Gans & van de Beek, N Engl J Med 2002.

</details>

**3.** A 72-year-old woman has suspected community-acquired bacterial meningitis. Which empirical regimen is most appropriate in a region with pneumococcal resistance?

- A. Amoxicillin alone
- B. Ceftriaxone + vancomycin + amoxicillin (ampicillin) + dexamethasone
- C. Meropenem alone
- D. Ceftriaxone alone
- E. Ceftriaxone + vancomycin

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Age > 50 years requires *Listeria* coverage with amoxicillin/ampicillin, because cephalosporins are inactive against *Listeria*. In regions with pneumococcal resistance, vancomycin (or rifampicin) is added to ceftriaxone. Dexamethasone is given with the first antibiotic dose.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Amoxicillin alone misses resistant pneumococci and β-lactamase-producing organisms.
- C – Meropenem alone is not the guideline regimen and leaves resistant pneumococci uncovered.
- D – Ceftriaxone alone covers neither *Listeria* nor cephalosporin-resistant pneumococci.
- E – Ceftriaxone + vancomycin misses *Listeria* in a 72-year-old.

**EDIC trap:** The classic error is omitting ampicillin/amoxicillin in elderly, immunosuppressed, pregnant or alcoholic patients.

**Ref:** van de Beek et al., ESCMID guideline, Clin Microbiol Infect 2016.

</details>

**4.** According to the ESCMID 2016 guideline:

- A. Dexamethasone may still be started up to 4 hours after the first antibiotic dose. — T / F
- B. Glycerol is not recommended. — T / F
- C. Dexamethasone should be continued when *Listeria monocytogenes* is identified. — T / F
- D. Routine prophylactic antiepileptic drugs are recommended. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – If antibiotics have already been started, dexamethasone can still be given up to 4 h after the first dose (grade C).
- B (True) – Glycerol is not recommended (grade D); it is considered harmful.
- C (False) – Dexamethasone is **stopped** if the pathogen is not *S. pneumoniae* or *H. influenzae*, so it is discontinued for *Listeria*.
- D (False) – Routine prophylactic antiepileptics are **not** recommended; treat seizures when they occur.

Also not routinely recommended: hypothermia, mannitol, hypertonic saline, immunoglobulins, heparin.

**EDIC trap:** Dexamethasone reduces vancomycin CSF penetration, but this is no reason to omit it.

**Ref:** van de Beek et al., ESCMID guideline, Clin Microbiol Infect 2016.

</details>

**5.** In the trial of induced hypothermia (32–34 °C) in comatose adults with bacterial meningitis (Mourvillier, JAMA 2013):

- A. Hypothermia improved neurological outcome
- B. Hypothermia reduced intracranial pressure-related deaths
- C. The trial was stopped early because of higher mortality with hypothermia
- D. Only children were enrolled
- E. No difference in mortality was found after full recruitment

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Mourvillier randomised 98 comatose adults with bacterial meningitis to hypothermia (32–34 °C for 48 h) or standard care. The trial was stopped early for harm: mortality 51% vs 31% (RR 1.99). ESCMID therefore recommends against therapeutic hypothermia.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Neurological outcome was not improved; the trend was towards worse outcome.
- B – There was no reduction in ICP-related deaths; mortality increased.
- D – Only adults were enrolled.
- E – Full recruitment was never reached because of early termination for excess mortality.

**EDIC trap:** Hypothermia and glycerol are the two adjuncts explicitly classed as harmful in bacterial meningitis.

**Ref:** Mourvillier et al., JAMA 2013; ESCMID guideline, Clin Microbiol Infect 2016.

</details>

**6.** Regarding herpes simplex encephalitis (IDSA 2008):

- A. Acyclovir should be started empirically in all patients with suspected encephalitis. — T / F
- B. A negative CSF HSV PCR on day 1 excludes the diagnosis. — T / F
- C. The dose is 5 mg/kg IV every 12 h for 7 days in normal renal function. — T / F
- D. EEG is recommended mainly to detect non-convulsive seizures. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – Empirical acyclovir is recommended in all patients with suspected encephalitis until results are available (A-III).
- B (False) – Early PCR can be falsely negative; if the picture fits, **repeat CSF HSV PCR in 3–7 days** and continue acyclovir.
- C (False) – The dose is **10 mg/kg IV every 8 h for 14–21 days** (adjusted for renal function).
- D (True) – EEG is recommended in all patients, mainly to detect non-convulsive seizures rather than to establish aetiology.

**EDIC trap:** Acyclovir causes crystal nephropathy – ensure hydration and renal dose adjustment, but do not delay it while awaiting PCR or MRI.

**Ref:** Tunkel et al., IDSA encephalitis guideline, Clin Infect Dis 2008.

</details>

**7.** The landmark trial establishing acyclovir as standard treatment of HSV encephalitis compared it with:

- A. Placebo
- B. Vidarabine
- C. Dexamethasone
- D. Foscarnet
- E. Ganciclovir

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Whitley et al. compared acyclovir with vidarabine (the previous standard) in biopsy-proven HSV encephalitis. Six-month mortality was 28% with acyclovir vs 54% with vidarabine, and normal function 38% vs 14%. Acyclovir has been the standard of care since.

**Why not the others:**

- A – A placebo comparison would have been unethical; vidarabine was already the accepted therapy.
- C – Corticosteroids in HSV encephalitis are supported only by retrospective data and are not routinely recommended.
- D – Foscarnet is used for acyclovir-resistant HSV and, with ganciclovir, for CMV – not the landmark comparator.
- E – Ganciclovir is used for CMV encephalitis, not tested against acyclovir for HSV.

**Ref:** Whitley et al., N Engl J Med 1986.

</details>

**8.** Indications for cranial imaging before lumbar puncture in suspected bacterial meningitis (ESCMID) include:

- A. Age over 60 years alone — T / F
- B. Severe immunocompromise — T / F
- C. Isolated cranial nerve palsy — T / F
- D. New-onset seizures — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – Age alone is not an ESCMID criterion for CT before LP.
- B (True) – Severe immunocompromise is an indication (risk of space-occupying lesions such as abscess or toxoplasmosis).
- C (False) – Isolated cranial nerve palsy is explicitly **excluded** from the "focal deficit" criterion.
- D (True) – New-onset seizures are an indication.

The four ESCMID indications: focal neurological deficit (not cranial nerve palsy), new-onset seizures, GCS < 10, severe immunocompromise. If CT is needed, give antibiotics and dexamethasone first, immediately after blood cultures.

**EDIC trap:** Never let CT delay antibiotics.

**Ref:** van de Beek et al., ESCMID guideline, Clin Microbiol Infect 2016.

</details>

**9.** In the French multicentre cohort of HSV encephalitis in the ICU (Jaquet, ICM 2019), which factor was associated with a BETTER functional outcome?

- A. Fever
- B. Older age
- C. Direct ICU admission rather than via the ward
- D. Mechanical ventilation
- E. MRI lesions in more than three lobes

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In 259 ICU patients with HSV encephalitis from 47 French ICUs, 71% had a poor functional outcome (mRS 3–6) at 90 days and mortality was 17%. Direct ICU admission (rather than transfer from the ward) was independently associated with a better outcome, suggesting that delayed ICU care is a modifiable risk.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Fever was independently associated with **poor** outcome (potentially modifiable).
- B – Older age was not the factor associated with better outcome.
- D – Mechanical ventilation was associated with poor outcome.
- E – MRI involvement of more than three lobes was associated with poor outcome.

**EDIC trap:** Note the negative phrasing – three of the options are risk factors for poor outcome.

**Ref:** Jaquet et al., Intensive Care Med 2019.

</details>

**10.** Regarding management of bacterial meningitis:

- A. Chemoprophylaxis is not indicated for close contacts of a patient with meningococcal meningitis. — T / F
- B. Intracranial pressure monitoring is recommended routinely in all patients. — T / F
- C. Culture-negative bacterial meningitis can be treated for 5 days. — T / F

Other ESCMID durations: meningococcus and *H. influenzae* ~7 days, pneumococcus ~10 days.

**O1. (open)** A 68-year-old man presents with fever, neck stiffness and a GCS of 9. Outline your initial management in the first hour, including the role of imaging and adjunctive therapy.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

**O2. (open)** Explain why dexamethasone must be given before or with the first antibiotic dose, and why its benefit in the de Gans trial differed between pneumococcal and meningococcal meningitis.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Antibiotic-induced bacterial lysis releases inflammatory mediators into the subarachnoid space → oedema, vasculitis, ischaemia.
- Dexamethasone dampens this inflammatory burst, so it must be present when lysis starts (before/with the first dose; ESCMID allows up to 4 h after).
- de Gans: 301 adults, unfavourable outcome 15 vs 25% (RR 0.59), mortality 7 vs 15%.
- Pneumococcal subgroup: unfavourable outcome 26 vs 52%, mortality 14 vs 34% – greatest benefit, as pneumococcus carries the worst prognosis.
- Meningococcus: no significant benefit.
- Hence ESCMID: stop dexamethasone if the pathogen is not *S. pneumoniae* or *H. influenzae*.
- Caveat: reduced vancomycin CSF penetration – not a reason to omit dexamethasone (consider rifampicin).

</details>


## W03 · D3 – Necrotising soft tissue infections and toxic shock syndrome

**1.** A 52-year-old diabetic presents with severe thigh pain, mild erythema, tachycardia and a LRINEC score of 4. CT shows fascial thickening without gas. The surgeon proposes observation because "LRINEC is low". The best response is:

- A. Await blood culture results
- B. Proceed to urgent surgical exploration if clinical suspicion persists, as LRINEC has limited sensitivity
- C. Agree – LRINEC < 6 excludes necrotising fasciitis
- D. Repeat LRINEC in 48 hours
- E. Start hyperbaric oxygen first

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** LRINEC ≥ 6 has a pooled sensitivity of only ~68% (specificity ~85%), so about one-third of NSTIs score < 6 and cases with LRINEC 0 are described. **3.** The principal rationale for adding clindamycin to a β-lactam in group A streptococcal necrotising fasciitis is:

- A. Better tissue penetration of clindamycin into necrotic tissue than β-lactams
- B. Inhibition of toxin production and activity independent of bacterial inoculum size
- C. Coverage of *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*
- D. Synergistic cell-wall inhibition
- E. Clindamycin is bactericidal against all GAS strains

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Clindamycin inhibits bacterial protein synthesis at the 50S ribosome and thereby suppresses production of superantigens, streptolysin and M protein. Its activity is independent of inoculum size, bypassing the Eagle effect, in which GAS in the stationary phase at high inoculum downregulates the PBPs targeted by β-lactams.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Necrotic, thrombosed tissue is poorly penetrated by any antibiotic; this is why surgery is essential.
- C – Clindamycin has no activity against *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*.
- D – Clindamycin does not act on the cell wall; it is a protein synthesis inhibitor.
- E – GAS resistance to clindamycin exists (up to ~15% in the USA), so it is never used alone.

**EDIC trap:** Linezolid also has an antitoxin effect and is an alternative if clindamycin resistance is present.

**Ref:** Stevens & Bryant, N Engl J Med 2017; Babiker et al., Lancet Infect Dis 2021.

**Why C:** Pain out of proportion to findings plus shock after minor trauma is NSTI (likely type II, GAS) until proven otherwise. LRINEC 5 does not exclude it (sensitivity ~68%). Management is resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics including clindamycin (antitoxin), and urgent surgical exploration – the only way to confirm the diagnosis.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Imaging must not delay surgical consultation in a shocked patient.
- B – IVIG is not first-line; INSTINCT showed no benefit, and it may only be considered in streptococcal TSS.
- D – Hyperbaric oxygen is not recommended and delays debridement.
- E – Flucloxacillin alone is inadequate and a 24-h wait allows fatal progression.

**EDIC trap:** Survival is better with debridement within 6 h.

**Ref:** Stevens et al., IDSA guideline, Clin Infect Dis 2014; Fernando et al., Ann Surg 2019.

**8.** Regarding adjunctive therapies in necrotising soft-tissue infection:

- A. INSTINCT showed improved 6-month physical function with IVIG. — T / F
- B. Adjunctive clindamycin in invasive GAS infection was associated with lower mortality in a large observational cohort. — T / F
- C. Clindamycin's efficacy is reduced by a high bacterial inoculum (Eagle effect). — T / F
- D. IDSA 2014 does not recommend hyperbaric oxygen. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – INSTINCT found **no difference** in SF-36 PCS at 6 months (36 vs 31, p = 0.81) with IVIG 25 g/day for 3 days.
- B (True) – In Babiker's cohort (1956 patients, 118 US hospitals), adjunctive clindamycin in invasive GAS was associated with lower mortality (aOR 0.44); no RCT exists.
- C (False) – The Eagle effect reduces the efficacy of **β-lactams** at high inoculum (PBP downregulation); clindamycin is unaffected by inoculum size.
- D (True) – IDSA 2014 recommends against hyperbaric oxygen (strong): unproven benefit, risk of delaying resuscitation and debridement.

**EDIC trap:** Clindamycin is never given alone – GAS resistance exists.

**Ref:** Madsen et al., INSTINCT, Intensive Care Med 2017; Babiker et al., Lancet Infect Dis 2021; Stevens et al., IDSA, Clin Infect Dis 2014.

**9.** A 25-year-old woman presents with shock, fever, diffuse erythroderma and vomiting during menstruation. Blood cultures are negative. The most likely diagnosis is:

- A. Necrotising fasciitis type I
- B. Anaphylaxis
- C. Staphylococcal (TSST-1–mediated) toxic shock syndrome
- D. Meningococcal sepsis
- E. Streptococcal toxic shock syndrome

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Shock, fever, diffuse erythroderma and vomiting during menstruation is classic menstrual staphylococcal TSS, mediated by the TSST-1 superantigen. Blood cultures are usually negative, unlike streptococcal TSS. Treatment: remove the tampon, resuscitate aggressively, β-lactam (anti-staphylococcal) + clindamycin.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Type I NSTI is polymicrobial, occurs in diabetics/perineum and presents with local necrosis, not erythroderma.
- B – Anaphylaxis follows an exposure, causes urticaria/bronchospasm rather than fever, and lacks the menstrual context.
- D – Meningococcal sepsis causes a petechial/purpuric rash, not diffuse erythroderma.
- E – Streptococcal TSS usually has a painful soft-tissue focus and positive blood cultures.

**EDIC trap:** Desquamation of palms and soles appears 1–2 weeks later.

**Ref:** Stevens & Bryant, N Engl J Med 2017.

**10.** A 58-year-old with lymphoma receiving chemotherapy has temperature 38.5 °C and ANC 0.1 × 10⁹/L. He is known to be colonised with ESBL-producing *E. coli* and is hypotensive. According to ECIL-4:

- A. Ceftazidime monotherapy is the preferred choice. — T / F
- B. A de-escalation approach starting with a carbapenem is appropriate. — T / F
- C. If he later stabilises and is afebrile ≥ 48 h after ≥ 72 h of IV therapy, antibiotics may be stopped even if still neutropenic. — T / F
- D. Adding a glycopeptide is contraindicated because of haemodynamic instability. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – Septic shock and ESBL colonisation require a de-escalation approach; ceftazidime is inactive against ESBL producers.
- B (True) – ECIL-4: complicated presentation (shock) or known resistant colonisation → start with a carbapenem (± aminoglycoside), then narrow.
- C (True) – Antibiotics may be stopped after ≥ 72 h IV if stable and afebrile ≥ 48 h, regardless of ANC.
- D (False) – Haemodynamic instability is an ECIL-4 **indication** to consider adding a glycopeptide.

**11.** A kidney transplant recipient with PJP and severe hypoxaemia is admitted to the ICU. Which statement is correct?

- A. Treatment should be withheld until BAL confirmation
- B. Atovaquone is the preferred first-line agent
- C. TMP-SMX 15–20 mg/kg/day (TMP component) IV for about 3 weeks is first-line
- D. Adjunctive corticosteroids significantly reduced 28-day mortality in non-HIV patients in the PIC trial
- E. Early NIV has been shown to reduce mortality in immunocompromised patients

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** First-line treatment of PJP in non-HIV patients is TMP-SMX 15–20 mg/kg/day (TMP component) divided into 3–4 doses, given IV in moderate/severe disease, for about 3 weeks (ECIL-6). Treatment should not be delayed for BAL.

**Why not the others:**

- A – BAL remains diagnostic several days after starting treatment, so therapy is not withheld.
- B – Atovaquone is an alternative for mild disease or intolerance, not first-line in severe PJP.
- D – In PIC, 28-day mortality was 21.5% vs 32.4% – not significant (p = 0.069); ECIL-6 advises against routine steroids in non-HIV PJP.
- E – IVNIctus showed no mortality benefit of early NIV (24.1% vs 27.3%).

**EDIC trap:** Steroids are standard in HIV-PJP with PaO₂ < 70 mmHg, not in non-HIV PJP.

**Ref:** Maschmeyer et al., ECIL-6, J Antimicrob Chemother 2016; Lemiale et al., PIC, Lancet Respir Med 2025.

**12.** Regarding acute respiratory failure in immunocompromised patients:

- A. In HIGH, high-flow nasal oxygen significantly reduced 28-day mortality. — T / F
- B. In Efraim, failure to identify the aetiology was associated with higher mortality. — T / F
- C. A positive viral PCR in respiratory samples always proves causality. — T / F
- D. Early antibiotics do not affect the yield of microbiological cultures. — T / F

**13.** A CMV-seropositive, non-immunosuppressed patient with septic shock and ARDS has low-level CMV DNAemia on day 10. Based on GRAIL, which statement is most accurate?

- A. Foscarnet is the first-line prophylactic agent
- B. CMV reactivation never occurs in immunocompetent ICU patients
- C. Ganciclovir increased ventilator-free days as the primary outcome
- D. Ganciclovir prophylaxis reduced mortality in such patients
- E. Ganciclovir reduced CMV reactivation but did not change IL-6 levels or mortality

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** GRAIL randomised 160 CMV-seropositive immunocompetent adults with sepsis or trauma and respiratory failure to ganciclovir or placebo. Ganciclovir significantly reduced plasma CMV reactivation but did not change the primary outcome (IL-6 change, days 1–14) or mortality. Routine anti-CMV prophylaxis is therefore not recommended.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Foscarnet is reserved for treatment when ganciclovir is limited by myelotoxicity or resistance; it is not a prophylactic agent here.
- B – Reactivation is common in seropositive critically ill patients during sepsis-related immunoparalysis.
- C – Ventilator-free days were a secondary outcome (improved in ITT), not the primary.
- D – No mortality reduction was shown.

**EDIC trap:** CMV reactivation is associated with worse outcome, but association ≠ causality.

**Ref:** Limaye et al., GRAIL, JAMA 2017.

**15.** Your ICU has endemic MRSA. Which single strategy has the strongest RCT evidence for reducing ICU bloodstream infections?

- A. Targeted decolonisation of screen-positive patients only
- B. Universal decolonisation with nasal mupirocin and daily chlorhexidine bathing
- C. Routine decolonisation of all carriers of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales
- D. Rapid PCR screening and isolation of carriers
- E. Universal glove and gown use

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** REDUCE MRSA (74 US ICUs, cluster RCT) showed that universal decolonisation – nasal mupirocin plus daily chlorhexidine bathing for all patients – reduced bloodstream infection from any pathogen by 44% (NNT 54) and MRSA clinical cultures by 37%, outperforming targeted decolonisation and screening + isolation.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Targeted decolonisation was inferior to universal decolonisation in REDUCE MRSA.
- C – ESCMID-EUCIC 2019 recommends against routine decolonisation of CRE carriers, and it does not address MRSA.
- D – In MOSAR, rapid PCR screening + isolation brought no further reduction in acquisition.
- E – In BUGG, universal glove and gown use did not reduce MRSA/VRE acquisition (primary outcome).

**EDIC trap:** Horizontal (universal) measures have stronger evidence than vertical (pathogen-specific) ones in endemic settings.

**Ref:** Huang et al., REDUCE MRSA, N Engl J Med 2013.

</details>
