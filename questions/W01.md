## W01 · D1 – Sepsis-3, SOFA and SOFA-2, screening

**1.** According to the Sepsis-3 definitions, sepsis is best defined as:

- A. Suspected infection plus ≥ 2 SIRS criteria
- B. Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection
- C. Infection plus serum lactate > 2 mmol/L
- D. Suspected infection plus qSOFA ≥ 2
- E. Infection requiring ICU admission

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** A = old Sepsis-1/2 (SIRS). D – qSOFA is not a diagnostic criterion. C – lactate is part of the definition of shock, not of sepsis. E – unrelated to the definition.

</details>

**2.** Regarding septic shock according to Sepsis-3:

- A. The criteria apply despite adequate fluid resuscitation. — T / F
- B. Hospital mortality in the derivation cohorts was approximately 10 %. — T / F
- C. Serum lactate > 4 mmol/L is required. — T / F
- D. A vasopressor requirement to maintain MAP ≥ 65 mmHg is required. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.** B – hospital mortality exceeded **40 %** (~10 % is the figure for sepsis, i.e. SOFA ≥ 2). C – the lactate threshold is **> 2 mmol/L**, not > 4 (the 4 mmol/L threshold was used in the older EGDT/SSC era).

</details>

- B. Systolic blood pressure ≤ 100 mmHg
- C. Altered mentation
- D. Heart rate > 90/min
- E. All of the above are qSOFA components

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.** Tachycardia is a SIRS criterion, not a qSOFA criterion.

</details>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.** B – the screening-tool recommendation is strong with moderate certainty of evidence. C – prehospital screening is a new conditional recommendation (very low certainty). D – qSOFA is recommended against as a single screening tool.

</details>

**5.** A patient with pneumonia has a baseline SOFA of 0. Which acute change defines sepsis according to Sepsis-3?

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.** GI and immune dysfunction were not included in SOFA-2. External validation in ~1.24 million patients in 6 cohorts.

**O1. (open)** A ward nurse calls you about a patient with suspected pneumonia who has qSOFA 2. Explain how you would classify this patient according to Sepsis-3, and why qSOFA alone is insufficient both to diagnose sepsis and to screen for it.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Sepsis-3 defines sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection.
- Organ dysfunction = acute increase in SOFA ≥ 2 points due to infection (baseline assumed 0 if unknown) – SOFA, not qSOFA, must be assessed.
- qSOFA (RR ≥ 22/min, altered mentation, SBP ≤ 100 mmHg) is a prognostic tool identifying higher risk of poor outcome; it is not part of the sepsis definition.
- qSOFA has low sensitivity, so it is a poor screening tool.
- SSC 2026 recommends NEWS, NEWS2, MEWS or SIRS over qSOFA as a single screening tool (strong recommendation, moderate certainty).
- If the patient additionally needs a vasopressor for MAP ≥ 65 mmHg with lactate > 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluids, he meets septic shock criteria (mortality > 40 %).

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why the original SOFA score needed updating and summarise the main features of SOFA-2.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Original SOFA (1996): 6 systems, 0–4 points each, max 24.
- Weaknesses: outdated vasopressor doses (dopamine as reference), no account of modern organ support (HFNO, ECMO, RRT, mechanical circulatory support), difficulty assessing GCS in sedated patients.
- SOFA-2 (JAMA 2025) developed by modified Delphi consensus plus data-driven analysis.
- Development/internal validation in ~2.1 million and external validation in ~1.2 million patients from diverse settings.
- Retains 6 systems, renamed: brain, respiratory, cardiovascular, liver, kidney, hemostasis; new variables and thresholds reflecting current organ support.
- GI and immune dysfunction were not included (lack of reliable, widely available variables).
- The sepsis definition (Δ ≥ 2 points) remains tied to the Sepsis-3 concept.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Outline your approach to the timing of antimicrobials according to SSC 2026 in (a) a patient with septic shock, (b) probable sepsis without shock, (c) possible sepsis without shock, and (d) possible sepsis with a low likelihood of infection. Explain the rationale for these differences.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Septic shock (possible or definite): antibiotics immediately, ideally within 1 h of recognition – strong recommendation, very low certainty.
- Probable/definite sepsis without shock: also immediately, ideally within 1 h – strong, very low certainty.
- Possible sepsis without shock: time-limited rapid assessment; if concern persists, antibiotics within 3 h of first suspicion – conditional.
- Low likelihood of infection without shock: defer antibiotics with close monitoring (new in 2026) – conditional.

- Take blood cultures before antibiotics if this does not substantially delay them; do not use PCT to decide on starting antibiotics.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain how a recommendation can be "strong" while resting on "very low certainty" evidence in the GRADE system, using an example from SSC 2026.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- GRADE separates strength of recommendation from certainty of evidence (high / moderate / low / very low).
- "We recommend" = strong (most informed clinicians would act this way); "we suggest" = conditional, with reasonable variability.
- Best practice statements are ungraded but self-evident.
- A strong recommendation can be issued when the benefit/risk balance is clear despite low-quality evidence.
- Example: antibiotics within 1 h in septic shock – strong / very low certainty, based on observational delay–mortality data.
- In SSC 2026 ~63 % of the 129 statements are conditional.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Explain why CLASSIC and CLOVERS were both "negative" for mortality yet tested different questions. What do they together imply for fluid management in septic shock?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- CLASSIC (NEJM 2022): ICU, Europe, septic shock < 12 h after ≥ 1 L of fluid – tested restriction after initial resuscitation.
- CLASSIC separation 1798 vs 3811 mL; 90-day mortality ~42 % in both arms, no signal of harm.

- Practice: ≥ 30 mL/kg within 3 h individualised, then dynamic measures, then active de-resuscitation after the acute phase.
