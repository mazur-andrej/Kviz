## W24 · D1 – Toxidromes and the general approach to the poisoned patient (NICO)

**1.** A 22-year-old man is brought to the emergency department agitated and hallucinating after a party. HR 132/min, BP 150/90 mmHg, temperature 38.6 °C, pupils 7 mm. Which additional finding would best support an anticholinergic rather than a sympathomimetic toxidrome?

- A. Seizure
- B. Hypertension
- C. Dry axillae and a palpable bladder
- D. Tachycardia
- E. Hyperthermia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Both toxidromes cause agitation, mydriasis, tachycardia, hypertension and hyperthermia. Anticholinergic agents block sweating and detrusor/gut muscarinic tone, so dry skin, urinary retention and absent bowel sounds point to anticholinergic poisoning, whereas sympathomimetics cause profuse sweating.

**Why not the others:**
- A – seizures occur in both (cocaine, amphetamines, TCA, diphenhydramine).
- B – hypertension is common to both.
- D – tachycardia is common to both.
- E – hyperthermia occurs in both and does not discriminate.

**EDIC trap:** "Wet or dry?" – the axilla is the quickest discriminator.

**Ref:** Boyer EW, NEJM 2005; Chyka PA, Clin Toxicol 2005.

</details>

**2.** Regarding single-dose activated charcoal in acute poisoning:

- A. It is most effective when given within 1 hour of ingestion. — T / F
- B. It adsorbs lithium and iron effectively. — T / F
- C. It may be given to a drowsy patient with an unprotected airway if a nasogastric tube is used. — T / F
- D. Its main serious complication is aspiration pneumonitis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – AACT/EAPCCT: benefit is greatest within 1 h; later use is considered for modified-release drugs or agents slowing gastric emptying.
- B (False) – metals (iron, lithium), alcohols, hydrocarbons and corrosives are poorly adsorbed.
- C (False) – an unprotected airway is a contraindication; a nasogastric tube does not protect against aspiration.
- D (True) – aspiration of charcoal causes severe pneumonitis.

**Ref:** Chyka PA, Clin Toxicol 2005.

</details>

**3.** A 34-year-old woman on sertraline 200 mg/day was given tramadol for back pain. Six hours later she is agitated and sweating, temperature 38.4 °C, HR 118/min, with inducible ankle clonus, ocular clonus and hyperreflexia in the lower limbs. What is the most appropriate management?

- A. Dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg IV
- B. Bromocriptine via nasogastric tube
- C. Paracetamol 1 g IV for the fever and observation
- D. Haloperidol 5 mg IV for agitation
- E. Stop serotonergic drugs, give benzodiazepines and cyproheptadine, and cool

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** She fulfils the Hunter criteria (inducible clonus with agitation and diaphoresis, ocular clonus) for serotonin toxicity from SSRI plus tramadol. Treatment is removal of the drugs, benzodiazepines, the 5-HT2A antagonist cyproheptadine (oral/NG) and cooling; paralysis and ventilation if temperature exceeds ~41 °C.

**Why not the others:**
- A – dantrolene is the treatment of malignant hyperthermia, not serotonin toxicity.
- B – bromocriptine is a dopamine agonist used in NMS and may worsen serotonin toxicity.
- C – hyperthermia is due to muscle activity; antipyretics are ineffective.
- D – antipsychotics may worsen hyperthermia and confuse the picture with NMS.

**Ref:** Dunkley EJC, QJM 2003; Boyer EW, NEJM 2005.

</details>

**4.** A 40-year-old man presents 90 minutes after an intentional overdose and is alert and cooperative. All of the following substances are poorly adsorbed by activated charcoal EXCEPT:

- A. Lithium carbonate
- B. Carbamazepine
- C. Ferrous sulfate
- D. Ethylene glycol
- E. Methanol

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Carbamazepine is well adsorbed by charcoal, and multiple-dose activated charcoal is recommended in life-threatening carbamazepine poisoning because it also interrupts enteric recirculation.

**Why not the others:**
- A – lithium (a metal ion) is not adsorbed; WBI and haemodialysis are the options.
- C – iron is not adsorbed; use WBI if tablets are seen on X-ray.
- D – ethylene glycol (an alcohol) is rapidly absorbed and not bound.
- E – methanol is not bound; fomepizole and haemodialysis are the treatments.

**Ref:** Chyka PA, Clin Toxicol 2005; AACT/EAPCCT, J Toxicol Clin Toxicol 1999.

</details>

**5.** Regarding the NICO trial of airway management in comatose poisoned patients:

- A. Patients with a Glasgow Coma Scale score below 9 were included. — T / F
- B. The conservative strategy reduced the rate of intubation to under 20 %. — T / F
- C. In-hospital mortality was significantly lower in the conservative arm. — T / F
- D. Patients with suspected cardiotoxic or seizure-inducing poisons were excluded. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – adults with suspected acute poisoning and GCS < 9 in French EDs and one ICU.
- B (True) – intubation 16.4 vs 57.8 %; pneumonia 6.9 vs 14.7 %.
- C (False) – there were no deaths in either arm; benefit came from shorter ICU and hospital stay (win ratio 1.85).
- D (True) – so the results do not apply to TCA, beta-blocker or calcium-channel blocker poisoning.

**Ref:** Freund Y (NICO), JAMA 2023.

</details>

**6.** A 24-year-old woman ingested about 60 ferrous sulfate tablets (65 mg elemental iron each) 5 hours ago. She weighs 55 kg, has vomited blood, BP 85/50 mmHg, pH 7.18, lactate 6 mmol/L; abdominal X-ray shows multiple radio-opaque tablets. Serum iron is pending. Besides resuscitation, what is the most appropriate treatment?

- A. Activated charcoal 50 g via nasogastric tube
- B. Wait for the serum iron level before giving any antidote
- C. Gastric lavage with sodium bicarbonate
- D. Intravenous desferrioxamine 15 mg/kg/h and whole-bowel irrigation once haemodynamically stable
- E. Oral desferrioxamine

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** She took ~70 mg/kg of elemental iron and has systemic toxicity (shock, metabolic acidosis), which is an indication for IV desferrioxamine regardless of the serum iron. Radio-opaque tablets in the gut can be removed with WBI, which is contraindicated while she is haemodynamically unstable.

**Why not the others:**
- A – charcoal does not bind iron.
- B – desferrioxamine is given for clinical toxicity irrespective of the level; waiting delays treatment.
- C – lavage is not routine; bicarbonate lavage is obsolete.
- E – oral desferrioxamine may increase absorption of the chelate; it has no role.

**EDIC trap:** Desferrioxamine infusions beyond 24 h are associated with ARDS.

**Ref:** Thanacoody R, Clin Toxicol 2015; Chyka PA, Clin Toxicol 2005.

</details>

**7.** Regarding toxidromes and calculated gaps:

- A. Organophosphate poisoning typically causes mydriasis and dry skin. — T / F
- B. A normal osmolal gap excludes methanol poisoning. — T / F
- C. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is characterised by hyperreflexia and clonus. — T / F
- D. Methaemoglobinaemia produces a gap between pulse oximetry and arterial oxygen tension–derived saturation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – cholinergic excess causes miosis, sweating, salivation and bronchorrhoea.
- B (False) – in late presentation methanol has been metabolised to formate: the osmolal gap falls while the anion gap rises.
- C (False) – NMS causes lead-pipe rigidity with hyporeflexia; clonus and hyperreflexia suggest serotonin toxicity.
- D (True) – SpO₂ reads ~ 85 % while calculated SaO₂ from a normal PaO₂ is high ("saturation gap"); co-oximetry confirms.

**Ref:** Eddleston M, Lancet 2008; Boyer EW, NEJM 2005.

</details>

**8.** A 45-year-old farmer drank an organophosphate insecticide 2 hours ago. He is drowsy with pinpoint pupils, profuse salivation, wheeze and crackles over both lungs, HR 48/min, BP 80/45 mmHg, and fasciculations. Which is the best end point for atropine titration?

- A. Clear chest on auscultation with resolution of bronchorrhoea, HR > 80/min and SBP > 80 mmHg
- B. Pupils dilated to 6 mm
- C. Heart rate above 120/min
- D. Disappearance of fasciculations
- E. Normalisation of red-cell acetylcholinesterase activity

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Atropine is given in doubling doses every 5 min until atropinisation: clear chest (no bronchorrhoea), HR > 80/min, SBP > 80 mmHg, dry axillae and pupils no longer pinpoint. Bronchorrhoea and bronchospasm are what kill, so a clear chest is the key target.

**Why not the others:**
- B – pupil size is a late and unreliable target; aiming for mydriasis leads to atropine toxicity.
- C – excessive tachycardia indicates over-atropinisation; tachycardia is not a target.
- D – fasciculations are nicotinic and do not respond to atropine.
- E – enzyme activity is not available in real time and recovers slowly.

**EDIC trap:** Avoid suxamethonium for intubation – plasma cholinesterase is inhibited, causing prolonged paralysis.

**Ref:** Eddleston M, Lancet 2008.

</details>

**9.** A 50-year-old man on a methadone programme is found apnoeic with pinpoint pupils. After naloxone 0.4 mg IV he wakes and breathes 14/min. Forty minutes later his respiratory rate falls to 6/min. What is the most appropriate next step?

- A. Flumazenil 0.5 mg IV
- B. Discharge after 1 hour if awake
- C. Immediate intubation and admission to ICU without further naloxone
- D. Activated charcoal via nasogastric tube
- E. Repeat the naloxone bolus and start a naloxone infusion with monitoring

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Naloxone has a half-life of ~60–90 min, much shorter than methadone (> 24 h), so re-narcotisation is expected. Repeat the effective bolus and start an infusion (about two-thirds of the reversing dose per hour), titrated to respiratory rate, in a monitored setting.

**Why not the others:**
- A – flumazenil reverses benzodiazepines, not opioids, and may provoke seizures.
- B – early discharge after long-acting opioid overdose is dangerous.
- C – intubation is reserved for failure of naloxone or aspiration; naloxone is effective here.
- D – the airway is unprotected and charcoal is not useful for methadone at this stage.

**Ref:** Chyka PA, Clin Toxicol 2005; Boyer EW, NEJM 2005.

</details>

**10.** A 29-year-old man with schizophrenia, started on high-dose haloperidol 5 days ago, develops temperature 40.1 °C, generalised rigidity, fluctuating BP and CK 28,000 U/L. Regarding his condition:

- A. Hyporeflexia and lead-pipe rigidity are typical. — T / F
- B. Cyproheptadine is the first-line specific treatment. — T / F
- C. Dantrolene and bromocriptine may be used in severe cases. — T / F
- D. Onset within a few hours of the first dose is required for the diagnosis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – NMS causes bradykinesia, lead-pipe rigidity and diminished reflexes (in contrast to serotonin toxicity).
- B (False) – cyproheptadine is used in serotonin toxicity; NMS is a dopamine-blockade syndrome.
- C (True) – plus stopping the antipsychotic, cooling, benzodiazepines, fluids for rhabdomyolysis.
- D (False) – NMS develops over days to weeks; rapid onset (< 24 h) suggests serotonin toxicity.

**Ref:** Boyer EW, NEJM 2005.

</details>

**11.** A 62-year-old woman receives 30 mL of bupivacaine 0.5 % for an interscalene block. Within 2 minutes she seizes and then develops broad-complex bradycardia progressing to pulseless electrical activity. CPR is started. What specific treatment should be given now?

- A. Lidocaine 1 mg/kg IV
- B. 20 % lipid emulsion 1.5 mL/kg IV bolus followed by an infusion
- C. Vasopressin 40 units IV
- D. Phenytoin 15 mg/kg IV
- E. Propofol 2 mg/kg IV as a lipid source

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** This is local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST). The Lipid Emulsion Workgroup recommends 20 % lipid emulsion for bupivacaine cardiotoxicity: 1.5 mL/kg bolus, then 0.25 mL/kg/min, with repeat boluses for persistent instability, alongside CPR with reduced adrenaline doses.

**Why not the others:**
- A – lidocaine is another sodium-channel blocking local anaesthetic and worsens toxicity.
- C – vasopressin is not recommended in LAST.
- D – phenytoin is a sodium-channel blocker and ineffective for toxic seizures.
- E – propofol contains too little lipid and is a myocardial depressant.

**Ref:** Gosselin S, Clin Toxicol 2016.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Outline your initial assessment and management of a 30-year-old found unconscious at home with empty packets of several unknown tablets.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- **ABC and immediate checks:** airway position, SpO₂, RR, BP, HR, temperature, **capillary glucose**; naloxone trial if miosis and bradypnoea.
- **Airway decision:** intubate for emergency criteria (hypoxaemia, respiratory failure, vomiting/aspiration, seizures, shock) or when cardiotoxic/proconvulsant poisoning is likely; otherwise close observation even with GCS < 9 (NICO).
- **History** from witnesses, packets, prescriptions: agents, amount, time, modified-release, co-ingestion.
- **Toxidrome examination:** pupils, skin, bowel sounds, bladder, tone, reflexes, clonus.
- **Investigations:** ECG (QRS, QTc, aVR), paracetamol and salicylate levels, blood gas, lactate, electrolytes, anion and osmolal gap, CK, renal and liver tests, specific levels as indicated; co-oximetry if CO/methaemoglobin suspected.
- **Decontamination:** SDAC 50 g within 1 h only if airway protected and agent adsorbable; WBI for iron/lithium/MR drugs; MDAC for carbamazepine, dapsone, phenobarbital, quinine, theophylline; no routine lavage.
- **Antidotes** guided by findings; **enhanced elimination** (alkalinisation, ECTR per EXTRIP).
- **Supportive care:** seizures (benzodiazepines), hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, VA-ECMO for refractory cardiogenic shock; poison centre advice; psychiatric review.

</details>


## W24 · D2 – Paracetamol and salicylate poisoning (SNAP, EXTRIP)

**1.** A 19-year-old woman (60 kg) took 16 g of immediate-release paracetamol at a single known time. She is asymptomatic; the 4-hour paracetamol concentration is 180 mg/L and ALT is normal. What is the most appropriate management?

- A. Repeat the level at 8 hours and treat only if it remains above the line
- B. Activated charcoal only, as she presented after 1 hour
- C. Fomepizole 15 mg/kg IV
- D. Start intravenous acetylcysteine now
- E. Urgent haemodialysis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** A single acute ingestion of ~ 270 mg/kg with a 4-h level of 180 mg/L lies above every treatment line (UK 100 mg/L, US/ANZ 150 mg/L). NAC is nearly fully hepatoprotective when started within 8 h of ingestion.

**Why not the others:**
- A – waiting loses the most effective window; a valid 4-h level is already above the line.
- B – charcoal is an adjunct and does not replace NAC; its benefit is greatest within 1–2 h.
- C – fomepizole has no standard role; data are insufficient outside very high-risk ingestions.
- E – ECTR is reserved for extreme levels (> 700–1,000 mg/L) with metabolic failure.

**Ref:** Dart RC, JAMA Netw Open 2023; Chiew AL, Med J Aust 2020.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the mechanism of paracetamol hepatotoxicity:

- A. The toxic metabolite NAPQI is produced mainly by CYP2E1. — T / F
- B. Hepatocellular necrosis predominantly affects the centrilobular (zone 3) region. — T / F
- C. Chronic alcohol use, malnutrition and enzyme-inducing drugs are recognised susceptibility factors. — T / F
- D. Current UK guidance uses a lower nomogram treatment line for patients with these susceptibility factors. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – CYP2E1 (and CYP3A4) oxidise ~ 5–10 % of a therapeutic dose to NAPQI, normally conjugated with glutathione.
- B (True) – zone 3 has the most CYP2E1 and the lowest oxygen tension.
- C (True) – they increase NAPQI production (induction) or reduce glutathione stores.
- D (False) – since 2012 the UK uses a single line (100 mg/L at 4 h) for all patients.

**Ref:** Dart RC, JAMA Netw Open 2023; Chiew AL, Med J Aust 2020.

</details>

**3.** A 25-year-old man took 2 g of paracetamol every few hours over the past day for toothache, about 14 g in total (75 kg). He presents 30 hours after the first dose. Paracetamol concentration is 25 mg/L and ALT 120 U/L (normal < 40). What is the most appropriate management?

- A. Start acetylcysteine without using the nomogram
- B. Plot the level on the nomogram from the time of the first dose and treat if above the line
- C. Discharge, because the level is below 100 mg/L
- D. Activated charcoal and repeat ALT in 24 hours
- E. Refer for liver transplant assessment

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** This is a staggered/repeated supratherapeutic ingestion – the nomogram is invalid. A potentially toxic dose with detectable paracetamol and a raised ALT mandates NAC, then reassessment of ALT, INR and paracetamol at the end of the regimen.

**Why not the others:**
- B – the nomogram applies only to single acute ingestions at a known time.
- C – a "low" level after staggered ingestion does not exclude toxicity.
- D – charcoal is useless 30 h after ingestion; delaying NAC worsens outcome.
- E – there is no evidence of liver failure (normal INR, no encephalopathy).

**Ref:** Dart RC, JAMA Netw Open 2023; Chiew AL, Med J Aust 2020.

</details>

**4.** A 28-year-old woman is in the ICU on day 3 after a paracetamol overdose that presented late. She is receiving acetylcysteine. After adequate fluid resuscitation, which single finding fulfils the King's College criteria for liver transplant listing?

- A. ALT 12,000 U/L
- B. INR 4.0
- C. Arterial pH 7.25
- D. Bilirubin 150 µmol/L
- E. Grade II encephalopathy

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In paracetamol ALF, arterial pH < 7.30 after adequate fluid resuscitation is a stand-alone King's criterion. Otherwise all three of INR > 6.5, creatinine > 300 µmol/L and grade III–IV encephalopathy within 24 h are required.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the height of ALT does not predict outcome.
- B – INR must exceed 6.5 and be combined with renal failure and grade III–IV encephalopathy.
- D – bilirubin is part of the non-paracetamol criteria only.
- E – grade III–IV encephalopathy is needed, and only in combination.

**EDIC trap:** Lactate > 3.5 mmol/L at 4 h or > 3.0 mmol/L at 12 h after resuscitation is the modified criterion.

**Ref:** O'Grady JG, Gastroenterology 1989; Wendon J, J Hepatol 2017.

</details>

**5.** Regarding intravenous acetylcysteine regimens:

- A. The SNAP regimen gives 100 mg/kg over 2 hours followed by 200 mg/kg over 10 hours. — T / F
- B. In the SNAP trial the 12-hour regimen caused less vomiting and fewer anaphylactoid reactions than the 21-hour regimen. — T / F
- C. Anaphylactoid reactions are IgE-mediated, and acetylcysteine must not be restarted after a reaction. — T / F
- D. Acetylcysteine is ineffective if started more than 24 hours after ingestion and should then be withheld. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – total 300 mg/kg over 12 h, with a slower initial rate.
- B (True) – vomiting/retching 36 vs 65 %, anaphylactoid reactions 5 vs 28 %, with no difference in ALT rises.
- C (False) – reactions are non-IgE, rate-related; stop, give antihistamine and restart at a slower rate.
- D (False) – NAC improves survival in established paracetamol ALF and is given whenever there is ongoing toxicity.

**Ref:** Bateman DN (SNAP), Lancet 2014; Dart RC, JAMA Netw Open 2023.

</details>

**6.** A 35-year-old man presents 3 hours after ingesting 100 aspirin tablets of 300 mg. All of the following are typical features of acute salicylate poisoning in an adult EXCEPT:

- A. Tinnitus and deafness
- B. A primary respiratory acidosis as the earliest acid–base disturbance
- C. High anion gap metabolic acidosis
- D. Non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema
- E. Hyperthermia and sweating

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Salicylates directly stimulate the respiratory centre, so the earliest disturbance in adults is a primary respiratory alkalosis, followed by a high anion gap metabolic acidosis from uncoupled oxidative phosphorylation. Respiratory acidosis appears only with exhaustion, CNS depression or co-ingested sedatives, and is ominous.

**Why not the others:**
- A – tinnitus and deafness are classic early symptoms.
- C – lactate and ketoacids accumulate (mixed disorder).
- D – increased capillary permeability causes non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, more often in chronic toxicity and the elderly.
- E – uncoupling generates heat.

**Ref:** Juurlink DN, Ann Emerg Med 2015.

</details>

**7.** Regarding management of salicylate poisoning:

- A. EXTRIP recommends extracorporeal treatment when the salicylate concentration exceeds 7.2 mmol/L (100 mg/dL). — T / F
- B. Altered mental status is an indication for extracorporeal treatment irrespective of the salicylate concentration. — T / F
- C. Urine alkalinisation aims for a urine pH of about 7.5–8.5. — T / F
- D. Hypokalaemia should be corrected because it impairs urine alkalinisation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – also > 6.5 mmol/L (90 mg/dL) with impaired kidney function.
- B (True) – altered mental status, new hypoxaemia requiring O₂ and failure of standard therapy are clinical indications (recommended).
- C (True) – ion trapping increases renal salicylate clearance many-fold; keep serum pH ≤ 7.55–7.60.
- D (True) – with hypokalaemia the distal tubule reabsorbs K⁺ in exchange for H⁺, acidifying the urine.

**Ref:** Juurlink DN, Ann Emerg Med 2015.

</details>

**8.** A 42-year-old man with acute salicylate poisoning (level 820 mg/L) becomes increasingly drowsy. RR 36/min, pH 7.41, PaCO₂ 2.4 kPa (18 mmHg), HCO₃⁻ 11 mmol/L. His GCS falls to 8 and he vomits. Intubation is judged necessary. Which approach is most appropriate?

- A. Rapid sequence induction followed by standard ventilation of 6 mL/kg at 14 breaths/min
- B. Delay intubation and give flumazenil to improve consciousness
- C. Perform intubation without sedation to preserve respiratory drive
- D. Stop the bicarbonate infusion before induction to avoid alkalaemia
- E. Bicarbonate bolus before induction, intubation by an experienced operator, high minute ventilation to match the pre-intubation PaCO₂, and urgent haemodialysis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Loss of compensatory hyperventilation after induction causes acute acidaemia, increasing non-ionised salicylate and its entry into the brain, with seizures and cardiac arrest. A bicarbonate bolus, rapid intubation, high minute ventilation with frequent gases and haemodialysis (altered mental status is an EXTRIP indication) mitigate this.

**Why not the others:**
- A – "normal" ventilation will produce severe respiratory acidosis in this patient.
- B – flumazenil is not indicated and may cause seizures.
- C – awake intubation without sedation is not appropriate in a vomiting, drowsy patient; the issue is ventilation afterwards.
- D – alkalaemia is protective; stopping bicarbonate worsens CNS salicylate penetration.

**Ref:** Juurlink DN, Ann Emerg Med 2015.

</details>

**9.** A 23-year-old man (70 kg) ingested 40 g of immediate-release paracetamol 4 hours ago. He is alert; the 4-hour paracetamol concentration is 520 mg/L, lactate 3.5 mmol/L, pH 7.34. What is the most appropriate initial management?

- A. Start acetylcysteine with an increased dose for a high-risk ingestion, consider activated charcoal and seek toxicology advice
- B. Standard acetylcysteine only, with no further action
- C. Immediate liver transplant listing
- D. Haemodialysis as the first-line therapy instead of acetylcysteine
- E. Wait for a repeat level at 8 hours to confirm absorption

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** A 40 g ingestion and a level above the high-risk line (300 mg/L at 4 h) define a high-risk ingestion; guidelines support increased NAC dosing (e.g. doubling the second bag in ANZ) with toxicology advice, and charcoal up to 4 h for ingestions ≥ 30 g in a cooperative patient. Early lactic acidosis reflects mitochondrial toxicity of very high concentrations.

**Why not the others:**
- B – standard dosing may be insufficient at very high concentrations.
- C – he has no liver failure; King's criteria apply to established ALF.
- D – ECTR is suggested only above ~ 700 mg/L with altered mental status or acidosis (without NAC) and never replaces NAC.
- E – he is already far above the line; delay is harmful.

**Ref:** Dart RC, JAMA Netw Open 2023; Chiew AL, Med J Aust 2020; Gosselin S, Clin Toxicol 2014.

</details>

**10.** Regarding paracetamol poisoning:

- A. A paracetamol concentration taken 2 hours after an acute ingestion can be plotted on the nomogram to decide on treatment. — T / F
- B. Fresh frozen plasma should be given routinely to correct the INR in paracetamol-induced acute liver failure. — T / F
- C. The 2023 US/Canada consensus recommends stopping acetylcysteine after exactly 21 hours regardless of laboratory results. — T / F

**Ref:** Dart RC, JAMA Netw Open 2023; Wendon J, J Hepatol 2017.

</details>

**11.** A 78-year-old woman taking regular high-dose aspirin for arthritis is admitted confused and tachypnoeic. pH 7.38, PaCO₂ 3.1 kPa (23 mmHg), HCO₃⁻ 13 mmol/L, anion gap 24 mmol/L, creatinine 190 µmol/L (baseline 80), salicylate 450 mg/L (3.3 mmol/L). What is the most appropriate management?

- A. Treat empirically for sepsis and recheck salicylate in 24 hours
- B. Urine alkalinisation alone, because the level is below 500 mg/L
- C. Intubate and ventilate to protect the airway
- D. Urgent haemodialysis in addition to supportive care
- E. Multiple-dose activated charcoal alone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Chronic salicylate toxicity causes serious toxicity at lower levels. EXTRIP recommends ECTR for altered mental status regardless of concentration, and AKI both limits salicylate excretion and makes alkalinisation hazardous; intermittent haemodialysis also corrects the acidosis.

**Why not the others:**
- A – chronic salicylism is often mistaken for sepsis; the diagnosis is already made.
- B – the level does not reflect tissue toxicity in chronic poisoning, and confusion is an indication for HD.
- C – intubation removes compensatory hyperventilation and is dangerous; it is not needed for airway protection here.
- E – charcoal has little role in chronic ingestion.

**Ref:** Juurlink DN, Ann Emerg Med 2015.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Describe the risk assessment and treatment of a patient who presents 6 hours after an acute paracetamol overdose.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:

- **Investigations:** paracetamol level (≥ 4 h), ALT, INR, creatinine, glucose, gas and lactate, salicylate level.

- A. In metformin poisoning, ECTR is continued until lactate is below 3 mmol/L and pH above 7.35. — T / F
- B. In valproate poisoning with hyperammonaemia, L-carnitine is given alongside ECTR. — T / F
- C. For atenolol poisoning, ECTR is suggested when kidney function is impaired. — T / F
- D. Continuous kidney replacement therapy is preferred over intermittent haemodialysis for lithium poisoning in a haemodynamically stable patient. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – stopping criteria in the metformin recommendation.
- B (True) – L-carnitine treats valproate-induced hyperammonaemia and hepatotoxicity.
- C (True) – with refractory bradycardia/hypotension (and recurrent torsade for sotalol).
- D (False) – intermittent HD is preferred; CKRT is an acceptable alternative when IHD is unavailable.

**14.** When deciding whether haemodialysis will enhance elimination of a poison, each of the following properties favours removal EXCEPT:

- A. Volume of distribution below 1 L/kg
- B. Protein binding below 80 %
- C. High endogenous (hepatic and renal) clearance
- D. Molecular weight below 500 Da
- E. Rapid equilibration between tissue and plasma (single-compartment kinetics)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** If the body already clears the poison efficiently, ECTR adds little to total clearance; ECTR is most useful when endogenous clearance is low (e.g. lithium in AKI, methanol under fomepizole).

**Why not the others:**
- A – a small Vd means most of the drug is in plasma, accessible to the filter.
- B – only unbound drug crosses the membrane.
- D – small molecules diffuse readily across dialysis membranes.
- E – rapid equilibration limits rebound and allows efficient removal.

**Ref:** Lavergne V, Clin Toxicol 2012; Ghannoum M, Kidney Int 2018. → [D5](D5.md)

**15.** Regarding extracorporeal treatment in poisoning:

- A. Antidotes such as fomepizole and acetylcysteine are removed by haemodialysis, requiring dose adjustment. — T / F
- B. Haemodialysis corrects the metabolic acidosis as well as removing metformin. — T / F
- C. Lithium concentrations may rebound after a session of intermittent haemodialysis. — T / F
- D. Most EXTRIP recommendations are based on very low quality evidence. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- D (True) – mostly 1D/2D grades based on case series and toxicokinetics.
