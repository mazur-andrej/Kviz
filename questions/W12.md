## W12 · D1 – COPD exacerbation and severe asthma in the ICU

**1.** A 68-year-old with a COPD exacerbation has pH 7.31, PaCO₂ 68 mmHg and RR 28/min despite bronchodilators and controlled oxygen. According to ERS/ATS 2017, the best next step is:

- A. High-flow nasal oxygen as first-line therapy
- B. Bilevel non-invasive ventilation
- C. Immediate intubation because pH is below 7.35
- D. CPAP 10 cmH₂O
- E. Increase FiO₂ to achieve SpO₂ ≥ 96 %

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Acute hypercapnic respiratory failure with pH ≤ 7.35 and PaCO₂ > 45 mmHg in a COPD exacerbation is the classic indication for bilevel NIV (ERS/ATS 2017: strong recommendation, high certainty). NIV unloads the respiratory muscles, lowers PaCO₂ and reduces intubation and mortality, as first shown by Brochard et al. (NEJM 1995).

**Why not the others:**

- A – ERS 2022 suggests a trial of NIV before HFNO in hypercapnic COPD; HFNO is a fallback when NIV is not tolerated.
- C – Intubation is reserved for NIV failure or contraindications (arrest, unprotected airway, haemodynamic instability); pH 7.31 alone is not an indication.
- D – CPAP gives no inspiratory pressure support, so it does not increase alveolar ventilation or correct hypercapnia.
- E – GOLD 2026 targets SpO₂ 88–92 %; hyperoxia worsens V/Q mismatch and PaCO₂ (Haldane effect).

**EDIC trap:** pH ≤ 7.35 is the NIV threshold; a lower pH (< 7.25) is not a contraindication but demands ICU-level monitoring with intubation immediately available.

**Ref:** Rochwerg B, et al. ERS/ATS NIV guidelines. *Eur Respir J* 2017;50:1602426; Oczkowski S, et al. ERS HFNC guidelines. *Eur Respir J* 2022;59:2101574; GOLD 2026.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the ERS/ATS 2017 NIV guideline:

- A. There is a strong recommendation to use NIV in acute asthma. — T / F
- B. NIV is recommended as a trial in COPD patients considered to need intubation, unless rapidly deteriorating. — T / F
- C. NIV is suggested against in COPD patients with hypercapnia but normal pH. — T / F
- D. NIV is suggested against for weaning hypercapnic patients from invasive ventilation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – Because of very uncertain evidence, ERS/ATS 2017 made **no recommendation** for or against NIV in acute asthma.
- B (True) – A trial of NIV is recommended in COPD patients considered to need intubation, unless they are rapidly deteriorating (strong, moderate certainty).
- C (True) – NIV is suggested **against** in hypercapnic COPD exacerbations without acidosis (conditional, low certainty), because no benefit has been shown.
- D (False) – NIV is suggested **to facilitate weaning** (early extubation to NIV) in hypercapnic patients (conditional, moderate certainty).

**EDIC trap:** NIV is suggested to prevent post-extubation failure in high-risk patients, but not to treat established post-extubation respiratory failure.

**Ref:** Rochwerg B, et al. ERS/ATS NIV guidelines. *Eur Respir J* 2017;50:1602426.

</details>

**3.** In the REDUCE trial in acute exacerbations of COPD, 5 days of prednisone 40 mg compared with 14 days:

- A. Increased the rate of re-exacerbation within 6 months
- B. Reduced mortality
- C. Required intravenous administration throughout
- D. Was only tested in ventilated patients
- E. Was non-inferior for re-exacerbation and halved the cumulative steroid dose

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** REDUCE randomised 314 patients presenting to the emergency department with a COPD exacerbation to prednisone 40 mg/day for 5 or 14 days. Re-exacerbation within 6 months was 35.9 % vs 36.8 %, meeting non-inferiority, while the cumulative steroid dose fell from 793 to 379 mg.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Re-exacerbation rates were virtually identical; the non-inferiority margin was met.
- B – Mortality did not differ and was not the primary end-point.
- C – Treatment was oral prednisone; oral steroids are as effective as IV in patients who can swallow.
- D – Patients were recruited in the emergency department and mostly treated on the ward, not ventilated in the ICU.

**EDIC trap:** GOLD 2026 keeps 40 mg prednisone-equivalent for 5 days; longer courses add side effects (hyperglycaemia, myopathy) without benefit.

**Ref:** Leuppi JD, et al. REDUCE. *JAMA* 2013;309:2223–2231; GOLD 2026.

</details>

**4.** Regarding dynamic hyperinflation in mechanically ventilated patients with severe airflow obstruction:

- A. Increasing the respiratory rate is an effective way to correct rising PaCO₂ in this setting. — T / F
- B. Intrinsic PEEP can be measured with an end-inspiratory occlusion in a passive patient. — T / F
- C. Expiratory flow returning to zero before the next breath suggests significant air trapping. — T / F
- D. Intrinsic PEEP imposes a threshold load that the patient must overcome to trigger the ventilator. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – A higher respiratory rate shortens expiratory time, increases trapped volume and intrinsic PEEP; rising PaCO₂ is managed with permissive hypercapnia.
- B (False) – Intrinsic PEEP is measured with an **end-expiratory** occlusion in a passive patient; an end-inspiratory occlusion gives plateau pressure.
- C (False) – Flow returning to zero means expiration is complete; air trapping is shown when expiratory flow does **not** return to zero before the next breath.
- D (True) – The patient must first generate a negative pressure equal to intrinsic PEEP before inspiratory flow starts, causing ineffective triggering and extra work of breathing.

**EDIC trap:** in spontaneously breathing COPD patients, external PEEP up to ~80 % of PEEPi reduces the trigger load; in passive asthmatics keep external PEEP low.

**Ref:** Leatherman J. Mechanical ventilation for severe asthma. *Chest* 2015;147:1671–1680; Rochwerg B, et al. *Eur Respir J* 2017.

</details>

**5.** Shortly after intubation, a 24-year-old with life-threatening asthma becomes profoundly hypotensive; peak pressure is high and breath sounds are bilaterally present but quiet. The most appropriate immediate action is:

- A. Increase PEEP to 15 cmH₂O
- B. Start adrenaline infusion before any other step
- C. Give a bolus of propofol
- D. Increase respiratory rate to 30/min
- E. Disconnect the patient from the ventilator briefly to allow full exhalation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Profound hypotension shortly after intubation, with high peak pressure and quiet bilateral breath sounds, is most likely dynamic hyperinflation: trapped gas and intrinsic PEEP raise intrathoracic pressure, cutting venous return and raising RV afterload. Disconnecting for 30–60 s lets the lungs empty and is both diagnostic (BP recovers) and therapeutic.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Extra PEEP further raises intrathoracic pressure and worsens hypotension.
- B – Adrenaline may be needed later, but it does not treat the cause; releasing trapped gas comes first.
- C – Propofol causes vasodilatation and myocardial depression and would deepen the hypotension.
- D – A higher rate shortens expiration and aggravates air trapping.

**EDIC trap:** if BP does not recover after disconnection, exclude tension pneumothorax (ultrasound, decompression) – it can coexist with hyperinflation.

**Ref:** Leatherman J. *Chest* 2015;147:1671–1680; GINA 2025.

</details>

**6.** According to the Rome proposal for COPD exacerbations:

- A. Symptoms worsen over ≤ 28 days. — T / F
- B. CRP ≥ 10 mg/L is one of the parameters used to define a moderate exacerbation. — T / F
- C. Severity is primarily defined by where the patient is treated (home, ward, ICU). — T / F
- D. A severe exacerbation includes hypercapnia with respiratory acidosis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – The Rome definition requires worsening dyspnoea and/or cough and sputum over **≤ 14 days**.
- B (True) – CRP ≥ 10 mg/L is one of five parameters (dyspnoea VAS ≥ 5, RR ≥ 24/min, HR ≥ 95/min, SpO₂ < 92 % or a fall > 3 %, CRP ≥ 10 mg/L); ≥ 3 define a moderate exacerbation.
- C (False) – Rome replaced the old grading by place of treatment (home, ward, ICU) with **objective, measurable** parameters.
- D (True) – A severe exacerbation has hypercapnia (PaCO₂ > 45 mmHg) **and** acidosis (pH < 7.35).

**EDIC trap:** only an arterial blood gas separates a moderate from a severe exacerbation; SpO₂ alone cannot.

**Ref:** Celli BR, et al. Rome proposal. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2021;204:1251–1258; GOLD 2026.

</details>

**7.** Which ventilator strategy is most appropriate for an intubated patient with status asthmaticus?

- A. VT 10 mL/kg, RR 20/min, I:E 1:1
- B. Target normal PaCO₂ at all costs
- C. Pressure control with inspiratory time 2 s and RR 25/min
- D. High PEEP of 15–20 cmH₂O to open airways
- E. VT 6–8 mL/kg PBW, low RR, high inspiratory flow, accept pH > 7.20

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** In status asthmaticus the goal is to minimise dynamic hyperinflation by reducing minute ventilation and lengthening expiratory time. VT 6–8 mL/kg PBW, a low rate (~10–14/min) and high inspiratory flow (60–100 L/min) give an I:E of 1:3–1:5; hypercapnia is accepted (pH > 7.20) as long as Pplat stays < 30 cmH₂O.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Large VT with I:E 1:1 maximises air trapping and barotrauma.
- B – Normalising PaCO₂ requires high minute ventilation, causing hyperinflation, hypotension and pneumothorax.
- C – A 2-s inspiratory time at 25/min leaves only ~0.4 s for expiration.
- D – High external PEEP in a passive asthmatic adds to end-expiratory lung volume; keep it low.

**EDIC trap:** minute ventilation, more than I:E ratio alone, determines trapped volume.

**Ref:** Leatherman J. Mechanical ventilation for severe asthma. *Chest* 2015;147:1671–1680; GINA 2025.

</details>

**8.** Regarding oxygen and high-flow therapy in hypercapnic COPD exacerbation:

- A. The target SpO₂ is 94–98 %. — T / F
- B. HFNC has been shown to be superior to NIV in reducing mortality in this population. — T / F
- C. ERS 2022 suggests a trial of HFNC before NIV. — T / F
- D. Excess oxygen does not affect PaCO₂ in hypercapnic COPD. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – GOLD 2026 targets SpO₂ **88–92 %** in hypercapnic COPD; 94–98 % applies to patients not at risk of hypercapnia.
- B (False) – Small RCTs suggest HFNC may lower PaCO₂ in mild–moderate acidosis, but neither equivalence nor superiority to NIV, nor any mortality benefit, has been shown.
- C (False) – ERS 2022 suggests a trial of **NIV before HFNC** (conditional, low certainty); HFNC is used if NIV is not tolerated.
- D (False) – Excess O₂ raises PaCO₂ by releasing hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (more V/Q mismatch and dead space), by the Haldane effect and, to a lesser degree, by reducing respiratory drive.

**EDIC trap:** a high SpO₂ on generous oxygen can hide worsening hypercapnia – check an ABG.

**Ref:** Oczkowski S, et al. ERS HFNC guidelines. *Eur Respir J* 2022;59:2101574; GOLD 2026.

</details>

**9.** Which finding in a patient with acute asthma is most concerning for impending respiratory failure?

- A. Expiratory wheeze throughout both lungs
- B. PaCO₂ 45 mmHg in a tachypnoeic, exhausted patient
- C. Serum potassium 3.4 mmol/L after salbutamol
- D. SpO₂ 94 % on 2 L/min oxygen
- E. Heart rate 110/min after salbutamol

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** In acute asthma, tachypnoea should drive PaCO₂ below normal; a "normal" PaCO₂ of ~45 mmHg in a tachypnoeic, exhausted patient means respiratory muscle fatigue and impending ventilatory failure. GINA and SIGN list normal or rising PaCO₂ as a life-threatening feature needing urgent ICU review.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Widespread wheeze shows that air is still moving; a silent chest is the ominous sign.
- C – Mild hypokalaemia is an expected β₂-agonist effect, not a marker of respiratory failure.
- D – SpO₂ 94 % on low-flow oxygen is within the GINA target of 93–95 %.
- E – Tachycardia of 110/min after salbutamol is expected and is at most a feature of severe asthma.

**EDIC trap:** hypercapnia is a late sign in asthma; "normal" PaCO₂ is a danger signal, not reassurance.

**Ref:** GINA 2025 report; SIGN 158 British guideline on the management of asthma, 2019.

</details>

**10.** In severe acute asthma (GINA 2025):

- A. Oxygen should be titrated to SpO₂ 93–95 % in adults. — T / F
- B. IV magnesium sulphate 2 g may be given for severe exacerbations not responding to initial treatment. — T / F
- C. Routine antibiotics are recommended. — T / F
- D. A "silent chest" is a sign of life-threatening asthma. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – GINA titrates oxygen to SpO₂ 93–95 % in adults (94–98 % in children 6–11 years); controlled oxygen gives better outcomes than high-concentration 100 % oxygen.
- B (True) – IV MgSO₄ 2 g over 20 min is not routine, but reduces admissions in adults with FEV₁ < 25–30 % predicted or failure to respond to initial treatment.
- C (False) – Most exacerbations are viral; antibiotics are not routinely recommended, only with strong evidence of bacterial infection (e.g. pneumonia).
- D (True) – A silent chest, like drowsiness, confusion or cyanosis, indicates life-threatening asthma and warrants ICU transfer.

**EDIC trap:** the GINA adult oxygen target (93–95 %) is lower than the older BTS target (94–98 %).

**Ref:** Global Initiative for Asthma. GINA 2025 report, ginasthma.org.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 70-year-old with COPD presents with pH 7.29, PaCO₂ 72 mmHg, RR 30/min and SpO₂ 97 % on 10 L/min via a face mask. Outline your initial management in the first 2 hours.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Look for mimics/aggravating conditions: PE, acute heart failure, pneumonia, pneumothorax.
- Controlled oxygen titrated down to SpO₂ 88–92 % (Venturi mask) – excess O₂ worsens V/Q and Haldane effect → ↑ PaCO₂.
- Short-acting bronchodilators (SABA ± SAMA); prednisone 40 mg/d for 5 days (REDUCE: non-inferior to 14 days, half the dose).
- Antibiotics (~5 days) – indicated when NIV/MV is needed or with purulent sputum plus another cardinal symptom; no methylxanthines.
- Bilevel NIV for pH ≤ 7.35 with hypercapnia (strong, high certainty): oronasal mask, IPAP ~10–12 titrated towards ~20 cmH₂O, EPAP ~4–6 cmH₂O.
- Check contraindications (arrest, unable to protect airway, non-cooperation, haemodynamic instability, excessive secretions, untreated pneumothorax).
- ABG after 1–2 h; failure to improve pH/PaCO₂ predicts failure – ICU monitoring with intubation immediately available. HFNO only if NIV is not tolerated.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain the physiology of dynamic hyperinflation and how it guides ventilator settings and the management of hypotension after intubation in status asthmaticus.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- ↑ airway resistance and expiratory flow limitation → incomplete expiration → air trapping → dynamic hyperinflation → intrinsic PEEP.
- Consequences: ↑ work of breathing, inspiratory threshold load, ↑ dead space, ↑ intrathoracic pressure → ↓ venous return, ↑ PVR → hypotension, even PEA.
- Settings: VT 6–8 mL/kg PBW, low RR 10–14/min, high inspiratory flow 60–100 L/min, I:E 1:3–1:5, low external PEEP in asthma, permissive hypercapnia (pH > 7.20), Pplat < 30 cmH₂O.
- Monitor auto-PEEP: end-expiratory occlusion in a passive patient; flow not returning to zero on the flow curve.
- High Ppeak with normal Pplat = ↑ resistance; both ↑ = ↓ compliance or hyperinflation.
- Hypotension after intubation: disconnect from the ventilator for 30–60 s, give fluids, exclude tension pneumothorax.
- Increasing RR for rising PaCO₂ worsens hyperinflation; ketamine is a useful bronchodilating sedative; ECCO₂R/VV-ECMO in refractory cases.

</details>


## W12 · D2 – Severe community-acquired pneumonia (sCAP)

**1.** Which of the following is a MAJOR criterion for severe community-acquired pneumonia according to ATS/IDSA 2019?

- A. Respiratory rate ≥ 30/min
- B. Multilobar infiltrates
- C. Confusion
- D. Septic shock requiring vasopressors
- E. PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤ 250

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** ATS/IDSA (2007 criteria, endorsed in 2019) define severe CAP by one of two major criteria – septic shock requiring vasopressors or respiratory failure requiring invasive ventilation – or by ≥ 3 minor criteria. Such patients need ICU care; the ERS/ESICM 2023 guideline uses the same definition.

**Why not the others:**

- A – RR ≥ 30/min is a minor criterion.
- B – Multilobar infiltrates are a minor criterion.
- C – Confusion/disorientation is a minor criterion.
- E – PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤ 250 is a minor criterion.

**EDIC trap:** the other minor criteria are BUN ≥ 20 mg/dL, WBC < 4 × 10⁹/L, platelets < 100 × 10⁹/L, T < 36 °C and hypotension requiring aggressive fluids; the P/F threshold is 250, not 200 or 300.

**Ref:** Metlay JP, et al. ATS/IDSA CAP guideline. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2019;200:e45–e67; Martin-Loeches I, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2023;49:615–632.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the CAPE COD trial:

- A. Patients with influenza were included and benefited most. — T / F
- B. Patients with septic shock requiring vasopressors at inclusion were excluded. — T / F
- C. Hydrocortisone 400 mg/day was used initially. — T / F
- D. 28-day mortality was approximately 6 % vs 12 %. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – Influenza pneumonia was an **exclusion** criterion, because corticosteroids are associated with harm in influenza.
- B (True) – Patients with septic shock requiring vasopressors at inclusion were excluded, so CAPE COD tested steroids in sCAP without shock.
- C (False) – Hydrocortisone was **200 mg/day** for 4 or 8 days (by clinical improvement), then tapered, for a total of 8 or 14 days.
- D (True) – 28-day mortality was 6.2 % vs 11.9 % (n = 800), with fewer intubations and fewer new vasopressor starts.

**EDIC trap:** CAPE COD excluded **both** septic shock and influenza – its results cannot be extrapolated to those groups.

**Ref:** Dequin PF, et al. CAPE COD. *N Engl J Med* 2023;388:1931–1941.

</details>

**3.** A 50-year-old returns from a hotel stay with high fever, diarrhoea, confusion, sodium 126 mmol/L and bilateral consolidation. The Legionella urinary antigen is negative. The most appropriate statement is:

- A. Legionella cannot cause severe pneumonia
- B. The urinary antigen detects mainly serogroup 1, so Legionella is not excluded
- C. Legionella is excluded; stop macrolide
- D. β-lactam monotherapy is sufficient for Legionella
- E. Hyponatraemia argues against Legionella

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** The Legionella urinary antigen detects mainly *L. pneumophila* serogroup 1, so its sensitivity is only ~70–80 % and a negative result does not exclude Legionella. With a typical picture (hotel water exposure, diarrhoea, confusion, hyponatraemia), send lower respiratory tract PCR/culture and continue a macrolide or fluoroquinolone.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Legionella is a classic cause of severe, ICU-requiring CAP.
- C – Stopping the macrolide would leave the most likely pathogen untreated.
- D – Legionella is intracellular; β-lactams do not reach it, so a macrolide or fluoroquinolone is needed.
- E – Hyponatraemia is a typical feature of Legionella, along with raised CK, deranged LFTs and diarrhoea.

**EDIC trap:** a negative urinary antigen never excludes Legionella; PCR detects all serogroups and species.

**Ref:** Metlay JP, et al. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2019;200:e45–e67; Martin-Loeches I, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2023;49:615–632.

</details>

**4.** According to the ERS/ESICM/ESCMID/ALAT 2023 sCAP guidelines:

- A. Procalcitonin is suggested to guide reduction of antibiotic duration. — T / F
- B. Fluoroquinolones are preferred over macrolides when added to a β-lactam. — T / F
- C. Empirical oseltamivir is suggested during influenza season when PCR is unavailable. — T / F
- D. High-flow nasal oxygen is suggested over conventional oxygen in hypoxaemic patients not requiring immediate intubation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

**EDIC trap:** ATS/IDSA 2019 accepts β-lactam plus macrolide **or** respiratory fluoroquinolone; ERS/ESICM 2023 prefers the macrolide.

**Ref:** Martin-Loeches I, et al. ERS/ESICM/ESCMID/ALAT sCAP guidelines. *Intensive Care Med* 2023;49:615–632.

</details>

**5.** The REMAP-CAP corticosteroid domain in severe CAP (2025) showed:

- A. Benefit restricted to influenza pneumonia
- B. Stopping for futility, with a large mortality reduction considered unlikely
- C. That fludrocortisone is required for benefit
- D. Clear mortality benefit from hydrocortisone in all subgroups
- E. Superiority of methylprednisolone over hydrocortisone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** In the REMAP-CAP corticosteroid domain (n = 658, randomised 536 hydrocortisone : 122 control), hydrocortisone 50 mg every 6 h for 7 days gave 90-day mortality of 15 % vs 9.8 %. The domain stopped for futility: a large mortality reduction was unlikely, but wide credible intervals did not exclude a small benefit or harm.

**Why not the others:**

- A – No benefit was shown in influenza; steroids are avoided in influenza pneumonia.
- C – Fludrocortisone was not part of the intervention; this belongs to septic-shock trials (APROCCHSS).
- D – There was no clear mortality benefit overall or in subgroups.
- E – Methylprednisolone was not compared with hydrocortisone in this domain.

**EDIC trap:** differences from CAPE COD (positive) include population (shock and influenza included), dose, timing and the small control group.

**Ref:** REMAP-CAP Investigators. *Intensive Care Med* 2025;51:665–680.

</details>

**6.** Regarding empirical antibiotic therapy for severe CAP:

- A. Anaerobic coverage is routinely required in suspected aspiration pneumonia without abscess or empyema. — T / F
- B. Prior isolation of MRSA or Pseudomonas in the respiratory tract is a relevant risk factor for resistant pathogens. — T / F
- C. ATS/IDSA 2019 recommends β-lactam monotherapy for severe CAP. — T / F
- D. Anti-MRSA therapy should be given to all patients admitted to ICU. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – In suspected aspiration without lung abscess or empyema, standard CAP therapy is used without specific anaerobic coverage (ERS/ESICM 2023 good practice statement; ATS/IDSA 2019).
- B (True) – Prior respiratory isolation of MRSA or *Pseudomonas*, or recent hospitalisation with IV antibiotics, is the strongest risk factor for resistant pathogens.
- C (False) – ATS/IDSA 2019 recommends **β-lactam plus macrolide** or **β-lactam plus respiratory fluoroquinolone** for severe CAP, not β-lactam monotherapy.
- D (False) – Anti-MRSA therapy is added only with individual risk factors (or necrotising, post-influenza pneumonia), not for every ICU admission.

**EDIC trap:** broad MRSA/*Pseudomonas* coverage "because it is ICU" is the classic wrong answer; de-escalate when cultures are negative.

**Ref:** Metlay JP, et al. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2019;200:e45–e67; Martin-Loeches I, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2023;49:615–632.

</details>

**7.** The 2025 ATS CAP guideline on corticosteroids states:

- A. Corticosteroids are strongly recommended for influenza pneumonia
- B. Corticosteroids are recommended for all hospitalised CAP
- C. Corticosteroids are conditionally recommended for severe CAP, but not for influenza pneumonia
- D. Corticosteroids should only be given with septic shock refractory to vasopressin
- E. No recommendation can be made

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The 2025 ATS CAP guideline suggests systemic corticosteroids in hospitalised adults with severe CAP (conditional, low quality of evidence), with the greatest expected benefit when started within 24 h in ICU patients with respiratory failure. The recommendation explicitly does not apply to influenza pneumonia.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Corticosteroids are not recommended in influenza pneumonia (observational data show higher mortality and superinfection).
- B – In non-severe CAP there is a strong recommendation against routine corticosteroids.
- D – The recommendation is not restricted to vasopressin-refractory shock; CAPE COD even excluded shock.
- E – A recommendation was made, informed mainly by CAPE COD and meta-analyses.

**EDIC trap:** "severe CAP – yes; influenza – no; non-severe CAP – no".

**Ref:** Jones BE, et al. ATS CAP guideline. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2025. doi:10.1164/rccm.202507-1692ST.

**8.** Regarding corticosteroid trials in severe CAP:

- A. ESCAPe used methylprednisolone 40 mg/day and found no significant difference in 60-day mortality. — T / F
- B. In CAPE COD, insulin requirements were higher with hydrocortisone. — T / F
- C. CAPE COD reduced the need for intubation among patients not intubated at baseline. — T / F
- D. REMAP-CAP used a Bayesian adaptive platform design. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

**EDIC trap:** an all-true K′ item is rare but possible – judge each statement on its own.

**9.** The main reason a macrolide (rather than β-lactam alone) is added in severe CAP is:

- A. Coverage of atypical pathogens such as Legionella and Mycoplasma, plus possible immunomodulation
- B. Anaerobic coverage
- C. Reduction of QT interval
- D. Activity against Pseudomonas
- E. Better activity against MRSA

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** *Mycoplasma*, *Chlamydophila* and *Legionella* lack a cell wall accessible to β-lactams, so a macrolide is added to cover atypical pathogens. Macrolides also have immunomodulatory effects (reduced neutrophil activation and cytokine release), which may explain the lower mortality seen with combination therapy in observational studies of severe CAP.

**Why not the others:**

- B – Macrolides do not add useful anaerobic coverage, and anaerobic coverage is not routinely needed.
- C – Macrolides **prolong** the QT interval – a side effect, not a benefit.

**5.** In a mechanically ventilated patient with a bronchopleural fistula and a large air leak, which ventilator change is most appropriate?

- D. Increase PEEP to 15 cmH₂O
- E. Reduce tidal volume, PEEP and inspiratory time, accepting hypercapnia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Flow through a bronchopleural fistula depends on the transpulmonary pressure across it and the time it is exposed to that pressure. Reducing tidal volume, PEEP, inspiratory time and rate lowers the leak, protects ventilation and promotes closure, at the cost of accepted hypercapnia.

**Why not the others:**

- C – An inspiratory pause prolongs the time at high pressure and increases the leak.

**EDIC trap:** declare "CICO" out loud – delay in starting front-of-neck access is the commonest fatal error.

**Ref:** Higgs A, et al. DAS/ICS/FICM/RCoA guidelines. *Br J Anaesth* 2018;120:323–352.

**7.** Which statement about tracheostomy timing is supported by the TracMan trial?

- A. Tracheostomy within 4 days reduces 30-day mortality
- B. All late-group patients eventually received tracheostomy
- C. Late tracheostomy increases 2-year mortality
- D. Early tracheostomy does not improve survival but reduces sedation exposure
- E. Clinicians accurately predict which patients will need prolonged ventilation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** In TracMan, tracheostomy within 4 days did not reduce 30-day mortality compared with tracheostomy at day 10 or later (30.8 % vs 31.5 %). Patients in the early group received fewer days of sedation, which is the main documented advantage.

**Why not the others:**

- A – 30-day mortality was not reduced.
- B – Fewer than half (~45 %) of the late group actually received a tracheostomy.
- C – 2-year mortality did not differ between groups.
- E – Clinicians were poor at predicting who would need prolonged ventilation, as most late-group patients never needed a tracheostomy.

**EDIC trap:** "early tracheostomy improves survival" is a common distractor – no large RCT has shown it.

**Ref:** Young D, et al. TracMan. *JAMA* 2013;309:2121–2129.

**8.** A patient 4 days after percutaneous tracheostomy develops sudden respiratory distress and the ventilator alarms for high pressure.

- A. If a suction catheter passes easily, the tube is likely patent. — T / F
- B. Forceful bag ventilation via the tube should be attempted before checking patency. — T / F
- C. High-flow oxygen should be applied to the face and the stoma. — T / F
- D. The inner cannula should be removed. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – If a suction catheter passes easily into the trachea, the tube is likely patent and in place; continue the ABCDE assessment for other causes.

**9.** A patient with a total laryngectomy is apnoeic. The correct route for oxygenation is:

- A. Laryngeal mask airway via the mouth
- B. Face mask over mouth and nose
- C. Via the neck stoma (e.g. paediatric face mask or tube in the stoma)
- D. Nasal high-flow
- E. Orotracheal intubation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** After a total laryngectomy the trachea ends in the neck stoma and is no longer connected to the mouth and nose. Oxygenation and ventilation are possible only via the stoma, using a paediatric face mask or LMA over the stoma, or a small cuffed tube inserted into it.

**Why not the others:**

- A – An LMA in the mouth sits above a blind-ending pharynx.
- B – A face mask over mouth and nose does not reach the lungs.
- D – Nasal high-flow delivers oxygen to the pharynx, not the trachea.
- E – Orotracheal intubation is anatomically impossible after laryngectomy.

- A. DNase alone is a suitable alternative to the combination. — T / F
- B. Intrapleural tPA 10 mg plus DNase 5 mg once daily for 7 days is the standard regimen. — T / F
- C. Surgical referral should be considered if sepsis persists despite drainage and intrapleural therapy. — T / F
- D. Intrapleural streptokinase is recommended. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**11.** A ventilated patient with ARDS has inspired VT 450 mL and expired VT 250 mL after a chest drain for pneumothorax, with continuous bubbling. Which intervention is LEAST appropriate?

- A. Reduce PEEP to the lowest acceptable level
- B. Accept permissive hypercapnia
- C. Increase chest drain suction to maximum
- D. Encourage spontaneous breathing modes when feasible
- E. Shorten inspiratory time

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The difference between inspired and expired VT (200 mL per breath) quantifies a large bronchopleural fistula. Maximal drain suction increases the pressure gradient across the fistula, increases leak flow, steals tidal volume and delays closure, so it is the least appropriate step; use the lowest effective suction or water seal.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Lowering PEEP reduces mean airway pressure and the leak, so it is appropriate.
- B – Permissive hypercapnia allows lower tidal volumes and pressures, so it is appropriate.
- D – Spontaneous breathing lowers airway pressures and fistula flow, so it is appropriate.
- E – A shorter inspiratory time reduces the time the fistula is exposed to high pressure, so it is appropriate.

**EDIC trap:** in "LEAST appropriate" questions, four options are correct management – pick the one that increases the leak.

**Ref:** Lois M, Noppen M. Bronchopleural fistulas: an overview. *Chest* 2005;128:3955–3965.

**12.** A patient with cavitary tuberculosis sequelae has massive haemoptysis from the left lung and is deteriorating.

- A. Death is usually due to exsanguination rather than asphyxia. — T / F
- B. Bronchial artery embolisation is the preferred definitive treatment. — T / F
- C. Blind advancement of a single-lumen tube usually enters the right main bronchus, isolating the healthy lung. — T / F
- D. Position the patient left side down. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – Death in massive haemoptysis is usually from **asphyxia**; a few hundred millilitres of blood can flood the airways long before exsanguination.
- B (True) – Bronchial artery embolisation is the preferred definitive treatment; surgery is reserved for failure or specific lesions.

**15.** A 40-year-old with an ICU intubation planned has Mallampati III, limited mouth opening (2.5 cm) and GCS 7; the operator is an anaesthetist and SpO₂ is 95 %. Which is correct?

- A. MACOCHA 12 – maximum risk
- B. MACOCHA 3 – low risk
- C. MACOCHA does not apply to ICU patients
- D. MACOCHA 7 – high risk of difficult intubation
- E. MACOCHA 2 – low risk

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** MACOCHA points: Mallampati III/IV = 5, mouth opening < 3 cm = 1, coma = 1, giving 7. OSA (2), reduced cervical mobility (1), SpO₂ < 80 % (1) and a non-anaesthetist operator (1) are absent here. A score ≥ 3 predicts difficult intubation.

**Why not the others:**

- A – 12 is the maximum score, which requires all seven items.
- B – 3 omits the 5 points for Mallampati III.
- C – MACOCHA was developed and validated specifically in ICU patients.
- E – 2 counts only the mouth opening and coma items.

**EDIC trap:** Mallampati alone gives 5 points – any patient with Mallampati III/IV is already high risk.

**Ref:** De Jong A, et al. MACOCHA score. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2013;187:832–839.
