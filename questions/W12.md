## W12 · D1 – COPD exacerbation and severe asthma in the ICU

**1.** A 68-year-old with a COPD exacerbation has pH 7.31, PaCO₂ 68 mmHg and RR 28/min despite bronchodilators and controlled oxygen. According to ERS/ATS 2017, the best next step is:

- A. High-flow nasal oxygen as first-line therapy
- B. Bilevel non-invasive ventilation
- C. Immediate intubation because pH is below 7.35
- D. CPAP 10 cmH₂O
- E. Increase FiO₂ to achieve SpO₂ ≥ 96 %

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** pH ≤ 7.35 with hypercapnia → bilevel NIV (strong, high certainty). HFNO only after a trial of NIV; hyperoxia worsens hypercapnia.

</details>

- D. NIV is suggested against for weaning hypercapnic patients from invasive ventilation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.** A: no recommendation in asthma. D: NIV is suggested to facilitate weaning in hypercapnic patients.

</details>

- A. Increased the rate of re-exacerbation within 6 months
- B. Reduced mortality
- C. Required intravenous administration throughout
- D. Was only tested in ventilated patients
- E. Was non-inferior for re-exacerbation and halved the cumulative steroid dose

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

</details>

**4.** Regarding dynamic hyperinflation in mechanically ventilated patients with severe airflow obstruction:

- A. Increasing the respiratory rate is an effective way to correct rising PaCO₂ in this setting. — T / F
- B. Intrinsic PEEP can be measured with an end-inspiratory occlusion in a passive patient. — T / F

**O1. (open)** A 70-year-old with COPD presents with pH 7.29, PaCO₂ 72 mmHg, RR 30/min and SpO₂ 97 % on 10 L/min via a face mask. Outline your initial management in the first 2 hours.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Look for mimics/aggravating conditions: PE, acute heart failure, pneumonia, pneumothorax.
- Controlled oxygen titrated down to SpO₂ 88–92 % (Venturi mask) – excess O₂ worsens V/Q and Haldane effect → ↑ PaCO₂.
- Short-acting bronchodilators (SABA ± SAMA); prednisone 40 mg/d for 5 days (REDUCE: non-inferior to 14 days, half the dose).
- Antibiotics (~5 days) – indicated when NIV/MV is needed or with purulent sputum plus another cardinal symptom; no methylxanthines.
- Bilevel NIV for pH ≤ 7.35 with hypercapnia (strong, high certainty): oronasal mask, IPAP ~10–12 titrated towards ~20 cmH₂O, EPAP ~4–6 cmH₂O.
- Check contraindications (arrest, unable to protect airway, non-cooperation, haemodynamic instability, excessive secretions, untreated pneumothorax).
- ABG after 1–2 h; failure to improve pH/PaCO₂ predicts failure – ICU monitoring with intubation immediately available. HFNO only if NIV is not tolerated.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain the physiology of dynamic hyperinflation and how it guides ventilator settings and the management of hypotension after intubation in status asthmaticus.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- ↑ airway resistance and expiratory flow limitation → incomplete expiration → air trapping → dynamic hyperinflation → intrinsic PEEP.
- Consequences: ↑ work of breathing, inspiratory threshold load, ↑ dead space, ↑ intrathoracic pressure → ↓ venous return, ↑ PVR → hypotension, even PEA.
- Settings: VT 6–8 mL/kg PBW, low RR 10–14/min, high inspiratory flow 60–100 L/min, I:E 1:3–1:5, low external PEEP in asthma, permissive hypercapnia (pH > 7.20), Pplat < 30 cmH₂O.
- Monitor auto-PEEP: end-expiratory occlusion in a passive patient; flow not returning to zero on the flow curve.
- High Ppeak with normal Pplat = ↑ resistance; both ↑ = ↓ compliance or hyperinflation.
- Hypotension after intubation: disconnect from the ventilator for 30–60 s, give fluids, exclude tension pneumothorax.
- Increasing RR for rising PaCO₂ worsens hyperinflation; ketamine is a useful bronchodilating sedative; ECCO₂R/VV-ECMO in refractory cases.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 62-year-old is admitted to the ICU with severe CAP (no MRSA/Pseudomonas risk factors), P/F 180 on HFNO, no vasopressors, influenza PCR negative. Outline your diagnostic and therapeutic plan.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Severity: ATS/IDSA criteria (1 major or ≥ 3 minor); ICU admission without delay.
- Microbiology: blood cultures and sputum Gram stain/culture before antibiotics; pneumococcal and Legionella urinary antigens (remember Legionella antigen detects only serogroup 1); influenza PCR in season.
- Empirical β-lactam + macrolide (ERS/ESICM prefer macrolide over fluoroquinolone; ATS/IDSA allows either); no routine MRSA/Pseudomonas or anaerobic coverage.
- HFNO over standard oxygen; NIV only in selected patients given the risk of delayed intubation.
- Consider hydrocortisone (CAPE COD: 200 mg/d for 4 days then taper to 8 or 14 days; 28-day mortality 6.2 vs 11.9 %) – patient resembles the CAPE COD population (ICU, P/F < 300, no shock, no influenza); ATS 2025 conditional recommendation, best within 24 h; monitor glucose/insulin requirements.
- Use procalcitonin to shorten (not start) antibiotics; treat for at least 5 days and until clinical stability.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why CAPE COD, ESCAPe and REMAP-CAP reached different conclusions about corticosteroids in severe CAP.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- CAPE COD (n = 800): hydrocortisone 200 mg/d → 28-day mortality 6.2 vs 11.9 % (p = 0.006), fewer intubations and vasopressor initiations.
- ESCAPe (n = 586): methylprednisolone 40 mg/d for 7 days → 60-day mortality 16 vs 18 %, NS; did not reach planned enrolment.
- REMAP-CAP (n = 658): hydrocortisone 50 mg every 6 h for 7 days → 90-day mortality 15 vs 9.8 %, stopped for futility; small benefit or harm not excluded.
- Differences in corticosteroid type and dose, and timing (CAPE COD within 24 h of meeting criteria).
- Population: CAPE COD excluded septic shock and influenza; REMAP-CAP included both.
- Low mortality in CAPE COD; in REMAP-CAP unequal randomisation (536:122) and a small control group gave wide intervals.
- Resulting guidance: ATS 2025 conditional recommendation for severe CAP but not influenza; ERS/ESICM 2023 (pre-CAPE COD) only with shock.

</details>

- Laryngectomy: mouth/nose are not connected to the lungs – oxygenate and intubate only via the stoma; never attempt orotracheal intubation.

</details>

**O2. (open)** A colleague wants to perform a tracheostomy on day 3 in a ventilated patient "to improve survival". Discuss the evidence on timing.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- TracMan (909 patients, 70 UK ICUs): early (≤ 4 days) vs late (≥ 10 days) – 30-day mortality 30.8 vs 31.5 %, no difference; no difference at 2 years.
