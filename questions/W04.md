## W04 · D1 – Physiology of shock: DO2/VO2, O2 extraction, types of shock

**1.** According to the 2014 ESICM consensus on circulatory shock, which statement is correct?

- A. Shock requires a systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg
- B. Shock is defined by a lactate > 4 mmol/L
- C. Hypotension is not required to define shock
- D. Shock is defined by a cardiac index < 2.2 L/min/m²
- E. Shock is present only when vasopressors are required

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** Shock = generalised circulatory failure with inadequate O2 utilisation by cells; hypotension is not required (Level 1, B).

</details>

**2.** Regarding oxygen delivery and consumption:

- A. Arterial oxygen content is determined mainly by haemoglobin concentration and SaO2. — T / F
- B. When DO2 falls above the critical DO2, VO2 falls in proportion to DO2. — T / F
- C. Below the critical DO2, VO2 remains independent of DO2. — T / F
- D. Dissolved oxygen normally contributes about half of arterial oxygen content. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.** B: above the critical DO2, VO2 is maintained by increased O2 extraction. C: below it, VO2 becomes supply-dependent and lactate rises. D: dissolved O2 is quantitatively negligible.

</details>

- D. Obstructive

**C.** Septic 62%, cardiogenic 16%, hypovolaemic 16%, other distributive 4%, obstructive 2%.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Explain the relationship between DO2 and VO2 as oxygen delivery falls, and why a normal or high ScvO2 does not exclude tissue hypoxia in septic shock.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- DO2 = CO × CaO2; CaO2 ≈ 1.34 × Hb × SaO2 (+ negligible dissolved O2); main adjustable determinant is CO.
- Initially, falling DO2 is compensated by increased extraction → VO2 stays constant (plateau), SvO2 falls.
- Below DO2crit, extraction is exhausted → VO2 becomes supply-dependent → anaerobic metabolism → lactate rises.
- SvO2 reflects the DO2/VO2 balance: falls with ↓ CO, ↓ Hb, ↓ SaO2, ↑ VO2 (fever, shivering, pain).
- In sepsis extraction is impaired (microcirculatory shunting, mitochondrial dysfunction) → dysoxia with normal/high SvO2.
- Therefore no single parameter suffices – combine lactate (repeated), ScvO2, P(cv-a)CO2 and clinical windows (skin, kidneys, brain).

</details>

**O2. (open)** Outline how you would identify the type of shock in a hypotensive patient whose clinical examination is inconclusive, and the initial treatment principles according to ESICM 2014.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Hypotension is not required; look for hypoperfusion (three windows) and measure lactate (and repeat).
- Echocardiography as preferred modality to determine the type of shock (Level 2, B); distinguishes cardiogenic vs obstructive vs hypovolaemic vs distributive.
- Arterial and central venous catheter if not responding or on vasopressors; ScvO2 and P(cv-a)CO2 to assess type and adequacy of CO.
- No routine PAC (Level 1, A); PAC/TPTD in complex cases or severe shock with ARDS.
- VIP: ventilate, infuse (fluid challenge guided by dynamic predictors, not CVP alone), pump (noradrenaline first line; dobutamine if low CO with persistent hypoperfusion).
- Initial MAP ≥ 65 mmHg, then individualise (lower in uncontrolled haemorrhage without TBI, higher with chronic hypertension).
- No absolute/supranormal DO2 target (Gattinoni 1995: no benefit).

</details>


## W04 · D2 – Fluid responsiveness: PLR, PPV/SVV, EEO, mini-fluid challenge

**1.** A sedated patient with septic shock is ventilated in volume control with a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg PBW, in sinus rhythm. Pulse pressure variation is 5 %. What is the best interpretation?

- A. A fluid bolus of 2 L should be given

- C. PPV is reliable because the patient is in sinus rhythm
- D. The patient is definitely not fluid responsive
- E. PPV is falsely positive because of low tidal volume

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.** At VT < 8 mL/kg PPV is unreliable (De Backer 2005) – falsely negative; a TV challenge (6 → 8 mL/kg), PLR or EEO helps.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A spontaneously breathing, non-intubated patient in atrial fibrillation has septic shock and persistent hypoperfusion after initial fluids. Explain which tests of fluid responsiveness are valid in this patient and which are not, and how you would perform the most suitable one.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- PPV/SVV invalid: spontaneous breathing and irregular rhythm (LIMITS); IVC variability unreliable.
- EEO requires intubation and a 15 s ventilatory pause – not applicable.
- PLR is suitable: works with spontaneous breathing and arrhythmias.
- Technique: start semi-recumbent, move trunk to horizontal and raise legs to 30–45°; measure CO/SV in real time (echo VTI, pulse contour, oesophageal Doppler) within ~1 min.
- Positive if CO rises ≥ 10%; BP alone is not sufficient (PP sensitivity 0.56 vs CO 0.85).
- Check for limitations: IAH (false negative), intracranial hypertension (contraindication).
- Mini-fluid challenge (100–150 mL over 1–2 min, ~5%) is an alternative but needs very precise CO measurement and the fluid cannot be withdrawn.
- A positive test supports fluid only if hypoperfusion persists and the risk is acceptable.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why pulse pressure variation fails during lung-protective ventilation at 6 mL/kg, and how the tidal volume challenge addresses this.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- PPV relies on cyclic changes in intrathoracic pressure reducing RV preload and, after pulmonary transit, LV SV during positive-pressure breaths.
- With low VT the intrathoracic pressure swing is small → small SV variation even in preload-dependent patients → false negatives.
- De Backer 2005: PPV threshold 12% correctly classified only 51% at VT < 8 mL/kg vs 88% at ≥ 8 mL/kg.
- Low compliance (ARDS) further reduces pressure transmission.
- Tidal volume challenge: transiently increase VT from 6 to 8 mL/kg PBW for ~1 min and observe the absolute change in PPV.
- A significant absolute rise in PPV (published thresholds ~1–3.5%) indicates preload dependence.
- Still requires controlled ventilation and sinus rhythm; alternatives are PLR or EEO.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Explain why the PAC-Man, Sandham and ESCAPE trials found no benefit of the pulmonary artery catheter, and outline the situations in which a PAC is still appropriate according to ESICM 2014.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- A PAC is a monitor, not a treatment; benefit requires an effective treatment protocol that follows from the data.

**O2. (open)** A patient with septic shock and ARDS has TPTD monitoring: EVLWI 15 mL/kg, PVPI 4.0, GEDVI normal, and a positive PLR. Interpret these values and explain how they inform fluid management.

- Rivers 2001: single centre, 263 ED patients, 6 h protocol (CVP 8–12 → MAP ≥ 65 → ScvO2 ≥ 70% via transfusion to Hct ≥ 30% and dobutamine); mortality 30.5 vs 46.5%.

- PRISM (3723 pts): 24.9 vs 25.4%, aOR 0.97; longer ICU stay, more CV support, higher costs; no subgroup benefited.
- Explanations: lower baseline mortality as "usual care" improved (early recognition, antibiotics, fluids), single-centre vs multicentre design, population differences.
- The core elements (early recognition, antibiotics, fluids) became standard; the rigid CVP/ScvO2/Hct protocol adds nothing.
- Modern approach: individualised targets, dynamic fluid responsiveness, lactate and CRT as perfusion guides (SSC 2021).

</details>

**O2. (open)** A patient with septic shock has MAP 68 mmHg on noradrenaline, ScvO2 75%, P(cv-a)CO2 8 mmHg, lactate 4.2 mmol/L and CRT 5 s. Interpret these findings and outline your next steps.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- ScvO2 ≥ 70% does not exclude hypoperfusion – impaired extraction/microcirculatory shunting in sepsis.
- P(cv-a)CO2 ≥ 6 mmHg indicates insufficient flow; a persistently high gap is associated with worse SOFA and 28-d mortality (Ospina-Tascón), even with ScvO2 ≥ 70%.
- CRT > 3 s = abnormal peripheral perfusion; responds within minutes (useful for titration), unlike lactate (hours).
- Lactate may be type A and/or B (adrenergic, reduced clearance) – trend it (≥ 20% fall per 2 h target, Jansen).
- Next: assess fluid responsiveness (PLR/EEO) before giving fluid; echocardiography for cardiac dysfunction; consider dobutamine if low CO; consider higher MAP (80–85) trial if chronic hypertension (ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 tiered approach).
- Evidence: ANDROMEDA-SHOCK (34.9 vs 43.4%, p = 0.06, ↓ SOFA, Bayesian > 90%); ANDROMEDA-SHOCK-2 win ratio 1.16, mortality unchanged.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A ventilated patient with pneumonia-related ARDS (driving pressure 20 cmH2O, PaCO2 55 mmHg) becomes hypotensive. Describe the echocardiographic findings that would suggest acute cor pulmonale and outline your management.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- ACP = RV dilatation (RV/LV end-diastolic area ratio > 0.6; ≥ 1 severe) + septal dyskinesia/paradoxical motion → D-shaped LV in short axis.
- Dilated, non-collapsing IVC with little respiratory variation; RV free wall thickness to distinguish acute from chronic overload.
- This patient has several risk factors (Mekontso Dessap): pneumonia, driving pressure ≥ 18 cmH2O, PaCO2 ≥ 48 mmHg (± PaO2/FiO2 < 150); prevalence 22%, severe ACP mortality 57 vs 42%.
- Avoid fluid boluses – potentially harmful (further RV dilatation, septal shift, ↓ LV filling).
- Noradrenaline to restore systemic pressure and prevent RV ischaemia; ± inotropes/inodilators.
- RV-protective ventilation: limit driving pressure and hypercapnia; consider prone positioning (protective).
- Exclude PE; mechanical circulatory support in the most severe cases.

**O2. (open)** Explain why IVC ultrasound is unreliable for predicting fluid responsiveness in the ICU, and what information the IVC can still provide.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Meta-analyses show low sensitivity and specificity of respiratory IVC variation; the result also depends on the measurement site.
- Ventilatory factors: high PEEP/low VT, assisted modes/NIV/CPAP, variable spontaneous breathing (false positives/negatives), hyperinflation/auto-PEEP.
- Cardiac factors: chronic RV dysfunction, severe TR, RV infarction, tamponade.
- Abdominal/local factors: intra-abdominal hypertension, IVC stenosis/thrombosis/filter/ECMO cannulae, lateral displacement during inspiration.
- ESICM 2021: strong recommendation against basic ultrasound to determine fluid responsiveness in persistent shock without signs of hypovolaemia.
- What it still shows: small collapsing IVC supports severe hypovolaemia; non-dilated IVC generally excludes tamponade; dilated non-collapsing IVC fits RV failure/tamponade.
- Prefer dynamic tests with real-time SV (e.g. PLR with VTI ≥ 10%).

</details>
