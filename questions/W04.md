## W04 · D1 – Physiology of shock: DO2/VO2, O2 extraction, types of shock

**1.** According to the 2014 ESICM consensus on circulatory shock, which statement is correct?

- A. Shock requires a systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg
- B. Shock is defined by a lactate > 4 mmol/L
- C. Hypotension is not required to define shock
- D. Shock is defined by a cardiac index < 2.2 L/min/m²
- E. Shock is present only when vasopressors are required

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The 2014 ESICM consensus defines shock as a life-threatening, generalised acute circulatory failure with **inadequate oxygen utilisation by the cells** – so **hypotension is not required** (Level 1, B). Hypoperfusion is recognised from the clinical windows (skin, kidneys, brain) and a lactate typically > 2 mmol/L.

**Why not the others:**

- A – SBP < 90 mmHg (or MAP < 65 mmHg, or a fall ≥ 40 mmHg) defines hypotension when present, not shock itself.
- B – Lactate in shock is typically > 2 mmol/L; 4 mmol/L is not a defining threshold.
- D – A low cardiac index describes low-flow states only; distributive shock often has a normal or high CO.
- E – Vasopressor need belongs to the Sepsis-3 definition of septic shock, not to the general definition of shock.

**EDIC trap:** "Normotensive shock" exists – a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg does not exclude shock.

**Ref:** Cecconi M, et al. Consensus on circulatory shock and hemodynamic monitoring. Task force of the ESICM. *Intensive Care Med* 2014;40:1795–1815.

</details>

**2.** Regarding oxygen delivery and consumption:

- A. Arterial oxygen content is determined mainly by haemoglobin concentration and SaO2. — T / F
- B. When DO2 falls above the critical DO2, VO2 falls in proportion to DO2. — T / F
- C. Below the critical DO2, VO2 remains independent of DO2. — T / F
- D. Dissolved oxygen normally contributes about half of arterial oxygen content. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – CaO2 = 1.34 × Hb × SaO2 + 0.003 × PaO2; the haemoglobin-bound fraction makes up almost all of it.
- B (False) – Above the critical DO2, VO2 stays constant (plateau) because O2 extraction rises; it is SvO2 that falls, not VO2.
- C (False) – Below the critical DO2 extraction is exhausted, so VO2 becomes supply-dependent and falls with DO2 → anaerobic metabolism, rising lactate.
- D (False) – Dissolved O2 (0.003 mL/dL per mmHg) is only ~1–2 % of CaO2 at normal PaO2; it matters only with hyperbaric oxygen.

**EDIC trap:** VO2 is supply-independent on the plateau; supply dependency appears only below DO2crit.

**Ref:** Vincent JL, De Backer D. Circulatory shock. *N Engl J Med* 2013;369:1726–1734.

</details>

**3.** In the large cohort of shock patients cited by Vincent & De Backer (NEJM 2013), the most frequent type of shock was:

- A. Anaphylactic
- B. Hypovolaemic
- C. Septic
- D. Obstructive
- E. Cardiogenic

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In the > 1600-patient RCT (De Backer, SOAP II) cited by Vincent & De Backer, **septic shock** was by far the most common type (62 %), followed by cardiogenic (16 %) and hypovolaemic (16 %), other distributive (4 %) and obstructive (2 %). Distributive shock overall therefore accounts for about two-thirds of cases.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Anaphylaxis belongs to the "other distributive" group, only 4 %.
- B – Hypovolaemic shock was 16 %, equal to cardiogenic and far behind septic.
- D – Obstructive shock was the rarest (2 %), although it must be actively excluded because it is rapidly reversible.
- E – Cardiogenic shock was 16 %.

**Ref:** Vincent JL, De Backer D. Circulatory shock. *N Engl J Med* 2013;369:1726–1734. De Backer D, et al. SOAP II. *N Engl J Med* 2010;362:779–789.

</details>

**4.** Regarding mixed/central venous oxygen saturation in shock:

- A. A normal ScvO2 does not exclude tissue hypoxia in septic shock. — T / F
- B. It is typically normal or high in distributive shock. — T / F
- C. It is typically low in hypovolaemic and cardiogenic shock. — T / F
- D. Fever and shivering may decrease SvO2 by increasing VO2. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – In sepsis microcirculatory shunting and mitochondrial dysfunction impair O2 extraction, so dysoxia can coexist with a normal or high ScvO2.
- B (True) – Distributive shock combines high flow with impaired extraction → SvO2 normal or high.
- C (True) – In low-flow states tissues increase extraction to maintain VO2 → wide arteriovenous difference and low SvO2 (also with anaemia).
- D (True) – SvO2 reflects the DO2/VO2 balance; a higher VO2 (fever, shivering, pain, agitation) lowers SvO2 at any given DO2.

**EDIC trap:** A low ScvO2 almost always means DO2 is insufficient; a normal one does not prove adequate tissue oxygenation.

**Ref:** Vincent JL, De Backer D. Circulatory shock. *N Engl J Med* 2013;369:1726–1734. Cecconi M, et al. Consensus on circulatory shock and hemodynamic monitoring. Task force of the ESICM. *Intensive Care Med* 2014;40:1795–1815.

</details>

**5.** A 70-year-old has hypotension, distended neck veins, a low cardiac output and elevated central venous pressure. Clinical examination does not clarify the cause. According to the ESICM consensus, the preferred next step to determine the type of shock is:

- A. Pulmonary artery catheter insertion
- B. Echocardiography
- C. Transpulmonary thermodilution
- D. Repeated lactate measurement alone
- E. CT angiography of the chest in all cases

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** When clinical examination does not identify the type of shock, ESICM recommends **echocardiography as the preferred modality** (Level 2, B) rather than invasive monitoring. With a high CVP and low CO it rapidly separates cardiogenic shock (poor LV/RV function) from obstructive shock (tamponade, acute cor pulmonale from PE), and it can be repeated at the bedside.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Routine PAC is recommended against (Level 1, A); it is reserved for refractory shock with RV dysfunction or complex cases.
- C – TPTD gives CO, GEDV and EVLW but cannot separate RV from LV failure or show the pericardium.
- D – Lactate confirms hypoperfusion and tracks its course but does not identify the type of shock.
- E – CT may be needed if PE is suspected, but not in all cases and not before bedside echo in an unstable patient.

**Ref:** Cecconi M, et al. Consensus on circulatory shock and hemodynamic monitoring. Task force of the ESICM. *Intensive Care Med* 2014;40:1795–1815.

</details>

**6.** Which statements reflect the 2014 ESICM consensus?

- A. Routine use of a pulmonary artery catheter is recommended. — T / F
- B. An absolute target for oxygen delivery should be used in all shock patients. — T / F
- C. Lactate should be measured in all patients with suspected shock and repeated. — T / F
- D. Static measures such as CVP alone should not be used to guide fluid resuscitation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – Routine PAC is recommended against (Level 1, A); PAC or TPTD is suggested only in severe or complex shock, especially with ARDS (Level 2, C).
- B (False) – ESICM recommends against an absolute DO2 target (Level 1, A), after trials of supranormal DO2 such as Gattinoni 1995 showed no benefit.
- C (True) – Measure lactate whenever shock is suspected (Level 1, C) and repeat it (Level 1, C).
- D (True) – Static measures – CVP, PAOP, GEDV – should not be used alone to guide fluids (Level 1, B); use a fluid challenge and dynamic predictors.

**EDIC trap:** The three Level 1 "against" statements – routine PAC, absolute DO2 target, static preload alone – are exam favourites.

**Ref:** Cecconi M, et al. Consensus on circulatory shock and hemodynamic monitoring. Task force of the ESICM. *Intensive Care Med* 2014;40:1795–1815.

</details>

**7.** The 1995 SvO2 Collaborative Group trial (Gattinoni, NEJM) showed that targeting a supranormal cardiac index or a normal SvO2 in critically ill patients:

- A. Reduced ICU mortality by about 10 %
- B. Did not reduce morbidity or mortality
- C. Reduced 6-month mortality only in septic patients
- D. Was achieved in nearly all patients of the cardiac index group
- E. Increased mortality significantly in the SvO2 group

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Gattinoni et al. randomised **762 critically ill patients in 56 ICUs** to a normal cardiac index (control), a supranormal cardiac index or a normal SvO2 (≥ 70 %). Mortality to ICU discharge was **48.4 % vs 48.6 % vs 52.1 %** and at 6 months 62.3 % vs 61.7 % vs 63.8 % – all non-significant, with no reduction in organ dysfunction.

**Why not the others:**

- A – No arm reduced ICU mortality.
- C – There was no 6-month mortality benefit, overall or in a septic subgroup.
- D – The supranormal CI target was achieved in only 44.9 % of patients.
- E – Mortality in the SvO2 arm (52.1 %) was not significantly higher.

**EDIC trap:** "Raise DO2 to supranormal values" is wrong; the goal is adequate DO2 relative to demand.

**Ref:** Gattinoni L, et al.; SvO2 Collaborative Group. *N Engl J Med* 1995;333:1025–1032. Cecconi M, et al. Consensus on circulatory shock and hemodynamic monitoring. Task force of the ESICM. *Intensive Care Med* 2014;40:1795–1815.

</details>

**8.** The "three windows" of tissue hypoperfusion include:

- A. Altered mental status — T / F
- B. Elevated serum troponin — T / F
- C. Mottled, cold skin — T / F
- D. Hyperglycaemia — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – The brain window: confusion, agitation or obtundation.
- B (False) – Troponin reflects myocardial injury and may point to a cardiogenic cause, but it is not a bedside window of tissue hypoperfusion.
- C (True) – The skin window: cold, clammy, mottled skin with prolonged capillary refill from vasoconstriction.
- D (False) – Stress hyperglycaemia is common but nonspecific; the third window is the kidney – urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h.

**Ref:** Vincent JL, De Backer D. Circulatory shock. *N Engl J Med* 2013;369:1726–1734.

**9.** According to the ESICM consensus, inotropic agents in shock should be added when:

- A. Ejection fraction is reduced, regardless of perfusion
- B. Cardiac output is low and hypoperfusion persists despite adequate preload
- C. ScvO2 exceeds 80 %
- D. The MAP target of 65 mmHg is not achieved with noradrenaline
- E. Lactate is below 2 mmol/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** ESICM suggests adding an inotrope (dobutamine first choice) when **cardiac output is low and hypoperfusion persists despite adequate preload** (Level 2, C). The trigger is the combination of low flow and markers of hypoperfusion (lactate, ScvO2, P(cv-a)CO2, skin), and dobutamine is titrated to perfusion, not to a fixed CI.

**Why not the others:**

- A – Inotropes are recommended against for isolated cardiac dysfunction without hypoperfusion (Level 1, B).
- C – A high ScvO2 does not suggest low output; a low ScvO2 would support adding an inotrope.
- D – Failure to reach the MAP target calls for vasopressor escalation (e.g. adding vasopressin), not an inotrope.
- E – A normal lactate argues against hypoperfusion needing inotropic support.

**EDIC trap:** "Low ejection fraction → dobutamine" is wrong unless hypoperfusion persists.

**Ref:** Cecconi M, et al. Consensus on circulatory shock and hemodynamic monitoring. Task force of the ESICM. *Intensive Care Med* 2014;40:1795–1815.

</details>

- A. Lower blood pressure may be tolerated in uncontrolled haemorrhage with severe traumatic brain injury. — T / F
- B. The MAP target should be individualised. — T / F
- C. A higher MAP may be considered in septic shock with chronic hypertension. — T / F
- D. An initial MAP target of at least 65 mmHg is recommended. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

**O1. (open)** Explain the relationship between DO2 and VO2 as oxygen delivery falls, and why a normal or high ScvO2 does not exclude tissue hypoxia in septic shock.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- DO2 = CO × CaO2; CaO2 ≈ 1.34 × Hb × SaO2 (+ negligible dissolved O2); main adjustable determinant is CO.
- Initially, falling DO2 is compensated by increased extraction → VO2 stays constant (plateau), SvO2 falls.
- Below DO2crit, extraction is exhausted → VO2 becomes supply-dependent → anaerobic metabolism → lactate rises.
- SvO2 reflects the DO2/VO2 balance: falls with ↓ CO, ↓ Hb, ↓ SaO2, ↑ VO2 (fever, shivering, pain).
- In sepsis extraction is impaired (microcirculatory shunting, mitochondrial dysfunction) → dysoxia with normal/high SvO2.
- Therefore no single parameter suffices – combine lactate (repeated), ScvO2, P(cv-a)CO2 and clinical windows (skin, kidneys, brain).

</details>

**O2. (open)** Outline how you would identify the type of shock in a hypotensive patient whose clinical examination is inconclusive, and the initial treatment principles according to ESICM 2014.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Hypotension is not required; look for hypoperfusion (three windows) and measure lactate (and repeat).
- Echocardiography as preferred modality to determine the type of shock (Level 2, B); distinguishes cardiogenic vs obstructive vs hypovolaemic vs distributive.
- Arterial and central venous catheter if not responding or on vasopressors; ScvO2 and P(cv-a)CO2 to assess type and adequacy of CO.
- No routine PAC (Level 1, A); PAC/TPTD in complex cases or severe shock with ARDS.
- VIP: ventilate, infuse (fluid challenge guided by dynamic predictors, not CVP alone), pump (noradrenaline first line; dobutamine if low CO with persistent hypoperfusion).
- Initial MAP ≥ 65 mmHg, then individualise (lower in uncontrolled haemorrhage without TBI, higher with chronic hypertension).
- No absolute/supranormal DO2 target (Gattinoni 1995: no benefit).

</details>


## W04 · D2 – Fluid responsiveness: PLR, PPV/SVV, EEO, mini-fluid challenge

**1.** A sedated patient with septic shock is ventilated in volume control with a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg PBW, in sinus rhythm. Pulse pressure variation is 5 %. What is the best interpretation?

- A. A fluid bolus of 2 L should be given
- B. PPV may be falsely negative because of the low tidal volume; perform a tidal volume challenge or PLR
- C. PPV is reliable because the patient is in sinus rhythm
- D. The patient is definitely not fluid responsive
- E. PPV is falsely positive because of low tidal volume

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** PPV depends on the size of the cyclic intrathoracic pressure swings; at VT 6 mL/kg the change in preload is too small, so a low PPV may be **falsely negative**. De Backer 2005: a PPV threshold correctly classified only 51 % of patients at VT < 8 mL/kg vs 88 % at ≥ 8 mL/kg. A tidal volume challenge (6 → 8 mL/kg for ~1 min), PLR with real-time CO or EEO resolves the question.

**Why not the others:**

- A – There is no evidence of responsiveness, and a large untested bolus risks fluid overload.
- C – Sinus rhythm is necessary but not sufficient; the low tidal volume remains a limitation.
- D – A PPV below 12–13 % at low VT cannot exclude responsiveness.
- E – Low tidal volume produces false-negative, not false-positive, results.

**EDIC trap:** PPV 5 % at VT 6 mL/kg is not proof of non-responsiveness (LIMITS).

**Ref:** De Backer D, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2005;31:517–523. Monnet X, Shi R, Teboul JL. Prediction of fluid responsiveness. What's new? *Ann Intensive Care* 2022;12:46.

</details>

**2.** Regarding passive leg raising (PLR):

- A. It should start from the semi-recumbent position. — T / F
- B. A cardiac output increase ≥ 10 % predicts fluid responsiveness. — T / F
- C. Changes in arterial pulse pressure are as sensitive as changes in cardiac output. — T / F
- D. Intra-abdominal hypertension has no effect on its reliability. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – Starting semi-recumbent mobilises splanchnic as well as leg venous blood, giving a larger preload challenge than starting supine.
- B (True) – A rise in CO/SV ≥ 10 %, measured in real time because the effect peaks within ~1 min, predicts responsiveness (AUROC 0.95).
- C (False) – Pulse pressure changes have a sensitivity of only 0.56 (AUROC 0.77) vs 0.85 (AUROC 0.95) for CO; blood pressure alone is not enough.
- D (False) – Intra-abdominal hypertension impedes venous return from the legs and splanchnic bed and can cause false-negative PLR.

**EDIC trap:** PLR judged by blood pressure alone is a classic wrong answer.

**Ref:** Monnet X, et al. Passive leg raising for predicting fluid responsiveness: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Intensive Care Med* 2016;42:1935–1947. Monnet X, Shi R, Teboul JL. Prediction of fluid responsiveness. What's new? *Ann Intensive Care* 2022;12:46.

</details>

**3.** In the original study by Michard et al. (AJRCCM 2000), the threshold of respiratory variation in pulse pressure discriminating responders from non-responders was:

- A. 20 %
- B. 10 %
- C. 13 %
- D. 5 %
- E. 8 %

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Michard et al. studied **40 ventilated septic patients** with acute circulatory failure; a respiratory variation in pulse pressure (ΔPP) of **13 %** separated responders from non-responders with **sensitivity 94 % and specificity 96 %**. ΔPP was more accurate than systolic pressure variation and static filling pressures.

**Why not the others:**

- A – 20 % is well above the threshold and would miss many responders.
- B – 10 % is the PLR threshold for the rise in cardiac output, not the ΔPP cut-off.
- D – 5 % is the threshold for the EEO test and the mini-fluid challenge.
- E – 8 is the minimum tidal volume (mL/kg) for PPV to be reliable, not the ΔPP threshold.

**EDIC trap:** Keep the numbers apart: PPV 13 %, PLR 10 %, EEO 5 %, EEO + EIO VTI sum 13 %, VT ≥ 8 mL/kg.

**Ref:** Michard F, et al. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2000;162:134–138.

</details>

**4.** Conditions that limit the reliability of pulse pressure variation include:

- A. Low tidal volume ventilation (< 8 mL/kg) — T / F
- B. Open chest — T / F
- C. Spontaneous breathing activity — T / F
- D. Atrial fibrillation — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – Low VT produces small intrathoracic pressure swings and false-negative PPV (De Backer 2005).
- B (True) – With an open chest, ventilation no longer transmits pressure changes to the heart (the "T" in LIMITS).
- C (True) – Spontaneous efforts make pressure swings irregular; respiratory variation in PP had an AUC of only ~0.68.
- D (True) – Irregular beats vary stroke volume independently of ventilation; other limits are a low HR/RR ratio and raised abdominal pressure.

**EDIC trap:** Only a minority of ICU patients meet all conditions for PPV, which is why PLR and EEO were developed.

**Ref:** Monnet X, Shi R, Teboul JL. Prediction of fluid responsiveness. What's new? *Ann Intensive Care* 2022;12:46. De Backer D, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2005;31:517–523.

</details>

**5.** The end-expiratory occlusion test:

- A. Requires a 5-second occlusion and a 15 % rise in cardiac output
- B. Requires an occlusion of about 15 seconds and a rise in cardiac output ≥ 5 %
- C. Is best assessed by changes in central venous pressure
- D. Is unreliable in patients with arrhythmias
- E. Can be performed in non-intubated spontaneously breathing patients on a face mask

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Interrupting ventilation at end-expiration for **~15 s** (> 12 s so the extra preload crosses the pulmonary circulation) removes the cyclic inspiratory impediment to venous return. A **cardiac output rise ≥ 5 %** predicts responsiveness (Monnet 2009: sensitivity 91 %, specificity 100 % with pulse contour CI; Messina 2019 meta-analysis AUROC 0.96).

**Why not the others:**

- A – A 5 s pause is too short for the preload change to reach the LV, and the threshold is 5 %, not 15 %.
- C – CVP is a static pressure, not flow; EEO needs a precise CO measurement.
- D – The original study included patients with arrhythmias, because the effect spans several beats.
- E – EEO requires an intubated patient who tolerates a 15 s pause in ventilation.

**Ref:** Monnet X, et al. End-expiratory occlusion test. *Crit Care Med* 2009;37:951–956. Messina A, et al. Functional hemodynamic tests: EEO and mini-fluid challenge meta-analysis. *Crit Care* 2019;23(1).

</details>

**6.** Regarding the mini-fluid challenge:

- A. Echocardiographic VTI is precise enough to detect a 5 % change reliably in all cases. — T / F
- B. It consists of about 100–150 mL given over 1–2 minutes. — T / F
- C. Like PLR, its haemodynamic effect is fully reversible in non-responders. — T / F
- D. The threshold for a positive response is about a 25 % increase in cardiac output. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – The least significant change of echo VTI is ~12 %, so a 5 % rise cannot be detected reliably; a precise method such as calibrated pulse contour is needed.
- B (True) – The mini-fluid challenge is 100–150 mL over 1–2 min; with 50 mL it is less reliable.
- C (False) – The fluid is actually given and cannot be withdrawn in a non-responder, unlike PLR or EEO.
- D (False) – The threshold is about 5 % (Messina 2019: AUROC 0.91, sensitivity 0.82, specificity 0.83).

**Ref:** Messina A, et al. Functional hemodynamic tests: EEO and mini-fluid challenge meta-analysis. *Crit Care* 2019;23(1). Monnet X, Shi R, Teboul JL. Prediction of fluid responsiveness. What's new? *Ann Intensive Care* 2022;12:46.

**7.** In the 2016 meta-analysis by Monnet et al., the pooled area under the ROC curve for PLR-induced changes in cardiac output was approximately:

- A. 0.85
- B. 0.95
- C. 0.70
- D. 0.77
- E. 0.60

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** The meta-analysis pooled **21 studies and 991 patients**: PLR-induced changes in cardiac output had a pooled sensitivity of 0.85, specificity of 0.91 and **AUROC 0.95**, using a threshold of about 10 %. Accuracy was maintained with spontaneous breathing, arrhythmias and low tidal volume.

**Why not the others:**

- A – 0.85 is the pooled sensitivity for CO changes, not the AUROC.
- C – 0.70 understates PLR; it is closer to PPV accuracy during spontaneous breathing (~0.68).
- D – 0.77 is the AUROC for PLR-induced changes in pulse pressure.
- E – 0.60 would make PLR a poor test, which it is not when CO is measured.

**Ref:** Monnet X, et al. Passive leg raising for predicting fluid responsiveness: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *Intensive Care Med* 2016;42:1935–1947.

**8.** Regarding the ESICM 2014 consensus on fluid management in shock:

- B. A target CVP of 8–12 mmHg should guide fluid administration. — T / F

**9.** A patient with septic shock and raised intracranial pressure after head injury is mechanically ventilated, in atrial fibrillation, with a calibrated pulse contour monitor. Which test of fluid responsiveness is most appropriate?

- A. CVP response to a 1 L bolus
- B. Inferior vena cava collapsibility
- C. End-expiratory occlusion test
- D. Passive leg raising
- E. Pulse pressure variation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** EEO remains valid in **atrial fibrillation** and requires only an intubated patient who tolerates a ~15 s pause. The calibrated pulse contour gives continuous, real-time CO precise enough to detect the ≥ 5 % rise. It is therefore the only listed test not invalidated by this patient's conditions.

**Why not the others:**

- A – CVP change does not predict responsiveness, and 1 L is a treatment rather than a test.
- B – IVC collapsibility has poor accuracy in ventilated patients, and ESICM 2021 recommends against basic ultrasound for this purpose.
- D – PLR lowers the trunk from the semi-recumbent position and is contraindicated in intracranial hypertension.
- E – AF invalidates PPV because stroke volume varies from beat to beat.

**Ref:** Monnet X, et al. End-expiratory occlusion test. *Crit Care Med* 2009;37:951–956. Monnet X, Shi R, Teboul JL. Prediction of fluid responsiveness. What's new? *Ann Intensive Care* 2022;12:46.

**10.** Regarding fluid responsiveness:

- A. A positive test of fluid responsiveness is by itself an indication for fluid. — T / F
- B. In meta-analysed studies only about half of patients were fluid responders. — T / F
- C. A classic 500 mL fluid challenge may itself be considered treatment rather than a test. — T / F
- D. Respiratory variation of IVC diameter has high sensitivity and specificity in ventilated patients. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – Fluid responsiveness is physiological – healthy people are responders; fluid needs hypoperfusion **plus** a positive test **plus** acceptable risk.

**EDIC trap:** "Fluid responsive" does not mean "needs fluid".

**Ref:** Monnet X, Shi R, Teboul JL. Prediction of fluid responsiveness. What's new? *Ann Intensive Care* 2022;12:46. Messina A, et al. Functional hemodynamic tests: EEO and mini-fluid challenge meta-analysis. *Crit Care* 2019;23(1).

**O1. (open)** A spontaneously breathing, non-intubated patient in atrial fibrillation has septic shock and persistent hypoperfusion after initial fluids. Explain which tests of fluid responsiveness are valid in this patient and which are not, and how you would perform the most suitable one.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

- PRISM (3723 pts): 24.9 vs 25.4%, aOR 0.97; longer ICU stay, more CV support, higher costs; no subgroup benefited.
- Explanations: lower baseline mortality as "usual care" improved (early recognition, antibiotics, fluids), single-centre vs multicentre design, population differences.
- The core elements (early recognition, antibiotics, fluids) became standard; the rigid CVP/ScvO2/Hct protocol adds nothing.
- Modern approach: individualised targets, dynamic fluid responsiveness, lactate and CRT as perfusion guides (SSC 2021).

</details>

- D – Diuresis does not correct hypotension or RV ischaemia and is not enough on its own.

- C – Monnet 2009 included patients with arrhythmias.
- D – ~15 s (> 12 s) is enough; a 60 s apnoea is unnecessary.

**Ref:** Messina A, et al. *Crit Care* 2019;23(1). Monnet X, et al. *Crit Care Med* 2009;37:951–956.

**12.** Regarding lactate as a resuscitation target:

- A. Adrenaline can raise lactate without tissue hypoperfusion. — T / F
- B. In ANDROMEDA-SHOCK, lactate-targeted resuscitation had higher 72-h SOFA than CRT-targeted resuscitation. — T / F
- C. Lactate changes within minutes of a fluid bolus. — T / F
- D. In the LACTATE study, lactate-guided therapy increased mortality after adjustment for risk factors. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – β2 stimulation by adrenaline increases aerobic glycolysis (type B hyperlactataemia).
- B (True) – SOFA at 72 h was 6.6 in the lactate arm vs 5.6 in the CRT arm (p = 0.045).
- C (False) – Lactate kinetics take hours; CRT and CO change within minutes.
- D (False) – Crude mortality was 43.5 % vs 33.9 % (NS), and after adjustment lactate-guided therapy **reduced** it (HR 0.61).

**Ref:** Hernández G, et al. *JAMA* 2019;321:654–664. Jansen TC, et al. *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 2010;182:752–761.

**13.** A hypotensive patient has a moderate pericardial effusion. Which finding argues most strongly against tamponade?

- A. A non-dilated IVC with normal respiratory variation
- B. Marked respiratory variation in mitral inflow
- C. Right atrial systolic collapse
- D. Right ventricular diastolic collapse
- E. Small left ventricular cavity

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Tamponade raises right atrial pressure, so the IVC is almost always **dilated with little respiratory variation**. A non-dilated IVC with normal variation therefore argues most strongly against tamponade (ESICM 2021: IVC size to judge its likelihood, strong).

**Why not the others:**

- B – Marked respiratory variation in mitral inflow supports tamponade (ventricular interdependence).
- C – RA systolic collapse indicates a haemodynamically important effusion.
- D – RV diastolic collapse is a specific sign of tamponade.
- E – A small LV cavity fits tamponade (underfilling) and is nonspecific.

**Ref:** Robba C, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2021;47:1347–1367.

**14.** Regarding monitoring devices in shock (ESICM 2014):

- A. Neither TPTD nor PAC is suggested in severe shock with ARDS. — T / F
- B. Routine cardiac output measurement is recommended in all patients who respond to initial therapy. — T / F
- C. Less invasive devices should be used only if validated in shock. — T / F
- D. Routine use of a pulmonary artery catheter is recommended. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**15.** A spontaneously breathing patient on CPAP in septic shock has an IVC of 2.3 cm with < 10 % respiratory variation. The best interpretation is:

- A. CVP-guided resuscitation to 8–12 mmHg is indicated
- B. The patient is definitely fluid unresponsive
- C. The patient has tamponade
- D. IVC variation is unreliable under CPAP; use a test such as PLR with cardiac output measurement
- E. The patient definitely needs diuretics

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Under CPAP with spontaneous breathing, IVC diameter reflects intrathoracic pressure and inspiratory effort more than preload reserve, so a 2.3 cm IVC with little variation can occur in a responder. Via et al. list NIV/CPAP and spontaneous breathing among the 10 situations where the IVC fails, and ESICM 2021 recommends against basic ultrasound for fluid responsiveness. PLR with real-time CO or VTI is valid here.

**Why not the others:**

- A – CVP targets should not guide fluids (Level 1, B).
- B – IVC findings under CPAP cannot exclude fluid responsiveness.
- C – Tamponade needs a pericardial effusion; a dilated IVC alone is nonspecific.
- E – Nothing indicates fluid overload; diuretics in septic shock would worsen perfusion.

**Ref:** Via G, Tavazzi G, Price S. *Intensive Care Med* 2016;42:1164–1167. Robba C, et al. *Intensive Care Med* 2021;47:1347–1367.

</details>
