## W26 · D1 – The high-risk surgical patient (risk assessment, POISE-3, OPTIMISE II, RELIEF)

**1.** A 74-year-old man is assessed before an elective right hemicolectomy. He reports that he "keeps fit". Which pre-operative assessment of functional capacity has the best evidence for predicting postoperative death or myocardial infarction?

- A. The anaesthetist's subjective assessment at the clinic visit
- B. Resting transthoracic echocardiography
- C. The Duke Activity Status Index questionnaire
- D. Resting spirometry
- E. Six-minute walk distance estimated by the patient

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In the METS study (n = 1,401), the Duke Activity Status Index predicted 30-day death or MI, and NT-proBNP added information. Subjective assessment by the clinician identified only 19 % of patients unable to achieve 4 METs and did not predict outcome.

**Why not the others:**
- A – subjective assessment had poor sensitivity and no prognostic value in METS.
- B – resting echocardiography is not a routine risk-stratification tool; it is indicated for suspected structural disease or heart failure.
- D – spirometry does not measure integrated cardiopulmonary reserve.
- E – a patient-estimated walking distance is a form of subjective assessment, not a validated tool.

**Ref:** Wijeysundera DN (METS), Lancet 2018; Halvorsen S, Eur Heart J 2022.

</details>

**2.** Regarding cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) before major surgery:

- A. One metabolic equivalent corresponds to an oxygen consumption of about 3.5 mL/kg/min. — T / F
- B. An anaerobic threshold below 11 mL/kg/min identifies patients at increased risk of postoperative complications. — T / F
- C. Severe symptomatic aortic stenosis is a contraindication to CPET. — T / F
- D. Oxygen consumption equals cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – 1 MET is resting O₂ consumption; ~ 4 METs ≈ two flights of stairs.
- B (True) – AT < 11 mL/kg/min marked high cardiovascular mortality in Older's studies and is used to triage to critical care.
- C (True) – exercise against a fixed outflow obstruction risks syncope, ischaemia and arrest.
- D (False) – by the Fick principle, VO₂ = cardiac output × (CaO₂ − CvO₂), the arteriovenous content difference.

**Ref:** Halvorsen S, Eur Heart J 2022; Wijeysundera DN, Lancet 2018.

</details>

**3.** A 68-year-old woman with hypertension and stable angina is scheduled for open repair of an abdominal aortic aneurysm in 3 days. She does not take a beta-blocker. Her heart rate is 78/min. What is the most appropriate approach to beta-blockade?

- A. Start metoprolol 200 mg extended-release 2 hours before surgery
- B. Start intravenous esmolol infusion at induction to target heart rate 55/min
- C. Start bisoprolol 10 mg daily from today
- D. Start atenolol and titrate to a heart rate of 50/min before surgery
- E. Do not start a beta-blocker routinely now; continue her usual medication

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Routine initiation of beta-blockers shortly before non-cardiac surgery is not recommended (ESC 2022, class III). In POISE, metoprolol started just before surgery reduced MI but increased death and stroke, largely through hypotension and bradycardia.

**Why not the others:**
- A – this is the POISE regimen that increased mortality (HR 1.33) and stroke (HR 2.17).
- B – aggressive intra-operative rate control causes hypotension; no outcome benefit.
- C – high-dose initiation a few days before surgery carries the same risk; if started at all it should be weeks earlier and low-dose titrated.
- D – tight rate targets increase hypotension and bradycardia.

**EDIC trap:** Chronic beta-blockers must be **continued** – withdrawal causes rebound ischaemia.

**Ref:** POISE Study Group, Lancet 2008; Halvorsen S, Eur Heart J 2022.

</details>

**4.** A 79-year-old man with diabetes, chronic kidney disease and previous stroke undergoes emergency hip fracture fixation. On day 1 his hs-troponin T is 72 ng/L (pre-operative 14 ng/L). He has no chest pain and the ECG is unchanged. Which statement about this finding is most accurate?

- A. It is independently associated with increased 30-day mortality
- B. It is of no significance in the absence of ischaemic symptoms
- C. It mandates urgent coronary angiography
- D. It reflects renal impairment and can be ignored
- E. It should be treated with thrombolysis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** This is myocardial injury after non-cardiac surgery (MINS). In VISION (n = 21,842), postoperative hs-TnT ≥ 65 ng/L, or 20 to < 65 ng/L with an absolute change ≥ 5 ng/L, was independently associated with 30-day mortality. Most MINS episodes are asymptomatic and are found only by troponin surveillance.

**Why not the others:**
- B – the prognostic impact is the same whether or not symptoms are present.
- C – angiography is for type 1 MI/ACS features (ongoing ischaemia, ST elevation, instability), not for every asymptomatic troponin rise.
- D – CKD raises baseline troponin, but a dynamic rise from 14 to 72 ng/L is myocardial injury.
- E – thrombolysis is contraindicated after surgery and is not a treatment for MINS.

**EDIC trap:** In MANAGE, dabigatran 110 mg twice daily reduced major vascular complications after MINS (HR 0.72).

**Ref:** VISION Investigators, JAMA 2017; Devereaux PJ (MANAGE), Lancet 2018.

</details>

**6.** A 66-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes on metformin and empagliflozin is scheduled for elective liver resection. When should empagliflozin be stopped?

- A. On the morning of surgery
- B. At least 3 days before surgery
- C. It should be continued throughout
- D. 12 hours before surgery
- E. Only if the pre-operative HbA1c is above 8 %

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** SGLT2 inhibitors predispose to euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis during fasting, surgical stress and reduced insulin. ESC 2022 recommends interrupting them for at least 3 days before intermediate- and high-risk surgery; they are restarted once the patient is eating and drinking.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the pharmacodynamic effect persists for days; stopping on the day of surgery is too late.
- C – continuing exposes the patient to ketoacidosis with near-normal glucose.
- D – 12 h is insufficient.
- E – the risk is unrelated to glycaemic control.

**EDIC trap:** Check ketones (not only glucose) in any postoperative patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor with an unexplained metabolic acidosis.

**Ref:** Halvorsen S, Eur Heart J 2022.

</details>

**8.** A 58-year-old man with a drug-eluting stent placed 6 weeks ago for an acute coronary syndrome, on aspirin and ticagrelor, is referred for elective inguinal hernia repair. What is the most appropriate plan?

- A. Stop both antiplatelet drugs 7 days before and operate next week
- B. Switch ticagrelor to enoxaparin bridging and operate
- C. Postpone elective surgery until about 12 months after the ACS
- D. Stop aspirin only and operate
- E. Operate under regional anaesthesia while continuing both drugs

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Stopping DAPT early after ACS and stent implantation markedly increases stent thrombosis and MI. Elective surgery should be deferred until DAPT is complete (~ 12 months after ACS); a time-sensitive operation would need multidisciplinary discussion with cardiology.

**Why not the others:**
- A – stopping both antiplatelet agents 6 weeks after an ACS stent is very high risk.
- B – heparin bridging does not protect against stent thrombosis.
- D – aspirin should be continued in all patients with prior PCI.
- E – neuraxial or deep blocks are contraindicated on ticagrelor; elective surgery is not justified now.

**Ref:** Halvorsen S, Eur Heart J 2022.

**11.** A 71-year-old woman with Hb 98 g/L and ferritin 18 µg/L is scheduled for elective colectomy for cancer in 4 weeks. Which statement best reflects the evidence on pre-operative intravenous iron in this setting?

- A. It halves the need for blood transfusion
- B. It reduces 30-day mortality

## W26 · D2 – Post-cardiac surgery care (LCOS, bleeding, tamponade, POAF, CALS)

**1.** A 67-year-old man is 3 hours after CABG. Chest-drain output was 250 mL/h for the first 2 hours, then fell abruptly to 10 mL in the last hour. HR 118/min, BP 84/52 mmHg on rising noradrenaline, CVP increased from 9 to 18 mmHg, cardiac index 1.6 L/min/m², urine output 10 mL/h. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A. Vasoplegic syndrome
- B. Cardiac tamponade
- C. Hypovolaemia from resolved bleeding
- D. Tension pneumothorax
- E. Acute right ventricular infarction from graft occlusion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Bleeding that suddenly stops draining while CVP rises and cardiac output falls suggests clotted drains with mediastinal clot causing tamponade. Early post-operative tamponade is often localised, so classical signs may be absent. TOE confirms, and treatment is urgent re-exploration.

**Why not the others:**
- A – vasoplegia gives a normal or high cardiac index with low SVR, not a low CI with rising CVP.
- C – hypovolaemia lowers CVP, and the drains were blocked, not dry.
- D – tension pneumothorax is possible but less likely; the drain pattern points to tamponade (examine and check the CXR/ultrasound).
- E – RV infarction can raise CVP and lower CO, but it does not explain the abrupt fall in drain output after heavy bleeding.

**Ref:** STS Task Force, Ann Thorac Surg 2017; Casselman FPA, Eur J Cardiothorac Surg 2025.

</details>

**3.** A 72-year-old woman is in the ICU 2 hours after mitral valve repair. She suddenly develops ventricular fibrillation on the monitor; the defibrillator is at the bedside. According to the cardiac-surgery–specific resuscitation guidance, what is the most appropriate immediate action?

- A. Start chest compressions and give adrenaline 1 mg IV
- B. Give amiodarone 300 mg IV, then shock
- C. Perform emergency resternotomy before any shock
- D. Deliver up to three stacked shocks before starting chest compressions
- E. Start DDD pacing at maximal output

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** In a monitored VF arrest after cardiac surgery, up to three rapid sequential shocks are given before external compressions, because early defibrillation is very effective and compressions may damage the repair or grafts. If they fail, start CPR, give amiodarone 300 mg and prepare resternotomy within 5 min.

**Why not the others:**
- A – compressions are delayed until the shocks are given, and routine adrenaline 1 mg is avoided (rebound hypertension, suture-line disruption).
- B – amiodarone comes after the three shocks have failed.
- C – resternotomy is performed if there is no ROSC after the initial measures, within 5 min.
- E – pacing is the first step for asystole or extreme bradycardia, not VF.

**Ref:** STS Task Force, Ann Thorac Surg 2017; Lott C, Resuscitation 2021.

</details>

**4.** A 64-year-old man is on day 1 after aortic valve replacement. He is in complete heart block, paced via ventricular epicardial wires (VVI, 80/min, output 5 mA). The ECG now shows pacing spikes not followed by QRS complexes and the BP has fallen. What is the most appropriate first action?

- A. Increase the pacing rate to 100/min
- B. Replace the pacing box battery
- C. Increase the sensitivity setting
- D. Give isoprenaline and remove the wires
- E. Increase the pacing output (mA)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Spikes without capture mean the delivered energy is below the myocardial threshold, which often rises over the first days (oedema, fibrosis around the wires). First increase the output, then check connections, reverse polarity and prepare transcutaneous or transvenous pacing.

**Why not the others:**
- A – a higher rate does not help if impulses do not capture.
- B – a battery problem causes failure to pace (no spikes); spikes are present here.
- C – sensitivity adjustments treat over- or undersensing, not loss of capture.
- D – isoprenaline may be a temporising bridge, but removing the wires in a pacing-dependent patient is dangerous.

**Ref:** STS Task Force, Ann Thorac Surg 2017.

</details>

## W26 · D3 – Organ donor management (DBD and DCD)

**15.** An 82-year-old woman is admitted with pneumonia. Two weeks ago she lived alone, washed and dressed herself, but needed her son's help with shopping, finances, transport and heavy housework. What is her Clinical Frailty Scale score?

- A. 3 – managing well
- B. 5 – mildly frail
- C. 4 – living with very mild frailty
- D. 7 – severely frail
- E. 9 – terminally ill

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Dependence on others for instrumental activities of daily living (finances, transport, heavy housework, shopping), while remaining independent in personal care, corresponds to CFS 5 (mildly frail). The score reflects the baseline about 2 weeks before the acute illness.

**Why not the others:**
- A – CFS 3 describes people whose medical problems are well controlled and who are independent.
- C – CFS 4 means independent but "slowed up" or symptomatic, not needing help with IADLs.
- D – CFS 7 means complete dependence for personal care.
- E – CFS 9 is life expectancy < 6 months in someone not otherwise evidently frail.

**Ref:** Rockwood K, CMAJ 2005; Flaatten H (VIP1), Intensive Care Med 2017. → [D5](D5.md)
