## W16 · D1 – Analgesia and sedation (PADIS 2018 + 2025 focused update), ABCDEF bundle

**1.** A 58-year-old man with Child–Pugh C cirrhosis and oliguric AKI (not yet on RRT) is intubated for pneumonia. He requires an opioid infusion to tolerate the ventilator. Which opioid's elimination is LEAST affected by his organ dysfunction?

- A. Morphine
- B. Fentanyl
- C. Hydromorphone
- D. Remifentanil
- E. Oxycodone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Remifentanil is an ester hydrolysed by non-specific plasma and tissue esterases, so its offset (3–10 min, context-insensitive) is independent of liver and kidney function. Its metabolite (remifentanil acid) is almost inactive, making it the most predictable opioid in combined hepatic and renal failure.

**Why not the others:**
- A – morphine is glucuronidated; M6G (active) and M3G (neuroexcitatory) accumulate in renal failure.
- B – fentanyl depends on hepatic CYP3A4 and accumulates with prolonged infusion (large volume of distribution).
- C – hydromorphone-3-glucuronide accumulates in renal failure (neuroexcitation).
- E – oxycodone depends on hepatic CYP3A4/2D6 metabolism and renal excretion of metabolites.

**EDIC trap:** "least affected by organ dysfunction" = esterase metabolism (remifentanil, also cisatracurium among NMBAs).

**Ref:** Devlin JW, Crit Care Med 2018 (PADIS).

</details>

**2.** A 66-year-old intubated woman on day 2 after laparotomy cannot self-report pain. Regarding pain assessment and management according to PADIS:

- A. A Behavioural Pain Scale (BPS) score of 7 indicates significant pain. — T / F
- B. Tachycardia and hypertension are valid stand-alone indicators of pain in this patient. — T / F
- C. Low-dose ketamine may be used as an adjunct to reduce postoperative opioid consumption. — T / F
- D. A Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool (CPOT) score of 1 indicates significant pain. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – BPS ranges 3–12; a score > 5 indicates significant pain.
- B (False) – PADIS states vital signs should **not** be used alone to assess pain; tachycardia/hypertension are only a cue to perform a validated behavioural assessment (BPS or CPOT).
- C (True) – low-dose ketamine is suggested as an adjunct to opioids after surgery to reduce opioid consumption (conditional).
- D (False) – CPOT ranges 0–8; significant pain is a score > 2, so 1 is not.

**EDIC trap:** know the cut-offs – BPS > 5, CPOT > 2; NRS ≥ 4 if the patient can self-report.

**Ref:** Devlin JW, Crit Care Med 2018 (PADIS).

</details>

**3.** A 34-year-old man with severe TBI has been sedated with propofol 5 mg/kg/h for 3 days together with noradrenaline. Lactate rises to 6 mmol/L, CK is 12,000 U/L, potassium 6.1 mmol/L and the ECG shows coved ST elevation in V1–V2. What is the most appropriate immediate action?

- A. Add dexmedetomidine to reduce the propofol requirement
- B. Give intravenous dantrolene
- C. Stop propofol and switch to an alternative sedative with supportive care
- D. Start a lipid emulsion bolus
- E. Increase noradrenaline and continue propofol at the same dose

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** This is propofol-related infusion syndrome (PRIS): high dose (> 4 mg/kg/h) for > 48 h with catecholamines in TBI, producing lactic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia and a Brugada-like ECG. Management is to stop propofol immediately, switch to another sedative and give supportive care (RRT for hyperkalaemia/acidosis, pacing or ECMO if refractory).

**Why not the others:**
- A – any continued propofol perpetuates the mitochondrial injury; reducing the dose is insufficient.
- B – dantrolene treats malignant hyperthermia, not PRIS.
- D – propofol is already a lipid emulsion; lipid rescue is for local anaesthetic toxicity.
- E – escalating catecholamines while continuing propofol worsens the syndrome.

**EDIC trap:** risk factors are dose, duration, catecholamines/steroids, TBI and low carbohydrate intake; monitor CK, lactate and triglycerides.

**Ref:** Devlin JW, Crit Care Med 2018 (PADIS).

</details>

**4.** Regarding randomised trials of sedation strategy in mechanically ventilated adults:

- A. In the trial by Kress et al. (2000), daily interruption of sedative infusions shortened the duration of mechanical ventilation. — T / F
- B. In the ABC trial (Girard 2008), pairing a spontaneous awakening trial with a spontaneous breathing trial was associated with lower 1-year mortality. — T / F
- C. In NONSEDA (2020), a non-sedation strategy significantly reduced 90-day mortality compared with light sedation. — T / F
- D. In the trial by Strøm et al. (2010), a no-sedation strategy increased days without ventilation but was associated with more agitated delirium. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – Kress (n = 128): daily interruption shortened mechanical ventilation (4.9 vs 7.3 days) and ICU stay.
- B (True) – ABC trial: SAT paired with SBT reduced 1-year mortality (44 vs 58 %, HR 0.68).
- C (False) – NONSEDA (n = 700): 90-day mortality 42.4 vs 37.0 % with non-sedation vs light sedation – **not significant**, and not a reduction.
- D (True) – Strøm (single centre): no sedation gave more ventilator-free days (13.8 vs 9.6) but more agitated delirium (20 vs 7 %).

**EDIC trap:** the benefit of protocolised light sedation does not extend to proven mortality benefit of "no sedation" (NONSEDA).

**Ref:** Kress JP, N Engl J Med 2000; Girard TD, Lancet 2008; Strøm T, Lancet 2010; Olsen HT, N Engl J Med 2020.

</details>

**5.** In the SPICE III trial (4000 patients), early sedation with dexmedetomidine compared with usual care resulted in:

- A. Lower 90-day mortality
- B. Similar 90-day mortality, with more bradycardia and hypotension
- C. Fewer days of mechanical ventilation and lower mortality in patients younger than 65 years
- D. Achievement of deep sedation targets without additional sedatives in most patients
- E. Higher 90-day mortality in all age groups

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** SPICE III (n = 4000) compared early dexmedetomidine-based sedation with usual care. 90-day mortality was identical (29.1 % in both groups), while bradycardia and hypotension were more frequent with dexmedetomidine.

**Why not the others:**
- A – mortality did not differ.
- C – in patients ≤ 65 years there was a signal of **higher**, not lower, mortality (heterogeneity by age).
- D – most dexmedetomidine patients needed supplemental propofol, midazolam or both to reach targets.
- E – a possible benefit was seen in older patients (> 65 years), so higher mortality was not universal.

**EDIC trap:** dexmedetomidine is a light-sedation drug; it does not reduce mortality.

**Ref:** Shehabi Y, N Engl J Med 2019 (SPICE III).

</details>

**6.** Regarding the 2025 PADIS focused update (Society of Critical Care Medicine):

- A. It strongly recommends benzodiazepines for anxiety in ventilated adults. — T / F
- B. It suggests melatonin to improve sleep in critically ill adults. — T / F
- C. It makes no recommendation for or against antipsychotics for delirium. — T / F
- D. It suggests enhanced mobilisation/rehabilitation over usual care. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – the panel made **no recommendation** on benzodiazepines for anxiety (insufficient evidence); it advises validated assessment and non-pharmacological measures.
- B (True) – melatonin is suggested to improve sleep (conditional, low certainty).
- C (True) – no recommendation for or against antipsychotics for delirium (low certainty, minimal effect on outcomes).
- D (True) – enhanced mobilisation/rehabilitation is suggested over usual care (conditional, moderate certainty).

**EDIC trap:** every 2025 recommendation is conditional or "no recommendation" – a statement containing "strongly recommends" is false. The update also conditionally suggests dexmedetomidine over propofol for light sedation.

**Ref:** Lewis K, Crit Care Med 2025.

</details>

**7.** A 71-year-old man is ventilated for pneumonia. He is agitated (RASS +2) on a low dose of propofol. Heart rate is 48/min with first-degree AV block. Which sedative choice is LEAST appropriate?

- A. Treat pain first with an opioid bolus and reassess
- B. Titrate propofol to RASS −1 to 0
- C. Assess for delirium with the CAM-ICU
- D. Look for causes of agitation such as hypoxaemia, urinary retention or withdrawal
- E. Start dexmedetomidine infusion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** He is already bradycardic (48/min) with first-degree AV block. α2-agonists lower heart rate; in A2B severe bradycardia (HR < 50/min) was ~1.6 times more frequent with dexmedetomidine or clonidine than with propofol. Starting dexmedetomidine is therefore the least appropriate choice.

**Why not the others:**
- A – analgesia first: pain is a common cause of agitation in ventilated patients.
- B – light sedation (RASS −1 to 0) with propofol is consistent with PADIS.
- C – agitation may be hyperactive delirium; screen with CAM-ICU.
- D – treat reversible causes (hypoxaemia, retention, withdrawal) before adding drugs.

**EDIC trap:** the PADIS 2025 suggestion of dexmedetomidine over propofol is conditional – baseline bradycardia or conduction disease favours alternatives.

**Ref:** Walsh TS, JAMA 2025 (A2B); Lewis K, Crit Care Med 2025.

</details>

**8.** Regarding the A2B (2025), MENDS2 (2021) and SESAR (2025) trials:

- A. In A2B, dexmedetomidine significantly shortened time to successful extubation compared with propofol. — T / F
- B. In A2B, α2-agonist sedation was associated with more agitation than propofol. — T / F
- C. In MENDS2, dexmedetomidine increased days alive without delirium or coma in septic patients. — T / F
- D. In SESAR, inhaled sevoflurane reduced 90-day mortality in ARDS compared with propofol. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – A2B (n = 1404): time to successful extubation was not significantly shorter with dexmedetomidine (sHR 1.09) or clonidine (sHR 1.05) than propofol.
- B (True) – α2-agonist sedation caused more agitation (RR ~1.5) and more severe bradycardia (RR ~1.6).
- C (False) – MENDS2 (sepsis, n = 438): no difference from propofol in days alive without delirium or coma, ventilator-free days, 90-day mortality or 6-month cognition.
- D (False) – SESAR (n = 687): inhaled sevoflurane **increased** 90-day mortality (52.9 vs 44.3 %) and reduced ventilator-free days.

**EDIC trap:** none of the newer sedative strategies (dexmedetomidine, clonidine, volatile agents) has outperformed propofol on hard outcomes.

**Ref:** Walsh TS, JAMA 2025; Hughes CG, N Engl J Med 2021; Jabaudon M, JAMA 2025.

</details>

**9.** A 49-year-old woman with moderate–severe ARDS (PaO₂/FiO₂ 110 mmHg) on day 1 needs deep sedation for prone positioning and neuromuscular blockade. Which approach is best supported by current evidence?

- A. Inhaled sevoflurane via an anaesthetic conserving device
- B. Dexmedetomidine as sole agent targeting RASS −5
- C. IV propofol with an opioid, targeting deep sedation during blockade
- D. No sedation, opioid boluses only
- E. Neuromuscular blockade with light sedation (RASS 0)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Neuromuscular blockade requires deep sedation to prevent awareness, and IV propofol plus an opioid is the standard, evidence-supported approach. During prone positioning with NMB, target deep sedation (e.g. RASS −5) and monitor with clinical signs ± processed EEG.

**Why not the others:**
- A – SESAR: inhaled sevoflurane in early moderate–severe ARDS caused fewer ventilator-free days and higher 90-day mortality (52.9 vs 44.3 %).
- B – dexmedetomidine cannot reliably achieve deep sedation as a sole agent.
- D – opioid boluses alone cannot provide amnesia/hypnosis during paralysis.
- E – light sedation during NMB risks awareness under paralysis – a never event.

**EDIC trap:** "light sedation for all" has an exception – NMB, refractory ICP, status epilepticus and severe ARDS need deep sedation.

**Ref:** Jabaudon M, JAMA 2025 (SESAR); Devlin JW, Crit Care Med 2018 (PADIS).

</details>

**10.** Regarding the ABCDEF bundle and early mobilisation:

- A. "B" stands for both spontaneous awakening and spontaneous breathing trials. — T / F
- B. In the ICU Liberation Collaborative (> 15,000 adults), complete bundle performance was associated with lower hospital mortality. — T / F
- C. The association between bundle performance and outcome was demonstrated in a large randomised controlled trial. — T / F
- D. In the TEAM trial, early active mobilisation increased days alive and out of hospital at 180 days. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – B = Both spontaneous awakening and spontaneous breathing trials (A = assess pain, C = choice of sedation, D = delirium, E = early mobility, F = family).
- B (True) – in > 15 000 adults, complete bundle performance was associated with lower hospital mortality (adjusted HR 0.32) and less delirium, coma and restraint use.
- C (False) – the ICU Liberation Collaborative was a **prospective cohort**, not an RCT; residual confounding is possible.
- D (False) – TEAM (n = 750): early active mobilisation did not increase days alive and out of hospital at 180 days, and adverse events were more frequent.

**EDIC trap:** PADIS 2025 still suggests enhanced mobilisation – TEAM tested an intense early protocol, not rehabilitation per se.

**Ref:** Pun BT, Crit Care Med 2019; TEAM Study Investigators, N Engl J Med 2022.

</details>

**11.** A 62-year-old man with AKI (creatinine 420 µmol/L) received midazolam and morphine infusions for 5 days. Both were stopped 36 h ago, but he remains unresponsive with pinpoint pupils and slow respiratory rate on pressure support. CT brain is normal. The most likely explanation is:

- A. Accumulation of active metabolites of morphine and midazolam
- B. Hypoactive delirium
- C. Propofol infusion syndrome
- D. Non-convulsive status epilepticus
- E. Critical illness polyneuropathy

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Morphine-6-glucuronide (active μ-agonist) and α-hydroxymidazolam glucuronide are renally excreted and accumulate in AKI, causing prolonged sedation, miosis and respiratory depression long after the infusions stop. A naloxone trial is diagnostic and therapeutic; switch to drugs not dependent on renal clearance.

**Why not the others:**
- B – hypoactive delirium does not cause pinpoint pupils and bradypnoea.
- C – he never received propofol, and PRIS presents with acidosis, rhabdomyolysis and arrhythmias.
- D – NCSE should be excluded with EEG if he fails to wake, but miosis and slow breathing point to opioid effect.
- E – polyneuropathy causes weakness, not unresponsiveness with miosis.

**EDIC trap:** benzodiazepine metabolites accumulate in renal failure too – avoid prolonged midazolam infusion in AKI.

**Ref:** Devlin JW, Crit Care Med 2018 (PADIS).

</details>

**12.** According to PADIS, which target best defines "light sedation" in a mechanically ventilated adult without a specific indication for deep sedation?

- A. RASS −4 to −3
- B. RASS +1 to +2
- C. BIS 40–60
- D. RASS −2 to 0
- E. SAS 1–2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** PADIS defines light sedation as being arousable with purposeful response to voice – roughly RASS −2 to +1 (or SAS 3–4); most protocols target RASS −2 to 0. Light sedation is associated with shorter ventilation and ICU stay.

**Why not the others:**
- A – RASS −4 to −3 is deep/moderate sedation, reserved for specific indications.
- B – RASS +1 to +2 is restless/agitated, not a target.
- C – BIS 40–60 corresponds to general-anaesthetic depth.
- E – SAS 1–2 is unarousable/very sedated; light sedation is SAS 3–4.

**EDIC trap:** RASS 0 = alert and calm; negative scores describe sedation, positive scores agitation.

**Ref:** Devlin JW, Crit Care Med 2018 (PADIS).

</details>

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:

## W16 · D2 – Delirium: diagnosis, prevention and treatment

**1.** A 74-year-old woman on day 4 of ventilation for pneumonia has RASS −1. She fails to squeeze on 4 of the 10 letters in "SAVEAHAART". Her nurse reports she was alert yesterday and drowsy today. Which conclusion is correct?

- A. CAM-ICU cannot be assessed because she is intubated
- B. CAM-ICU is negative because Feature 4 has not been tested
- C. CAM-ICU is positive
- D. She is in coma and delirium cannot be assessed
- E. An ICDSC score is needed before CAM-ICU can be interpreted

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** CAM-ICU is positive with Feature 1 (acute change or fluctuating course – alert yesterday, drowsy today) plus Feature 2 (inattention: > 2 errors on the letters test; she made 4) plus either Feature 3 (altered level of consciousness, any RASS ≠ 0 – she is −1) or Feature 4. Features 1 + 2 + 3 are present, so she is delirious.

**Why not the others:**
- A – CAM-ICU was designed and validated for intubated, non-verbal patients.
- B – Feature 4 is tested only if Feature 3 is absent.
- D – coma is RASS −4/−5; at −1 she is assessable.
- E – the ICDSC is an alternative tool, not a prerequisite.

**EDIC trap:** RASS −1 counts as altered consciousness for Feature 3 – only RASS 0 is "normal".

**Ref:** Ely EW, JAMA 2001 (CAM-ICU).

</details>

**2.** Regarding screening tools for ICU delirium:

- A. A patient with RASS −4 should be scored as CAM-ICU negative. — T / F
- B. The ICDSC is scored over a whole shift, and a score of 4 or more indicates delirium. — T / F
- C. In the original validation, the CAM-ICU had sensitivity and specificity above 90 % in ventilated patients. — T / F
- D. Inattention is required for a CAM-ICU diagnosis of delirium. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – RASS −4/−5 is coma: the patient is "unable to assess", not CAM-ICU negative; reassess when sedation lightens.
- B (True) – the ICDSC has 8 items scored over a shift; ≥ 4 = delirium, 1–3 = subsyndromal delirium.
- C (True) – original validation: sensitivity 93–100 % and specificity 98–100 % in ventilated patients (lower in later routine-care studies).
- D (True) – Features 1 (acute onset/fluctuation) and 2 (inattention) are both mandatory.

**EDIC trap:** a sedated patient at RASS −4 is never "delirium-free" – coma and delirium are separate brain-dysfunction states.

**Ref:** Ely EW, JAMA 2001; Bergeron N, Intensive Care Med 2001.

</details>

**3.** A 68-year-old man is extubated after cardiac surgery. He lies quietly, answers slowly, is disoriented and cannot follow a conversation. Nurses describe him as "settled". Which statement is most accurate?

- A. Hypoactive delirium is uncommon in the ICU and carries a better prognosis than hyperactive delirium
- B. This presentation excludes delirium because there is no agitation
- C. Low-dose haloperidol should be started to shorten delirium duration
- D. This is likely hypoactive delirium, the most common ICU subtype
- E. A benzodiazepine at night will help restore the sleep–wake cycle

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** A quiet, slow, disoriented patient who cannot follow a conversation has inattention and altered consciousness – the picture of hypoactive delirium. Hypoactive and mixed forms dominate in the ICU; pure hyperactive delirium is uncommon. Without routine CAM-ICU/ICDSC screening it is frequently missed.

**Why not the others:**
- A – hypoactive delirium is the most common subtype and carries a similar or worse prognosis.
- B – agitation is not required; inattention defines delirium.
- C – antipsychotics do not shorten delirium (MIND-USA, AID-ICU) and are reserved for distressing symptoms.
- E – benzodiazepines are a modifiable risk factor for delirium.

**EDIC trap:** "settled" or "sleepy" on the nursing chart may be hypoactive delirium – screen every shift.

**Ref:** Devlin JW, Crit Care Med 2018 (PADIS); Girard TD, N Engl J Med 2018.

</details>

**4.** Regarding the outcomes associated with ICU delirium:

- A. In ventilated patients, delirium was independently associated with higher 6-month mortality. — T / F
- B. In BRAIN-ICU, about one third of survivors had cognitive scores similar to moderate traumatic brain injury at 12 months. — T / F
- C. Cognitive impairment after critical illness occurred only in patients older than 65 years in BRAIN-ICU. — T / F
- D. A longer duration of delirium was associated with better long-term cognitive scores. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – Ely 2004: delirium in ventilated patients was independently associated with 6-month mortality (adjusted HR ~3.2).
- B (True) – BRAIN-ICU: at 12 months 34 % had cognitive scores similar to moderate TBI and 24 % similar to mild Alzheimer's disease.
- C (False) – impairment occurred in **both** older and younger patients; age > 65 was not required.
- D (False) – longer delirium duration was associated with **worse** global cognition at 3 and 12 months.

**EDIC trap:** delirium is a dose-dependent risk factor – each additional day matters for long-term cognition.

**Ref:** Ely EW, JAMA 2004; Pandharipande PP, N Engl J Med 2013.

</details>

**5.** A 59-year-old man has been ventilated for 6 days after abdominal sepsis. He repeatedly tries to remove his tube when sedation is reduced and is CAM-ICU positive. Oxygenation and mechanics would allow extubation. According to PADIS and the DahLIA trial, the most appropriate pharmacological option is:

- A. Midazolam infusion to RASS −3
- B. Dexmedetomidine infusion
- C. Scheduled IV haloperidol to shorten delirium
- D. Rivastigmine
- E. Deep propofol sedation until delirium resolves

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** PADIS 2018 suggests dexmedetomidine for ventilated adults whose agitated delirium prevents weaning or extubation. In DahLIA (n = 71), adding dexmedetomidine increased ventilator-free hours at 7 days (144.8 vs 127.5 h) and accelerated resolution of delirium.

**Why not the others:**
- A – benzodiazepines worsen delirium and prolong ventilation.
- C – scheduled haloperidol does not shorten delirium (MIND-USA, AID-ICU); PADIS suggests against routine use.
- D – rivastigmine increased mortality and delirium duration in a trial stopped early.
- E – deep propofol sedation delays extubation and does not treat delirium.

**EDIC trap:** dexmedetomidine is suggested here for agitation preventing extubation – not as routine delirium treatment.

**Ref:** Reade MC, JAMA 2016 (DahLIA); Devlin JW, Crit Care Med 2018 (PADIS).

</details>

**6.** Regarding randomised trials of antipsychotics for ICU delirium:

- A. In MIND-USA, haloperidol significantly increased days alive without delirium or coma compared with placebo. — T / F
- B. In AID-ICU, haloperidol did not significantly increase days alive and out of hospital at 90 days. — T / F
- C. In REDUCE, prophylactic haloperidol improved 28-day survival in patients at high risk of delirium. — T / F
- D. Ziprasidone shortened delirium compared with placebo in MIND-USA. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – MIND-USA (n = 566): haloperidol did not change days alive without delirium or coma compared with placebo.
- B (True) – AID-ICU (n = 1000): 35.8 vs 32.9 days alive and out of hospital at 90 days (adjusted difference 2.9, 95 % CI −1.2 to 7.0) – not significant.
- C (False) – REDUCE (n = 1789): prophylactic haloperidol 1–2 mg 8-hourly did not improve 28-day survival or reduce delirium.
- D (False) – ziprasidone was no better than placebo in MIND-USA.

**EDIC trap:** PADIS 2025 makes no recommendation for or against antipsychotics – they treat distressing symptoms, not delirium duration.

**Ref:** Girard TD, N Engl J Med 2018; Andersen-Ranberg NC, N Engl J Med 2022; van den Boogaard M, JAMA 2018.

</details>

**7.** A 63-year-old woman is admitted to the ICU after emergency laparotomy. Which intervention is MOST likely to reduce her risk of delirium?

- A. Prophylactic haloperidol 1 mg IV 8-hourly
- B. Prophylactic atorvastatin
- C. Night-time lorazepam to promote sleep
- D. Ketamine infusion for prevention
- E. A multicomponent non-pharmacological programme (reorientation, sensory aids, sleep hygiene, early mobilisation)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** PADIS suggests a multicomponent non-pharmacological strategy (reorientation, cognitive stimulation, sensory aids such as glasses and hearing aids, sleep hygiene, early mobilisation, family involvement) to reduce delirium. It is the only intervention among these with supportive evidence and no meaningful harm.

**Why not the others:**
- A – prophylactic haloperidol did not reduce delirium or mortality (REDUCE); PADIS suggests against it.
- B – statins for delirium prevention are not recommended.
- C – benzodiazepines increase delirium risk.
- D – PADIS suggests against ketamine for delirium prevention.

**EDIC trap:** no drug has proven efficacy for delirium prevention; melatonin (PADIS 2025) is suggested for sleep, with Pro-MEDIC negative for delirium.

**Ref:** Devlin JW, Crit Care Med 2018 (PADIS); van den Boogaard M, JAMA 2018.

</details>

**8.** A 55-year-old man is admitted to the ICU with pneumonia. On day 3 he becomes tremulous, sweaty and hallucinating, with HR 130/min and temperature 38.4 °C. He drank about 1 L of spirits daily. Regarding his management:

- A. Thiamine should be given before glucose-containing fluids. — T / F
- B. Dexmedetomidine alone reliably prevents withdrawal seizures. — T / F
- C. Haloperidol is the first-line drug for alcohol withdrawal delirium. — T / F
- D. Hypomagnesaemia should be corrected. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – give IV thiamine before glucose-containing fluids to avoid precipitating Wernicke's encephalopathy.
