## W29 · D1 – Central venous and arterial catheters: insertion, complications, ultrasound guidance

**1.** A 63-year-old woman with septic shock needs a central venous catheter. She has a platelet count of 90 × 10⁹/L, a PaO₂/FiO₂ of 250 and no coagulopathy; all three sites are anatomically suitable. Which site is associated with the lowest combined risk of catheter-related bloodstream infection and symptomatic deep vein thrombosis?

- A. Subclavian vein
- B. Left internal jugular vein
- C. Femoral vein
- D. Right internal jugular vein
- E. Peripherally inserted central catheter via the basilic vein

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** In 3SITES (3,471 catheters), the composite of CRBSI and symptomatic DVT was 1.5 per 1,000 catheter-days with subclavian, 3.6 with jugular and 4.6 with femoral catheters. The trade-off is a higher rate of mechanical complications (mostly pneumothorax, 2.1 %).

**Why not the others:**
- B – left jugular shares the jugular risks and adds a longer, angulated course.
- C – femoral had the highest combined risk.
- D – the jugular site had more infections and thromboses than subclavian.
- E – PICCs carry a high DVT rate and were not part of the comparison.

**Ref:** Parienti JJ (3SITES), NEJM 2015.

</details>

**2.** Regarding ultrasound guidance for central venous cannulation in adults:

- A. Static ultrasound marking of the vein followed by a landmark-guided puncture is as effective as real-time guidance. — T / F
- B. Real-time ultrasound guidance of internal jugular cannulation reduces arterial puncture compared with the landmark technique. — T / F
- C. Confirming the guidewire inside the vein on ultrasound before dilation reduces the risk of arterial dilation. — T / F
- D. A non-pulsatile vessel on colour Doppler is enough to identify it as a vein. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – real-time (dynamic) guidance is recommended; static pre-marking is inferior.
- B (True) – Cochrane 2015: arterial puncture RR ~0.28, fewer total complications, higher success.
- C (True) – wire visualisation in short and long axis before dilating is a key safety step.
- D (False) – colour Doppler alone is unreliable; use compressibility, shape, wall thickness and wire visualisation.

**Ref:** Lamperti M, Intensive Care Med 2012; Lamperti M, Eur J Anaesthesiol 2020; Brass P, Cochrane 2015.

</details>

**3.** During insertion of a right internal jugular catheter in a 70-year-old man, a 12 Fr dialysis catheter is placed. Blood gas from the catheter shows PaO₂ 13 kPa on FiO₂ 0.4 and the transduced trace is pulsatile at 110/60 mmHg. What is the most appropriate next step?

- A. Remove the catheter and apply firm pressure for 10 minutes
- B. Leave the catheter in place and call vascular surgery or interventional radiology
- C. Start heparin infusion to prevent carotid thrombosis
- D. Withdraw the catheter by 5 cm and re-transduce
- E. Use the catheter for haemodialysis as planned

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** The catheter has been placed into the carotid artery. After dilation and insertion of a large-bore catheter, removal and compression risk haemorrhage, a neck haematoma compressing the airway, and stroke. The catheter should be left in place and removed surgically or endovascularly.

**Why not the others:**
- A – compression is adequate only for a small-needle puncture, not for a dilated arteriotomy.
- C – systemic heparin increases bleeding and does not address the problem.
- D – partial withdrawal can still uncover the arteriotomy and cause bleeding.
- E – using an arterial catheter for dialysis is dangerous (embolism, stroke).

**EDIC trap:** Prevention is to confirm the guidewire in the vein on ultrasound before dilating.

**Ref:** Lamperti M, Eur J Anaesthesiol 2020; Lamperti M, Intensive Care Med 2012.

</details>

**4.** While a 58-year-old man sits upright, his internal jugular catheter is removed. He suddenly becomes breathless, SpO₂ drops to 82 %, blood pressure falls to 75/40 mmHg and end-tidal CO₂ (via nasal capnography) falls abruptly. Which position should he be placed in?

- A. Sitting upright with legs dependent
- B. Right lateral decubitus with head up
- C. Prone
- D. Left lateral decubitus with head down
- E. Supine with legs elevated only

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** This is venous air embolism after line removal in an upright patient. Left lateral decubitus with Trendelenburg (Durant manoeuvre) keeps air in the right ventricular apex, away from the outflow tract. Give 100 % oxygen, occlude the site, aspirate via a catheter if possible, and support the circulation.

**Why not the others:**
- A – upright posture favours further air entry and cerebral embolism.
- B – right lateral puts the RV outflow tract in a dependent position, worsening obstruction.
- C – prone has no role and prevents resuscitation.
- E – leg elevation alone does not trap the air in the RV.

**EDIC trap:** Lines should be removed with the patient supine or head-down, during expiration or Valsalva, with an occlusive dressing.

**Ref:** Lamperti M, Eur J Anaesthesiol 2020.

</details>

**5.** Regarding prevention and diagnosis of catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI):

- A. In CLEAN, skin antisepsis with chlorhexidine–alcohol reduced catheter-related infection compared with povidone iodine–alcohol. — T / F
- B. Routine replacement of central venous catheters every 7 days reduces CRBSI. — T / F
- C. A differential time to positivity of 2 hours or more (catheter blood positive earlier than peripheral blood) supports CRBSI. — T / F
- D. Guidewire exchange is the preferred management of a catheter infected with *Staphylococcus aureus*. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – 0.28 vs 1.77 per 1,000 catheter-days (HR 0.15).
- B (False) – scheduled replacement is not recommended; it adds mechanical complications without reducing infection.
- C (True) – DTP ≥ 2 h is a validated criterion when the catheter is kept in situ.
- D (False) – *S. aureus* CRBSI requires catheter removal and new-site insertion; guidewire exchange is only for non-infected malfunctioning lines.

**Ref:** Mimoz O (CLEAN), Lancet 2015; Buetti N, Infect Control Hosp Epidemiol 2022.

</details>

**6.** A 45-year-old man with acute myeloid leukaemia needs a right internal jugular central venous catheter under ultrasound guidance. Platelets are 14 × 10⁹/L, INR 1.3 and he is not bleeding. According to the 2025 AABB/ICTMG guideline, what is the most appropriate approach to platelet transfusion?

- A. Transfuse to above 50 × 10⁹/L before insertion
- B. Transfuse to above 30 × 10⁹/L before insertion
- C. Proceed without prophylactic platelet transfusion
- D. Give fresh frozen plasma and platelets before insertion
- E. Insert a subclavian catheter instead to avoid transfusion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The 2025 AABB/ICTMG guideline suggests platelet transfusion before CVC placement at compressible sites only when platelets are below 10 × 10⁹/L. With real-time ultrasound at the jugular site, bleeding is rare and prophylactic transfusion adds risk and cost without clear benefit.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 50 × 10⁹/L is the threshold for major non-neuraxial surgery.
- B – no evidence supports this target for compressible-site CVC.
- D – a mildly raised INR does not predict procedural bleeding; FFP is not indicated.
- E – the subclavian artery is non-compressible; it is the worst site in thrombocytopenia.

**Ref:** Metcalf RA, JAMA 2025.

</details>

**7.** Regarding arterial pressure monitoring:

- A. An overdamped arterial trace under-reads systolic pressure and over-reads diastolic pressure. — T / F
- B. A transducer placed 10 cm below the phlebostatic axis under-reads arterial pressure by about 7.5 mmHg. — T / F
- C. Mean arterial pressure is the value most distorted by damping errors. — T / F
- D. A fast-flush test with 6–8 oscillations before returning to baseline indicates optimal damping. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – air bubbles, clots or compliant tubing blunt the waveform; MAP is roughly preserved.
- B (False) – a transducer below the reference level **over-reads** (hydrostatic column ≈ 0.75 mmHg/cm, so ~ 7.5 mmHg for 10 cm).
- C (False) – MAP is the **least** affected value; it is the most robust number in both over- and underdamped systems.
- D (False) – many oscillations indicate an underdamped (resonant) system; optimal is 1–2 oscillations.

**Ref:** Scheer B, Crit Care 2002.

</details>

**8.** All of the following are recommended components of a central-line insertion and maintenance bundle EXCEPT:

- A. Maximal sterile barrier precautions
- B. Skin antisepsis with chlorhexidine in alcohol
- C. Daily review of the need for the catheter
- D. Chlorhexidine-impregnated dressing for short-term catheters
- E. Systemic antibiotic prophylaxis before insertion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Systemic antibiotic prophylaxis before CVC insertion does not prevent CRBSI and promotes resistance. It is not part of any CLABSI prevention bundle.

**Why not the others:**
- A – cap, mask, sterile gown, gloves and full-body drape are core elements.
- B – chlorhexidine–alcohol is superior to povidone-iodine (CLEAN).
- C – unnecessary catheter-days are the main modifiable risk.
- D – chlorhexidine dressings reduce CRBSI in ICU patients (Timsit 2012).

**Ref:** Buetti N, Infect Control Hosp Epidemiol 2022; Timsit JF, AJRCCM 2012.

</details>

**9.** A 52-year-old woman has a radial arterial catheter for 5 days. Which statement about complications of peripheral arterial catheters is most accurate?

- A. A normal Allen test reliably excludes the risk of hand ischaemia
- B. Femoral arterial catheters have a lower bloodstream infection rate than radial catheters
- C. Arterial catheters are not a source of bloodstream infection and need no sterile insertion bundle
- D. Temporary radial artery occlusion is common, but permanent ischaemic damage is rare
- E. The brachial artery is preferred because of its excellent collateral circulation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** In a large review of radial catheters, temporary occlusion occurred in about 20 % but permanent ischaemic damage in only ~ 0.09 %. Radial is the first-choice site for this reason.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the Allen test does not predict ischaemic complications.
- B – femoral arterial catheters carry a higher infection risk than radial.
- C – arterial catheter BSI is ~ 1 per 1,000 catheter-days, comparable to short-term CVCs; full aseptic technique is needed.
- E – the brachial artery is an end artery with poor collaterals.

**Ref:** Scheer B, Crit Care 2002; O'Horo JC, Crit Care Med 2014.

</details>

**10.** A 66-year-old man has a left subclavian central venous catheter inserted under ultrasound guidance. Regarding confirmation of the catheter tip position:

- A. The ideal tip position is the lower superior vena cava or the cavo-atrial junction. — T / F
- B. On chest radiograph, the carina is a useful landmark for the upper limit of the pericardial reflection. — T / F

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

</details>

**11.** A 76-year-old woman with CKD stage 4 needs a temporary haemodialysis catheter for AKI on CKD; she may need a permanent arteriovenous fistula later. Which site is LEAST appropriate?

- A. Right internal jugular vein
- B. Left subclavian vein
- C. Right femoral vein
- D. Left internal jugular vein
- E. Left femoral vein

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Subclavian dialysis catheters cause central venous stenosis, which can make an ipsilateral arteriovenous fistula fail. Right internal jugular is preferred, then femoral; the subclavian route is avoided in patients who may need long-term dialysis.

**Why not the others:**
- A – right IJ is first choice (straight course, low stenosis).
- C – femoral is acceptable, especially in bedbound patients (catheter ≥ 24 cm).
- D – left IJ is acceptable but has a longer, angulated course.
- E – acceptable alternative to right femoral.

**Ref:** Lamperti M, Eur J Anaesthesiol 2020; Parienti JJ (3SITES), NEJM 2015.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 60-year-old man in septic shock needs a central venous catheter. Describe how you would insert it safely and minimise complications.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- **Indication and site:** vasopressors; weigh infection/DVT (femoral worst, subclavian best) vs pneumothorax (subclavian worst); in hypoxaemia or coagulopathy prefer right IJ under ultrasound.
- **Preparation:** consent where possible, checklist, check platelets/anticoagulation (transfuse only if < 10 × 10⁹/L at a compressible site), monitoring, assistant, head-down position for IJ/SC.
- **Asepsis:** hand hygiene, maximal sterile barrier, 2 % chlorhexidine–alcohol, sterile probe cover.
- **Technique:** real-time ultrasound (short or long axis), visualise needle tip, **confirm wire in vein (short + long axis) before dilation**, never lose hold of the wire, aspirate and flush all lumens, occlude hubs (air embolism).
- **Confirmation:** lung sliding (pneumothorax), contrast echo or ECG guidance/CXR for tip at cavo-atrial junction; transduce or blood gas if arterial placement suspected.
- **Complications management:** arterial cannulation with large-bore catheter – leave in, call vascular/IR; air embolism – left lateral head-down, 100 % O₂.
- **Maintenance:** chlorhexidine dressing, daily review, remove when not needed; no scheduled replacement; CRBSI work-up with paired cultures and DTP.

</details>


## W29 · D2 – Chest drain, pericardiocentesis, paracentesis, lumbar puncture

**1.** A 45-year-old ventilated man with ARDS develops sudden hypotension and rising airway pressures. Lung ultrasound shows absent lung sliding on the right with a lung point. After needle decompression, a chest drain is planned. Where should it be inserted?

- A. Second intercostal space in the mid-clavicular line, below the lower border of the rib
- B. Within the triangle bordered by latissimus dorsi, pectoralis major and the 5th intercostal space, just above the upper border of a rib
- C. Seventh intercostal space in the posterior axillary line
- D. Through the 5th intercostal space just below the lower border of the rib in the mid-axillary line
- E. Any site, provided a trocar is used to ensure rapid entry

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** The "safe triangle" avoids the internal mammary artery, the long thoracic nerve, breast tissue and the diaphragm. The needle or dissection passes just above the upper border of the rib, because the intercostal neurovascular bundle runs in the subcostal groove.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the 2nd space is used for needle decompression, but a drain there is uncomfortable and risks the internal mammary vessels; the lower border of the rib is where the bundle lies.
- C – low posterior sites risk liver, spleen and diaphragm injury.
- D – correct level, but passing below the rib risks the intercostal artery.
- E – trocars cause organ perforation and must not be used.

**Ref:** Roberts ME (BTS), Thorax 2023.

</details>

**2.** A 64-year-old man has fever and a moderate left pleural effusion 10 days after pneumonia. Ultrasound shows septations. Regarding pleural infection:

- A. A pleural fluid pH below 7.2 is an indication for chest drainage. — T / F
- B. Pleural fluid for pH should be measured with a blood-gas analyser. — T / F
- C. In MIST2, intrapleural DNase alone reduced the need for surgery. — T / F
- D. Intrapleural tPA combined with DNase improved radiological clearance compared with placebo. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – pH < 7.2, pus, or positive Gram stain/culture indicate drainage.
- B (True) – litmus paper or pH meters are inaccurate; avoid air and lidocaine contamination.
- C (False) – DNase alone was not effective and was associated with more surgical referral.
- D (True) – tPA 10 mg + DNase 5 mg twice daily for 3 days also reduced surgery referral and hospital stay.

**Ref:** Rahman NM (MIST2), NEJM 2011; Roberts ME (BTS), Thorax 2023.

</details>

**3.** A 71-year-old woman has a 2.5 L malignant pleural effusion drained through a 12 F chest drain. After 1.8 L has drained in 30 minutes she develops cough, chest tightness and hypoxaemia; CXR shows new unilateral alveolar opacities in the drained lung. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A. Re-expansion pulmonary oedema
- B. Hospital-acquired pneumonia
- C. Haemothorax from intercostal artery injury
- D. Tension pneumothorax
- E. Contralateral aspiration

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Rapid re-expansion of a lung collapsed by a large effusion can cause unilateral increased-permeability oedema. Drainage should be limited to about 1–1.5 L at a time, or stopped if chest discomfort or cough develops.

**Why not the others:**
- B – pneumonia does not appear within 30 minutes of drainage.
- C – haemothorax would cause opacification of the pleural space, not alveolar opacities, and bloody drainage.
- D – tension pneumothorax gives a lucent hemithorax with mediastinal shift.
- E – contralateral aspiration would affect the opposite lung.

**Ref:** Roberts ME (BTS), Thorax 2023.

</details>

**4.** Regarding echocardiographic and clinical features of cardiac tamponade:

- A. Right atrial collapse is a sensitive sign of tamponade. — T / F
- B. Right ventricular diastolic collapse is more specific than right atrial collapse. — T / F
- C. An inspiratory fall in systolic blood pressure of more than 10 mmHg is a recognised clinical sign. — T / F
- D. Respiratory variation of mitral inflow velocity greater than 25 % supports the diagnosis. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – late diastolic/early systolic RA collapse is sensitive; lasting > 1/3 of the cycle it is more specific.
- B (True) – RV diastolic collapse is less sensitive but more specific.
- C (True) – pulsus paradoxus > 10 mmHg; the IVC, by contrast, is typically **plethoric** without inspiratory collapse.
- D (True) – exaggerated ventricular interdependence: mitral > 25 %, tricuspid > 40 %.

**Ref:** Adler Y, Eur Heart J 2015; Schulz-Menger J, Eur Heart J 2025.

</details>

**5.** A 67-year-old man presents with tearing chest pain, BP 80/50 mmHg and distended neck veins. CT shows a type A aortic dissection with a moderate pericardial effusion and echocardiographic RV diastolic collapse. What is the most appropriate management of the effusion?

- A. Urgent echo-guided subxiphoid pericardiocentesis with full drainage
- B. Intubation and positive-pressure ventilation, then pericardiocentesis
- C. Intravenous furosemide and nitrates
- D. Fluoroscopy-guided percutaneous balloon pericardiotomy
- E. Immediate surgical repair of the aorta with surgical drainage of the pericardium

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Haemopericardium from type A dissection is treated by emergency surgery. Percutaneous pericardiocentesis can raise aortic pressure, extend the dissection and cause fatal rupture; ESC guidelines advise against it, except perhaps controlled removal of small volumes as a bridge in extremis.

**Why not the others:**
- A – full needle drainage risks rebleeding and rupture.
- B – positive-pressure ventilation reduces venous return and can precipitate arrest in tamponade.
- C – diuretics and vasodilators worsen tamponade physiology.
- D – balloon pericardiotomy is for recurrent malignant effusions.

**Ref:** Adler Y, Eur Heart J 2015; Schulz-Menger J, Eur Heart J 2025.

</details>

**6.** A 55-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis and tense ascites is admitted with fever and confusion. INR is 2.1 and platelets 48 × 10⁹/L. What is the most appropriate next step?

- A. Correct INR with fresh frozen plasma before any paracentesis
- B. Delay paracentesis until platelets are above 50 × 10⁹/L
- C. Perform a diagnostic paracentesis now, without routine correction of coagulation
- D. Treat empirically for SBP without paracentesis because of the bleeding risk
- E. Perform a CT of the abdomen before considering paracentesis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** EASL recommends diagnostic paracentesis in every cirrhotic patient with ascites admitted to hospital, especially with fever or encephalopathy. Bleeding is rare (< 1 %) and routine FFP or platelet transfusion is not recommended.

**Why not the others:**
- A – INR does not predict bleeding in cirrhosis; FFP adds volume and delays diagnosis.
- B – a platelet threshold of 50 × 10⁹/L is not required.
- D – empirical treatment without a sample prevents confirmation (PMN > 250/mm³) and culture.
- E – imaging is not needed before a diagnostic tap.

**Ref:** EASL, J Hepatol 2018.

</details>

**7.** A 60-year-old woman with cirrhosis undergoes paracentesis. Regarding ascites management:

- A. An ascitic neutrophil count above 250/mm³ establishes spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. — T / F
- B. After removal of 8 L, about 64 g of albumin should be given. — T / F
- C. Sending ascitic fluid in a plain sterile container gives a higher culture yield than bedside inoculation into blood-culture bottles. — T / F

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

**8.** A 34-year-old woman presents with fever, headache, neck stiffness and GCS 14. There is no focal deficit, no seizure, no papilloedema and she is immunocompetent. Blood cultures have been taken. What is the most appropriate next step?

- A. CT head, then lumbar puncture, then antibiotics
- B. Lumbar puncture without prior CT, with immediate dexamethasone and antibiotics
- C. MRI brain before any further management
- D. Antibiotics only after CSF results are available
- E. Lumbar puncture only after platelet transfusion

**Why not the others:**
- A – routine CT delays treatment without benefit in this patient.
- C – MRI delays treatment and is not indicated.

</details>

**10.** A 41-year-old man has a lumbar puncture: opening pressure 32 cmH₂O, white cells 1,800/µL (90 % neutrophils), protein 2.4 g/L, CSF/serum glucose ratio 0.2, lactate 6.1 mmol/L. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- C. Bacterial meningitis
- D. Guillain–Barré syndrome
- E. Subarachnoid haemorrhage

**C.**

## W29 · D3 – Bronchoscopy and BAL; emergency and failed airway

**1.** A 59-year-old man ventilated in volume-control mode (Vt 450 mL, PEEP 10 cmH₂O) through a 7.0 mm endotracheal tube needs bronchoscopy for left lower-lobe collapse. Only a standard adult bronchoscope (outer diameter 5.8 mm) is available. What is the most likely consequence if bronchoscopy proceeds through this tube?

- A. A fall in peak airway pressure
- B. Development of intrinsic PEEP and reduced delivered tidal volume
- C. An increase in PaO₂
- D. A fall in PaCO₂
- E. No change in ventilation because the scope is smaller than the tube

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** A 5.8 mm scope in a 7.0 mm tube obstructs most of the lumen, greatly increasing expiratory resistance. This causes gas trapping (intrinsic PEEP), barotrauma risk and reduced effective ventilation. The tube internal diameter should be at least 2 mm larger than the scope.

**Why not the others:**
- A – peak pressure rises because resistance increases.
- C – oxygenation usually falls, especially with suctioning.
- D – PaCO₂ rises as minute ventilation falls.
- E – the residual lumen is too small for normal expiration.

**Ref:** Du Rand IA (BTS), Thorax 2013.

</details>

**2.** A 66-year-old man ventilated for 6 days has new fever, purulent secretions and a new infiltrate. Regarding microbiological sampling for suspected ventilator-associated pneumonia:

- A. A quantitative BAL culture threshold of 10⁴ CFU/mL is commonly used. — T / F

- D. Clot at the catheter tip
- E. Incorrect zeroing with the transducer closed to air
