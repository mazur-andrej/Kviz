## W18 · D1 – Acute kidney injury: KDIGO definition and staging, pathophysiology, biomarkers, prevention

**1.** A 68-year-old man (80 kg) with pneumonia has a baseline creatinine of 90 µmol/L. On day 2 his creatinine is 190 µmol/L and his urine output has been 0.4 mL/kg/h for the last 14 hours. What is his KDIGO AKI stage?

- A. No AKI – the creatinine has not tripled
- B. Stage 1
- C. Stage 2
- D. Stage 3
- E. Cannot be staged without a 24-hour urine collection

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Creatinine 190/90 = 2.1 × baseline (stage 2), and urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥ 12 h is also stage 2. Patients are staged by whichever criterion gives the highest stage.

**Why not the others:**
- A – AKI requires only a 1.5 × rise in 7 days or 0.3 mg/dL (26.5 µmol/L) in 48 h.
- B – both criteria exceed stage 1 (1.5–1.9 × or UO < 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6–12 h).
- D – stage 3 needs ≥ 3 ×, SCr ≥ 354 µmol/L, KRT, UO < 0.3 mL/kg/h for 24 h or anuria for 12 h.
- E – KDIGO staging uses hourly urine output and serum creatinine; no timed collection is needed.

**Ref:** KDIGO AKI Work Group, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

</details>

**2.** Regarding serum creatinine as a marker of kidney function in the ICU:

- A. Sepsis reduces creatinine production, so creatinine may underestimate the severity of AKI. — T / F
- B. Trimethoprim can raise serum creatinine without reducing the glomerular filtration rate. — T / F
- C. A large positive fluid balance tends to raise serum creatinine concentration. — T / F
- D. Creatinine rises within 2 hours of a tubular injury. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – reduced muscle perfusion and production in sepsis blunt the creatinine rise.
- B (True) – trimethoprim (and cimetidine) inhibits tubular creatinine secretion.
- C (False) – fluid overload dilutes creatinine and can mask AKI; correction for fluid balance is advised.
- D (False) – creatinine is a late marker, lagging 24–48 h; damage biomarkers rise within hours.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012; Ostermann M, JAMA Netw Open 2020.

</details>

**3.** A 63-year-old woman had coronary artery bypass grafting 4 hours ago. Creatinine and urine output are normal, but urinary [TIMP-2]·[IGFBP7] is 0.9 (ng/mL)²/1000. Which action is best supported by randomised evidence?

- A. Start a furosemide infusion to keep urine output > 1 mL/kg/h
- B. Start low-dose dopamine 2 µg/kg/min
- C. Start early haemodiafiltration
- D. Implement the KDIGO bundle (stop nephrotoxins and ACE inhibitors, optimise haemodynamics, monitor creatinine and urine output)
- E. Give N-acetylcysteine

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** A [TIMP-2]·[IGFBP7] > 0.3 identifies high risk of moderate–severe AKI. In PrevAKI, applying the KDIGO bundle in biomarker-positive cardiac surgery patients reduced AKI within 72 h (55.1 vs 71.7 %).

**Why not the others:**
- A – diuretics do not prevent AKI (KDIGO 1B).
- B – low-dose dopamine is recommended against (1A).
- C – there is no indication for KRT; early KRT does not improve outcome.
- E – NAC does not prevent AKI.

**Ref:** Meersch M (PrevAKI), Intensive Care Med 2017.

</details>

**4.** Regarding acute kidney injury biomarkers and tests:

- A. [TIMP-2]·[IGFBP7] reflects G1 cell-cycle arrest of tubular cells. — T / F
- B. Plasma NGAL is highly specific for kidney injury in septic patients. — T / F
- C. Cystatin C is a marker of glomerular filtration that is less dependent on muscle mass than creatinine. — T / F
- D. In the furosemide stress test, a urine output of less than 200 mL in 2 hours predicts progression to KDIGO stage 3. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – both are cell-cycle arrest markers ("tubular stress"); a cut-off > 0.3 identifies high risk.
- B (False) – plasma NGAL is released by neutrophils and rises in sepsis and inflammation, so its specificity is limited.
- C (True) – a functional marker, useful in sarcopenia; affected by steroids and thyroid disease.
- D (True) – furosemide 1.0–1.5 mg/kg; < 200 mL in 2 h had ~87 % sensitivity and ~84 % specificity.

**Ref:** Kashani K, Crit Care 2013; Chawla LS, Crit Care 2013; Ostermann M, JAMA Netw Open 2020.

</details>

**5.** A 55-year-old man with septic shock is resuscitated with fluids and noradrenaline; he has KDIGO stage 1 AKI. All of the following are recommended to prevent progression of AKI EXCEPT:

- A. Furosemide to convert oliguric to non-oliguric AKI
- B. Avoiding aminoglycosides when an alternative is available
- C. Using isotonic crystalloids rather than hydroxyethyl starch
- D. Targeting an adequate mean arterial pressure with vasopressors
- E. Avoiding a large positive fluid balance

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** KDIGO recommends against diuretics to prevent or treat AKI (1B/2C); converting oliguria to non-oliguria improves the numbers, not the kidney. Diuretics are used only for fluid overload.

**Why not the others:**
- B – nephrotoxin avoidance is part of the KDIGO bundle.
- C – starches increase KRT (6S, CHEST); crystalloids are preferred.
- D – vasopressors with fluids are recommended in vasomotor shock (1C).
- E – fluid overload and venous congestion worsen kidney outcomes.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012; Joannidis M, Intensive Care Med 2017.

</details>

**6.** A 72-year-old woman has severe metabolic acidaemia (pH 7.12, HCO₃⁻ 11 mmol/L, lactate 3 mmol/L) and KDIGO stage 2 AKI after abdominal sepsis. Which statement about sodium bicarbonate is best supported by the BICARICU-2 trial (2025)?

- A. It significantly reduced day-90 mortality in the whole trial population
- B. It did not reduce day-90 mortality
- C. It reduced day-28 mortality from 63 % to 46 %
- D. It is contraindicated in AKI
- E. It increased mortality because of hypernatraemia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** BICARICU-2 randomised 640 ICU patients with severe metabolic acidaemia and moderate–severe AKI; day-90 mortality was 62.1 % with bicarbonate vs 61.7 % without. The hypothesis generated by the BICAR-ICU subgroup was not confirmed.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the primary end point was neutral (absolute difference 0.4 percentage points, P = .91).
- C – 46 vs 63 % was the AKIN 2–3 stratum of BICAR-ICU (2018), not BICARICU-2.
- D – bicarbonate is not contraindicated; it may still be used for selected indications.
- E – no excess mortality was found.

**Ref:** Jaber S (BICARICU-2), JAMA 2025; Jaber S (BICAR-ICU), Lancet 2018.

</details>

**7.** Regarding the KDIGO definition and timeline of acute kidney injury:

- A. A creatinine rise of 26.5 µmol/L (0.3 mg/dL) within 48 hours is sufficient to diagnose AKI. — T / F
- B. Starting kidney replacement therapy automatically classifies the patient as stage 3. — T / F
- C. Acute kidney disease describes kidney dysfunction persisting between 7 and 90 days. — T / F
- D. Anuria for 6 hours is a stage 3 criterion. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – the AKIN-derived absolute criterion.
- B (True) – initiation of KRT is a stage 3 criterion regardless of creatinine.
- C (True) – AKD bridges AKI (≤ 7 days) and CKD (> 90 days).
- D (False) – stage 3 requires anuria ≥ 12 h (or UO < 0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥ 24 h).

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

</details>

**8.** A 49-year-old man develops AKI on day 3 after emergency laparotomy for perforated diverticulitis. His intra-abdominal pressure is 24 mmHg, MAP 70 mmHg and CVP 14 mmHg. Which mechanism most directly explains the falling urine output?

- A. Acute interstitial nephritis from piperacillin–tazobactam
- B. Reduced renal perfusion pressure and venous congestion due to intra-abdominal hypertension
- C. Obstruction of the ureters by the drains
- D. Contrast-associated nephropathy
- E. Glomerular hyperfiltration

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** IAP 24 mmHg is grade III intra-abdominal hypertension. Renal perfusion pressure ≈ MAP − IAP (here ~46 mmHg), and raised renal venous pressure and parenchymal compression reduce GFR; oliguria is an early sign of abdominal compartment syndrome.

**Why not the others:**
- A – AIN is possible with β-lactams but does not fit the clear haemodynamic picture.
- C – ureteric obstruction by drains is rare; ultrasound would exclude it.
- D – no contrast exposure is mentioned.
- E – hyperfiltration raises, not lowers, GFR.

**Ref:** Joannidis M, Intensive Care Med 2017.

</details>

**9.** Regarding the pathophysiology of AKI in the critically ill:

- A. In septic AKI, total renal blood flow is often preserved or even increased. — T / F
- B. The outer medulla is particularly vulnerable to ischaemia because of its low tissue oxygen tension. — T / F
- C. A fractional excretion of sodium < 1 % reliably distinguishes pre-renal from intrinsic AKI in ICU patients on diuretics. — T / F
- D. Extensive tubular necrosis is the typical histological finding in septic AKI. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – septic AKI is driven by microcirculatory dysfunction, inflammation and tubular metabolic adaptation rather than global ischaemia.
- B (True) – the S3 segment and thick ascending limb work in a low-PO₂ environment.
- C (False) – urinary indices are unreliable in the ICU, especially with diuretics and sepsis.
- D (False) – histology usually shows patchy, mild changes with little necrosis.

**Ref:** Joannidis M, Intensive Care Med 2017; KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

</details>

**10.** A 74-year-old woman (45 kg, sarcopenic) has a creatinine of 70 µmol/L on day 4 of septic shock, unchanged from admission, but her urine output has been 0.25 mL/kg/h for 26 hours. What is the most accurate interpretation?

- A. Stage 3 AKI by urine-output criteria; creatinine underestimates the injury
- B. Stage 1 AKI
- C. No AKI, because creatinine is normal
- D. Pre-renal state only – AKI cannot be diagnosed on urine output
- E. Stage 2 AKI

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Urine output < 0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥ 24 h meets stage 3. In low muscle mass and sepsis creatinine production is reduced, so a "normal" creatinine is falsely reassuring.

**Why not the others:**
- B – the urine output criterion gives a higher stage; the highest stage applies.
- C – urine output criteria alone define AKI.
- D – oliguria is a full KDIGO criterion and carries excess mortality.
- E – stage 2 is < 0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥ 12 h; she exceeds it.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

</details>

**11.** A 60-year-old man is discharged from the ICU after septic shock with KDIGO stage 3 AKI that did not require dialysis; creatinine at discharge is 140 µmol/L (baseline 85). What does KDIGO recommend for follow-up?

- A. No follow-up because he did not need dialysis
- B. Lifelong avoidance of all RAAS inhibitors
- C. Routine kidney biopsy at 4 weeks
- D. Repeat creatinine only if symptoms develop
- E. Evaluation at 3 months for resolution, new or worsening chronic kidney disease

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** AKI survivors are at markedly increased risk of CKD, ESKD and cardiovascular events. KDIGO recommends evaluation 3 months after AKI; the 2026 draft extends follow-up recommendations for acute kidney disease.

**Why not the others:**
- A – risk of CKD is increased even without KRT.
- B – RAAS inhibitors are restarted when the patient is stable, as indicated (e.g. heart failure, proteinuria).
- C – biopsy is reserved for suspected intrinsic renal disease.
- D – CKD is usually asymptomatic.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012; KDIGO 2026 AKI/AKD draft.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Define AKI according to KDIGO and list the staging criteria. What are the limitations of serum creatinine in critically ill patients?

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- **Definition:** SCr ↑ ≥ 0.3 mg/dL (26.5 µmol/L) in 48 h, or ≥ 1.5 × baseline within 7 days, or UO < 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h.
- **Stages:** 1 = 1.5–1.9 × or ≥ 0.3 mg/dL, UO < 0.5 for 6–12 h; 2 = 2.0–2.9 ×, UO < 0.5 for ≥ 12 h; 3 = ≥ 3 ×, SCr ≥ 4.0 mg/dL (354 µmol/L), KRT, UO < 0.3 for ≥ 24 h or anuria ≥ 12 h; highest criterion applies. AKD 7–90 days, CKD > 90 days.
- **Limitations:** late and functional (lag 24–48 h, > 50 % GFR loss), non-steady state; reduced production (sepsis, sarcopenia, liver disease); dilution by fluid overload; assay interference (ketones, flucytosine); drugs blocking secretion (trimethoprim, cimetidine); unknown baseline.
- **Alternatives:** urine output, cystatin C, measured clearance, damage biomarkers ([TIMP-2]·[IGFBP7], NGAL) – subclinical AKI (ADQI), integrated in the KDIGO 2026 draft.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Describe the evidence-based measures to prevent AKI or its progression in a high-risk ICU patient.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Identify risk: clinical risk factors, e-alerts, damage biomarkers ([TIMP-2]·[IGFBP7] > 0.3) and implement the **KDIGO bundle** (PrevAKI: AKI 55.1 vs 71.7 %).
- Haemodynamics: individualised MAP ≥ 65 mmHg (higher in chronic hypertension), functional assessment of fluid responsiveness, avoid venous congestion and fluid overload; treat intra-abdominal hypertension.
- Fluids: isotonic, preferably balanced crystalloids; avoid starches (and gelatins).
- Nephrotoxins: avoid/stop NSAIDs, aminoglycosides (or once daily with levels), vancomycin levels/AUC dosing, lipid amphotericin, minimise contrast; pause ACEi/ARB for 48 h after high-risk surgery.
- Glycaemic control (avoid hyperglycaemia), adequate protein (no restriction).
- Not effective: diuretics (except fluid overload), low-dose dopamine, fenoldopam, ANP, NAC; bicarbonate does not improve survival (BICARICU-2).

</details>


## W18 · D2 – Timing of kidney replacement therapy: STARRT-AKI, AKIKI, AKIKI 2, ELAIN, IDEAL-ICU

**1.** A 64-year-old man with septic shock from pneumonia has KDIGO stage 3 AKI (creatinine 3.4 × baseline, urine output 0.2 mL/kg/h for 18 h). K⁺ is 5.2 mmol/L, pH 7.31, PaO₂/FiO₂ 260; he is on noradrenaline 0.2 µg/kg/min. What is the most appropriate approach to kidney replacement therapy?

- A. Start CRRT within 6 hours because he has stage 3 AKI
- B. Start intermittent haemodialysis immediately to clear inflammatory mediators
- C. Start CRRT when urea exceeds 20 mmol/L
- D. Start high-volume haemofiltration at 70 mL/kg/h
- E. Watchful waiting with close monitoring, starting KRT if an urgent indication or prolonged oligo-anuria develops

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Without an urgent indication, accelerated KRT did not reduce mortality in AKIKI, IDEAL-ICU or STARRT-AKI and increased adverse events and KRT dependence. About 40–50 % of patients in delayed arms never needed KRT.

**Why not the others:**
- A – stage 3 alone is not an indication (AKIKI: 48.5 vs 49.7 %).
- B – removal of mediators is not proven and IHD is poorly tolerated in shock.
- C – a single urea threshold of 20 mmol/L is not an indication; AKIKI used BUN > 112 mg/dL (urea > 40 mmol/L).
- D – high-volume haemofiltration did not improve survival in septic shock (IVOIRE).

**Ref:** Bagshaw SM (STARRT-AKI), N Engl J Med 2020; Gaudry S (AKIKI), N Engl J Med 2016.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the STARRT-AKI trial (2020):

- A. More than 2,900 patients were included in the analysis. — T / F
- B. Accelerated KRT reduced 90-day mortality compared with the standard strategy. — T / F
- C. Dependence on KRT at 90 days was more frequent in the accelerated group. — T / F
- D. All patients in the standard arm eventually received KRT. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – 3,019 randomised, 2,927 in the modified intention-to-treat analysis.
- B (False) – 43.9 vs 43.7 % (RR 1.00).
- C (True) – 10.4 vs 6.0 %; adverse events were also more frequent (23.0 vs 16.5 %).
- D (False) – 38 % of the standard arm never received KRT.

**Ref:** Bagshaw SM, N Engl J Med 2020.

</details>

**3.** A 58-year-old woman has been anuric for 80 hours with septic AKI. Her urea is 46 mmol/L (BUN ≈ 129 mg/dL), K⁺ 5.6 mmol/L and pH 7.24; she is haemodynamically stable on low-dose noradrenaline. According to AKIKI 2, what is the best decision?

- A. Wait until urea exceeds 50 mmol/L (BUN 140 mg/dL) or K⁺ > 6 develops
- B. Start KRT now
- C. Give furosemide 250 mg to induce diuresis and avoid KRT
- D. Start KRT only if she develops pericarditis
- E. Give sodium bicarbonate and reassess in 72 hours

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** In AKIKI 2, among patients with oliguria > 72 h or BUN > 112 mg/dL, further postponement ("more-delayed", until mandatory criteria) did not increase KRT-free days and was associated with higher 60-day mortality (55 vs 44 %, adjusted HR 1.65).

**Why not the others:**
- A – this is the "more-delayed" strategy that appeared harmful.
- C – diuretics do not treat AKI or avoid KRT (KDIGO 2C).
- D – waiting for uraemic complications is unsafe.
- E – BICARICU-2 showed no mortality benefit of bicarbonate, and a further 72 h delay is too long.

**Ref:** Gaudry S (AKIKI 2), Lancet 2021.

</details>

**4.** Regarding randomised trials of KRT timing:

- A. In AKIKI, about half of the patients in the delayed group never received KRT. — T / F
- B. ELAIN was a large multicentre trial in medical septic patients. — T / F
- C. IDEAL-ICU compared early KRT with a strategy of delaying KRT for 7 days. — T / F
- D. In AKIKI, catheter-related bloodstream infections were less frequent with the early strategy. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – 49 % of the delayed group avoided KRT.
- B (False) – ELAIN was single-centre (n = 231), mostly cardiac surgery patients at KDIGO stage 2 with raised NGAL.
- C (False) – the delayed arm waited 48 h (septic shock, RIFLE "failure"); 90-day mortality 58 vs 54 %; stopped for futility.
- D (False) – they were more frequent with early KRT (10 vs 5 %).

**Ref:** Gaudry S, N Engl J Med 2016; Zarbock A, JAMA 2016; Barbar SD, N Engl J Med 2018.

</details>

**5.** A 70-year-old man with stage 3 AKI after aortic surgery has K⁺ 5.8 mmol/L, pH 7.27, urine output 0.3 mL/kg/h for 20 h and stable haemodynamics. Which of the following would be an ABSOLUTE indication to start KRT now?

- A. Serum creatinine of 400 µmol/L
- B. Positive [TIMP-2]·[IGFBP7] test
- C. Pulmonary oedema with PaO₂/FiO₂ 150 not responding to furosemide
- D. Urea 25 mmol/L
- E. KDIGO stage 3 by creatinine criteria

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Diuretic-resistant fluid overload with hypoxaemia (STARRT-AKI used PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤ 200) is a conventional indication. Others are K⁺ ≥ 6 mmol/L, pH ≤ 7.20 with HCO₃⁻ ≤ 12 mmol/L and uraemic complications.

**Why not the others:**
- A – a creatinine value alone is not an indication.
- B – biomarkers predict risk but have not been shown to improve outcome as triggers.
- D – urea 25 mmol/L is below AKIKI criteria (> 40 mmol/L).
- E – stage 3 alone did not justify early KRT (AKIKI, STARRT-AKI).

**Ref:** Bagshaw SM, N Engl J Med 2020; KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

</details>

**6.** A 66-year-old woman has been on CRRT for 9 days for septic AKI. Shock has resolved. Her urine output over the last 24 hours, without diuretics, was 650 mL. Which statement best guides discontinuation of KRT?

- A. KRT must continue until creatinine normalises
- B. Furosemide infusion should be started to hasten renal recovery
- C. Urine output cannot be used while on CRRT
- D. Spontaneous urine output above about 400–500 mL/day without diuretics predicts successful discontinuation
- E. Discontinuation should only be attempted after 21 days

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** In BEST Kidney, urine output > 436 mL/day without diuretics was the strongest predictor of successful cessation of CRRT; the threshold is much higher (~2,300 mL/day) if diuretics are given.

**Why not the others:**
- A – creatinine is cleared by CRRT and lags; full normalisation is not required.
- B – KDIGO suggests not using diuretics to enhance kidney recovery or shorten KRT (2B).
- C – urine output is the main clinical marker of native recovery during CRRT.
- E – there is no minimum duration.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

</details>

**7.** Regarding potential harms associated with KRT in critically ill patients:

- A. Hypophosphataemia is a frequent complication of continuous KRT. — T / F
- B. KRT can remove antibiotics and lead to underdosing. — T / F
- C. Intradialytic hypotension may delay renal recovery. — T / F
- D. Hypothermia and hypomagnesaemia can occur during continuous KRT. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – phosphate is removed effectively; replacement fluids with phosphate are used.
- B (True) – hydrophilic, low-protein-bound drugs (β-lactams, vancomycin) are cleared – dose by effluent rate and levels.
- C (True) – repeated ischaemic hits are a proposed reason for higher KRT dependence after accelerated KRT in STARRT-AKI.
- D (True) – extracorporeal heat loss (may mask fever) and loss of magnesium, especially with citrate, are common.

**Ref:** Bagshaw SM, N Engl J Med 2020; Gaudry S, N Engl J Med 2016.

</details>

**8.** A 55-year-old man is admitted after a cardiac arrest from hyperkalaemia (K⁺ 8.2 mmol/L) and has anuric AKI. ROSC was achieved after calcium, insulin–glucose and bicarbonate. Repeat K⁺ is 6.9 mmol/L with peaked T waves. What is the most appropriate next step?

- A. Emergency haemodialysis

- C. Oral calcium polystyrene sulphonate and reassess tomorrow
- D. Watchful waiting according to STARRT-AKI
- E. Furosemide 40 mg IV

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Refractory life-threatening hyperkalaemia in anuric AKI is an emergency indication for KRT; intermittent haemodialysis removes potassium fastest. Medical therapy only shifts K⁺ temporarily.

**Why not the others:**
- B – insulin–glucose is a temporising shift, effective for only 4–6 h.
- C – resins act slowly (hours to days) and are not suitable for emergencies.
- D – STARRT-AKI excluded patients with urgent indications such as K⁺ ≥ 6.0.
- E – an anuric kidney does not respond to diuretics.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012; Bagshaw SM, N Engl J Med 2020.

</details>

**10.** A 46-year-old man with bipolar disorder is admitted with confusion and coarse tremor; serum lithium is 4.6 mmol/L and creatinine 280 µmol/L (baseline 80). Which is the most appropriate treatment?

- A. Activated charcoal and observation
- B. Forced diuresis with furosemide

- E. Wait for AKIKI criteria before KRT

**Ref:** Decker BS (EXTRIP), Clin J Am Soc Nephrol 2015.

**11.** Early KRT reduced 90-day mortality in the ELAIN trial but not in AKIKI, IDEAL-ICU or STARRT-AKI. Which feature of ELAIN best explains why its result (lower mortality with early KRT) differed from later trials?

- A. It used intermittent haemodialysis only
- B. It enrolled only patients with chronic kidney disease
- C. It started KRT at KDIGO stage 3 in both arms

**Why not the others:**
- A – ELAIN used CVVHDF.
- B – patients with advanced CKD were excluded.
- C – the early arm started at stage 2; the delayed arm within 12 h of stage 3.
- D – that describes STARRT-AKI.

**Ref:** Zarbock A (ELAIN), JAMA 2016.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Summarise the evidence on the timing of KRT initiation in critically ill patients with severe AKI and give a practical approach.

## W18 · D3 – Kidney replacement therapy: modalities, dose, vascular access and citrate anticoagulation

**1.** A 38-year-old man with traumatic brain injury (ICP monitor, ICP 22 mmHg) develops rhabdomyolysis-associated anuric AKI requiring KRT. Which modality is most appropriate?

- A. Intermittent haemodialysis, 4-hour sessions with high dialysate flow
- B. Continuous kidney replacement therapy
- C. Peritoneal dialysis
- D. Slow continuous ultrafiltration (SCUF) only
- E. Daily sustained low-efficiency dialysis with high-efficiency membrane

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** KDIGO suggests CRRT rather than intermittent therapy in patients with acute brain injury or raised ICP (2B). Gradual solute removal avoids the rapid fall in plasma osmolality that drives water into brain cells (dialysis disequilibrium).

**Why not the others:**
- A – rapid urea clearance with IHD can raise ICP.
- C – PD is inefficient and poorly controllable in a hypercatabolic trauma patient.
- D – SCUF removes fluid but provides negligible solute clearance.
- E – SLED is a reasonable compromise elsewhere, but continuous therapy gives the most stable osmolality.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the physical principles of kidney replacement therapy:

- A. Diffusion is more efficient than convection at removing middle molecules such as β₂-microglobulin. — T / F
- B. Predilution replacement fluid reduces the clearance achieved for a given effluent rate. — T / F
- C. In postdilution haemofiltration, the filtration fraction should be kept below about 20–25 %. — T / F
- D. Convection requires a replacement (substitution) fluid to maintain volume. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – convection (solvent drag) clears middle molecules better; diffusion favours small solutes.
- B (True) – blood is diluted before the filter, so solute concentration in the ultrafiltrate falls (~15–20 % loss of efficiency).
- C (True) – higher filtration fraction haemoconcentrates blood in the filter and causes clotting.
- D (True) – large ultrafiltrate volumes must be replaced; net removal is the difference.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

</details>

- A. Prescribe 25–30 mL/kg/h effluent so that the delivered dose is 20–25 mL/kg/h

- C. Prescribe 70 mL/kg/h (high-volume haemofiltration) for septic shock
- D. Prescribe 15 mL/kg/h to protect residual renal function
- E. Dose is irrelevant as long as urea falls

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** KDIGO recommends a delivered effluent of 20–25 mL/kg/h (1A). Because of downtime and predilution, a somewhat higher prescription is usually needed, and the delivered dose should be monitored.

**Why not the others:**
- B – RENAL: 40 vs 25 mL/kg/h, 90-day mortality 44.7 vs 44.7 %.
- C – IVOIRE: 70 vs 35 mL/kg/h, no benefit.

**5.** A 52-year-old man with alcoholic hepatitis and septic shock (lactate 9 mmol/L) is on CVVHDF with citrate. Over 12 hours, systemic ionised calcium falls to 0.85 mmol/L despite increasing calcium infusion; total calcium is 3.1 mmol/L; anion gap is 22 mmol/L. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A. Citrate overdose with metabolic alkalosis
- B. Filter clotting
- C. Hypoparathyroidism
- D. Citrate accumulation due to impaired metabolism
- E. Laboratory error

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Total/ionised Ca ratio = 3.1/0.85 ≈ 3.6 (> 2.5), falling iCa despite more calcium and a high-anion-gap acidosis indicate that citrate is not being metabolised (liver failure, shock with high lactate). Reduce or stop citrate and change anticoagulation.

**Why not the others:**
- A – excess citrate delivery with normal metabolism causes metabolic **alkalosis**, not acidosis with a high Ca ratio.
- B – clotting does not lower systemic iCa or widen the anion gap.
- C – hypoparathyroidism would not raise total calcium.
- E – the pattern is internally consistent.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

**6.** A 76-year-old woman needs CRRT; she has no bleeding risk and no history of HIT. Which statement about the RICH trial (2020) comparing citrate with systemic heparin is correct?

- A. Citrate reduced 90-day mortality by 10 %
- B. Heparin prolonged filter life
- C. Bleeding complications were more frequent with citrate
- D. Infections were less frequent with citrate
- E. Citrate prolonged filter life (47 vs 26 h) without a difference in 90-day mortality

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** RICH (n = 638, stopped early) showed median filter life 47 vs 26 h and 90-day mortality 51.2 vs 53.6 % (not significant), with less bleeding (5.1 vs 16.9 %). Citrate is therefore the default anticoagulant for CRRT.

**Why not the others:**
- A – mortality was not significantly different.
- B – citrate, not heparin, prolonged filter life.
- C – bleeding was less frequent with citrate.

**10.** A 59-year-old man on CVVHDF (effluent 30 mL/kg/h) for septic AKI is receiving meropenem. Cultures remain positive on day 3. Which principle of antibiotic dosing during CRRT is correct?

- A. Loading doses should be halved during CRRT
- B. Meropenem is not removed by CRRT because it is a small molecule
- C. Doses should follow the anuric (end-stage kidney disease) recommendations
- D. Maintenance doses should account for effluent rate and be guided by therapeutic drug monitoring where available
- E. Protein-bound drugs are removed more efficiently than unbound drugs

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Hydrophilic, low-protein-bound drugs such as β-lactams are cleared in proportion to effluent flow; dosing should be adapted to the delivered dose and checked by drug levels. Underdosing is common in the early phase.

**Why not the others:**
- A – loading depends on volume of distribution, which is unchanged or increased; full loading doses are given.
- B – small molecules are removed efficiently by both diffusion and convection.
- C – anuric dosing ignores extracorporeal clearance and risks underdosing.
- E – only the unbound fraction passes the membrane.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

**11.** A 68-year-old woman on CVVHDF with citrate develops pH 7.53, HCO₃⁻ 35 mmol/L, normal anion gap, normal lactate and a total/ionised calcium ratio of 2.0. Her blood flow was recently increased. What is the most likely cause?

- A. Citrate accumulation from liver failure
- B. Excess citrate (buffer) load relative to its removal
- C. Hypoventilation
- D. Contraction alkalosis from diuretics
- E. Inadequate dialysate flow causing acidosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** With normal metabolism, every citrate becomes three bicarbonates; increasing blood flow (and hence citrate dose) without increasing dialysate/effluent flow causes metabolic alkalosis. Correct by reducing blood flow/citrate or increasing dialysate flow.

**Why not the others:**
- A – accumulation causes a high Ca ratio (> 2.5) and high-anion-gap acidosis.
- C – hypoventilation causes respiratory acidosis.
- D – no diuretics are described; she is on CRRT.
- E – the problem is alkalosis, not acidosis.

**Ref:** KDIGO, Kidney Int Suppl 2012.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Explain how regional citrate anticoagulation works during CRRT, how it is monitored, and how you would recognise and manage citrate accumulation.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

**O2. (open)** Compare intermittent haemodialysis and continuous KRT in the ICU and state when you would choose each.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Principles: IHD diffusion, high flows, short sessions; CRRT continuous convection/diffusion, low flows.

## W18 · D4 – Rhabdomyolysis and contrast-associated acute kidney injury

**1.** A 78-year-old woman is found on her floor after an estimated 36-hour "long lie". CK is 48,000 U/L, K⁺ 5.9 mmol/L, creatinine 190 µmol/L, urine dark brown; dipstick is strongly positive for blood but microscopy shows no red cells. What is the most important initial treatment?

- A. Mannitol 1 g/kg IV
- B. Isotonic crystalloid infusion titrated to a urine output of about 200–300 mL/h
- C. Furosemide 80 mg IV to flush the tubules
- D. Immediate high cut-off haemodiafiltration to remove myoglobin
- E. Calcium gluconate to correct the expected hypocalcaemia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Early, generous isotonic fluid therapy corrects hypovolaemia from muscle sequestration and increases tubular flow, reducing myoglobin cast formation. Urine output is titrated to ~200–300 mL/h while watching for fluid overload.

**Why not the others:**
- A – mannitol has no proven benefit and can cause hyperosmolality and AKI.
- C – loop diuretics acidify urine and do not prevent AKI.
- D – myoglobin removal by extracorporeal techniques is not proven; KRT is for conventional indications.
- E – asymptomatic hypocalcaemia should not be treated (calcium deposition, later hypercalcaemia).

**Ref:** Bosch X, N Engl J Med 2009.

</details>

- B. Free haem scavenges nitric oxide and promotes renal vasoconstriction. — T / F
- C. Myoglobin is too large to be filtered by the glomerulus. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – the basis for (unproven) urinary alkalinisation.
- B (True) – together with endothelin and hypovolaemia.
- C (False) – myoglobin (17.8 kDa) is freely filtered.

**3.** A 26-year-old man is admitted after a 6-hour building-collapse entrapment of both legs. Six hours after extrication, his left calf is tense and painful on passive stretch. BP 130/80 mmHg; anterior compartment pressure is 58 mmHg. What is the most appropriate management?

- A. Elevate the leg above heart level and repeat pressure in 4 hours
- B. Apply compression bandages
- C. Urgent fasciotomy
- D. Start mannitol to reduce compartment oedema
- E. Hyperbaric oxygen as first-line treatment

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Compartment pressure > 30 mmHg (or ΔP = diastolic BP − compartment pressure < 30 mmHg; here 80 − 58 = 22 mmHg) with clinical signs indicates acute compartment syndrome requiring urgent fasciotomy to prevent further muscle necrosis.

**Why not the others:**
- A – elevation above heart level reduces arterial inflow; delay worsens necrosis.
- B – compression increases compartment pressure.
- D – mannitol is not an alternative to decompression.
- E – hyperbaric oxygen is at most an adjunct.

**Ref:** Bosch X, N Engl J Med 2009.

**5.** A 34-year-old man is sedated with propofol at 5 mg/kg/h for 3 days for refractory status epilepticus and receives noradrenaline. He develops a new right bundle branch block, lactate 7 mmol/L, CK 25,000 U/L, triglycerides 6 mmol/L and K⁺ 6.2 mmol/L. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A. Propofol infusion syndrome
- B. Malignant hyperthermia
- C. Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
- D. Septic shock with myocarditis
- E. Exertional rhabdomyolysis from seizures alone

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** High-dose (> 4 mg/kg/h) prolonged (> 48 h) propofol with catecholamines, metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hypertriglyceridaemia, hyperkalaemia and ECG changes (Brugada-like, RBBB) is typical of propofol infusion syndrome. Stop propofol immediately.

**Why not the others:**

**6.** A 66-year-old man with rhabdomyolysis after a fall (CK 30,000 U/L) has received 6 L of crystalloid. He is now anuric for 8 hours, with bilateral crackles, SpO₂ 90 % on 6 L/min O₂ and K⁺ 6.6 mmol/L. All of the following are appropriate EXCEPT:

## W18 · D5 – Hyperkalaemia and urgent dialysis indications

**1.** A 74-year-old man on ramipril and spironolactone presents with weakness. K⁺ is 7.4 mmol/L; the ECG shows absent P waves and QRS 160 ms; heart rate 42/min. What is the first drug to give?

- A. Sodium bicarbonate 8.4 % 100 mL
- B. Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate 10 g orally
- C. Furosemide 80 mg IV
- D. 10 mL 10 % calcium chloride IV
- E. Atropine 3 mg IV

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Severe hyperkalaemia with ECG changes requires immediate membrane stabilisation: 10 mL 10 % calcium chloride (or 30 mL 10 % calcium gluconate). It acts within minutes but does not lower K⁺, so insulin–glucose and salbutamol follow at once.

**Why not the others:**
- A – bicarbonate is not routine; reserved for arrest or severe acidosis.
- B – SZC takes ~1 h; it is a removal step, not an emergency stabiliser.
- C – diuretics are slow and depend on kidney function.
- E – the bradycardia is caused by hyperkalaemia; treat the cause first.

**Ref:** Lott C (ERC), Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the drugs used to treat acute hyperkalaemia:

- A. Calcium chloride lowers the serum potassium concentration by about 1 mmol/L. — T / F
- B. Insulin with glucose lowers potassium within 15–30 minutes and the effect lasts about 4–6 hours. — T / F
- C. Nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg has an effect additive to insulin–glucose. — T / F
- D. Hypoglycaemia after insulin–glucose occurs mainly in the first 10 minutes. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**
