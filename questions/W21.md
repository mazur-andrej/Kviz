## W21 · D1 – Nutrition in the ICU: timing, route and calories (NUTRIREA-3)

**1.** A 64-year-old man (75 kg) has been ventilated for 24 hours for faecal peritonitis after Hartmann's procedure. Noradrenaline has been stable at 0.15 µg/kg/min for 8 hours and lactate fell from 5.2 to 1.6 mmol/L. Bowel sounds are absent. What is the most appropriate nutritional strategy?

- A. Withhold nutrition until bowel sounds return
- B. Start low-dose enteral nutrition via a nasogastric tube and advance progressively
- C. Start full-target parenteral nutrition (25 kcal/kg/day) today
- D. Start full-target enteral nutrition at 25 kcal/kg/day immediately
- E. Withhold all nutrition until noradrenaline is stopped

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Shock is controlled (stable dose, falling lactate), so ESICM and ESPEN recommend starting low-dose EN within 48 h and advancing over days. Absent bowel sounds are not a contraindication to EN.

**Why not the others:**
- A – bowel sounds are a poor marker of gut function; waiting for them delays EN unnecessarily.
- C – early full PN offers no benefit and early PN was harmful in EPaNIC (more infections).
- D – full-dose early EN in shock increased bowel ischaemia and GI intolerance (NUTRIREA-2) and standard-dose feeding was not better than low-dose (NUTRIREA-3).
- E – EN can be started once shock is controlled, even on vasopressors.

**EDIC trap:** "Uncontrolled shock" (escalating doses, rising lactate) is the contraindication, not vasopressors per se.

**Ref:** Reintam Blaser A, Intensive Care Med 2017; Singer P, Clin Nutr 2023.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the NUTRIREA-3 trial (ventilated adults with shock):

- A. Patients in the low-intake arm received about 6 kcal/kg/day and 0.2–0.4 g/kg/day of protein during the first week. — T / F
- B. Low intake reduced day-90 mortality significantly. — T / F
- C. Time to readiness for ICU discharge was shorter with low intake. — T / F
- D. Only parenteral nutrition was allowed in both arms. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – low arm 6 kcal/kg and 0.2–0.4 g/kg protein vs standard 25 kcal/kg and 1.0–1.3 g/kg, for 7 days.
- B (False) – day-90 mortality was 41.3 vs 42.8 %, not significantly different.
- C (True) – median 8 vs 9 days; there were also fewer vomiting, diarrhoea, bowel ischaemia and liver dysfunction episodes.
- D (False) – both EN and PN were allowed; the trial compared dose, not route.

**Ref:** Reignier J (NUTRIREA-3), Lancet Respir Med 2023.

</details>

**3.** A 58-year-old woman (70 kg) with ARDS on day 3 is sedated with propofol 1 % at 25 mL/h and receives enteral nutrition. Indirect calorimetry measures 1,700 kcal/day. Approximately how many kilocalories per day does the propofol provide?

- A. 100 kcal
- B. 250 kcal
- C. 660 kcal
- D. 1,100 kcal
- E. None – propofol has no caloric value

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Propofol 1 % in a lipid emulsion provides ~1.1 kcal/mL; 25 mL/h × 24 h = 600 mL × 1.1 ≈ 660 kcal/day – almost 40 % of her measured expenditure. It must be subtracted from the EN target to avoid overfeeding.

**Why not the others:**
- A, B – underestimate the lipid load (10 % emulsion = 0.1 g fat/mL).
- D – would correspond to ~1,000 mL/day.
- E – the soybean-oil emulsion is a real energy source and can also cause hypertriglyceridaemia.

**Ref:** Singer P, Clin Nutr 2019; Singer P, Clin Nutr 2023.

</details>

**4.** A 70-year-old man is mechanically ventilated after a cardiac arrest. All of the following are reasons to DELAY enteral nutrition according to the ESICM 2017 guideline EXCEPT:

- A. Gastric residual volume of 650 mL over 6 hours
- B. Overt mesenteric ischaemia on CT
- C. Active upper gastrointestinal bleeding
- D. Targeted temperature management at 36 °C
- E. Uncontrolled shock with rising lactate and escalating vasopressor doses

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** ESICM suggests low-dose EN during therapeutic hypothermia/TTM, with advancement after rewarming. Temperature control is not a reason to withhold EN.

**Why not the others:**
- A – GRV > 500 mL/6 h is a reason to delay EN (and start prokinetics).
- B – overt bowel ischaemia is a contraindication.
- C – active upper GI bleeding delays EN until haemostasis.
- E – uncontrolled shock is the main contraindication.

**Ref:** Reintam Blaser A, Intensive Care Med 2017.

</details>

**5.** Regarding energy expenditure and energy targets in ICU patients:

- A. Indirect calorimetry is the recommended method to determine energy expenditure in ventilated patients. — T / F
- B. When indirect calorimetry is not available, VCO₂ from the ventilator is preferable to predictive equations. — T / F
- C. During the early acute phase, ESPEN recommends providing more than 100 % of measured energy expenditure to prevent an energy deficit. — T / F
- D. Predictive equations such as 25 kcal/kg/day are accurate within ±10 % in most ICU patients. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – ESPEN grade B; IC measures VO₂ and VCO₂ (Weir equation).
- B (True) – EE ≈ VCO₂ × 8.19; VO₂ from a pulmonary artery catheter is another option.
- C (False) – early hypocaloric nutrition ≤ 70 % of EE; progress to 80–100 % after day 3; overfeeding is harmful.
- D (False) – equations are inaccurate in a large proportion of patients (over- and underestimation).

**Ref:** Singer P, Clin Nutr 2019; Singer P, Clin Nutr 2023.

</details>

**6.** A 45-year-old woman with alcohol use disorder and a BMI of 16 kg/m² is admitted with aspiration pneumonia. On day 2 of enteral feeding her phosphate falls from 1.0 to 0.45 mmol/L. Which management is best supported by randomised evidence?

- A. Continue full feeding and give phosphate only if the level falls below 0.3 mmol/L
- B. Stop feeding completely for 5 days
- C. Switch to parenteral nutrition at the same dose
- D. Increase the caloric target to correct the malnutrition faster
- E. Restrict caloric intake for 48 hours, replace phosphate, then increase gradually

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** This is refeeding hypophosphataemia. In Doig's RCT (n = 339), protocolised caloric restriction for 48 h, with electrolyte replacement and then gradual return to target, reduced 60-day mortality compared with continued standard intake.

**Why not the others:**
- A – waiting for severe hypophosphataemia risks respiratory muscle weakness, arrhythmias and haemolysis.
- B – prolonged starvation worsens the deficit; restriction is temporary.
- C – the route does not prevent refeeding; PN can cause it as well.
- D – increasing intake aggravates the intracellular shift of phosphate.

**Ref:** Doig GS, Lancet Respir Med 2015.

</details>

**7.** Regarding the route of nutrition in critically ill adults:

- A. In the CALORIES trial, early parenteral nutrition increased infectious complications compared with early enteral nutrition. — T / F
- B. In NUTRIREA-2, early full-dose enteral nutrition in ventilated patients with shock was associated with more bowel ischaemia than parenteral nutrition. — T / F
- C. In EPaNIC, starting supplemental parenteral nutrition on day 8 rather than within 48 hours led to fewer ICU infections. — T / F
- D. Commercial parenteral nutrition bags contain all required trace elements and vitamins. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – at similar calories, infections and 30-day mortality (33.1 vs 34.1 %) did not differ.
- B (True) – bowel ischaemia 2 vs < 1 %; also more vomiting, diarrhoea and colonic pseudo-obstruction with EN.
- C (True) – infections 22.8 vs 26.2 %, and faster discharge alive with late PN.
- D (False) – vitamins and trace elements must be added daily.

**Ref:** Harvey SE, NEJM 2014; Reignier J, Lancet 2018; Casaer MP, NEJM 2011.

</details>

**8.** A 52-year-old man has been in the ICU for 5 days after polytrauma. He tolerates nasogastric feeding at 40 mL/h; gastric residual volume is 200 mL over 4 hours. What should be done with the feeding?

- A. Stop EN and start parenteral nutrition
- B. Continue and advance EN towards target
- C. Switch to post-pyloric feeding immediately
- D. Stop EN for 24 hours
- E. Start erythromycin and metoclopramide for 7 days

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** The ESPEN/ESICM threshold for gastric intolerance is a GRV > 500 mL/6 h; 200 mL/4 h is acceptable, so EN should be continued and advanced.

**Why not the others:**
- A – there is no EN failure; switching to PN is unnecessary.
- C – post-pyloric feeding is reserved for persistent intolerance despite prokinetics or high aspiration risk.
- D – stopping feeding for a normal residual adds to the energy deficit.
- E – prokinetics are not indicated; when used, limit them to 24–72 h because of tachyphylaxis and QT effects.

**Ref:** Reintam Blaser A, Intensive Care Med 2017; Singer P, Clin Nutr 2019.

</details>

**9.** A 66-year-old woman has been ventilated for 4 days in a medical ICU. Enteral feeding meets only 30 % of the target despite prokinetics, and she was well nourished before admission. According to ESPEN, what is the most appropriate next step regarding parenteral nutrition?

- A. Start full-dose parenteral nutrition from day 1 of admission in all patients
- B. Never use supplemental parenteral nutrition in the ICU
- C. Consider supplemental parenteral nutrition, weighed on a case-by-case basis, after day 3–7 if EN remains insufficient
- D. Start parenteral glutamine to improve gut tolerance
- E. Give supplemental PN only once albumin falls below 25 g/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** ESPEN suggests supplemental PN when EN fails to cover the target after day 3–7, assessed individually. Early supplemental PN in well-nourished patients is not beneficial (EPaNIC).

**Why not the others:**
- A – early PN increased infections and delayed recovery in EPaNIC.
- B – PN is appropriate when EN is persistently insufficient or contraindicated.
- D – glutamine is not recommended in unstable/complex ICU patients (REDOXS harm).
- E – albumin reflects inflammation and capillary leak, not nutritional status.

**Ref:** Singer P, Clin Nutr 2019; Singer P, Clin Nutr 2023; Casaer MP, NEJM 2011.

</details>

**10.** Regarding the metabolic response to critical illness:

- A. Endogenous glucose production in the early phase is not fully suppressed by exogenous nutrition. — T / F
- B. Overfeeding increases CO₂ production and may prolong weaning. — T / F
- C. Serum albumin is a reliable marker of nutritional status in the ICU. — T / F
- D. Hypertriglyceridaemia may result from high-dose propofol and parenteral lipids. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – stress hormones and insulin resistance maintain gluconeogenesis, so feeding adds to endogenous energy (risk of overfeeding).
- B (True) – carbohydrate excess and lipogenesis raise VCO₂ and minute ventilation demand.
- C (False) – albumin falls with inflammation, dilution and capillary leak; it is not a nutrition marker.
- D (True) – monitor triglycerides with propofol and IV lipid emulsions.

**Ref:** Singer P, Clin Nutr 2019; Casaer MP, NEJM 2011.

</details>

**11.** A 40-year-old man with severe traumatic brain injury has been ventilated for 36 hours and is haemodynamically stable. He has vomited twice on nasogastric feeding at 20 mL/h, with gastric residual volumes of 600 mL over 6 hours despite 48 hours of IV erythromycin. Which is the most appropriate next step?

- A. Stop all nutrition for 7 days
- B. Increase the gastric feeding rate to overcome the ileus
- C. Switch to total parenteral nutrition at full target
- D. Continue erythromycin indefinitely
- E. Insert a post-pyloric feeding tube and continue enteral nutrition

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Persistent gastric intolerance (GRV > 500 mL/6 h, vomiting) despite prokinetics is the indication for post-pyloric feeding (ESPEN/ESICM), which preserves enteral nutrition and reduces aspiration risk.

**Why not the others:**
- A – prolonged starvation is harmful; EN can usually continue post-pylorically.
- B – increasing the gastric rate increases aspiration risk.
- C – PN is considered if EN fails by any route; early full-target PN is not beneficial.
- D – prokinetics lose efficacy after 48–72 h (tachyphylaxis) and prolong QT.

**Ref:** Singer P, Clin Nutr 2019; Reintam Blaser A, Intensive Care Med 2017.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Describe how you would plan nutrition during the first week for a 60-year-old ventilated patient with septic shock who is now stabilised on noradrenaline.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- **Screening/assessment:** risk of malnutrition after 48 h (NUTRIC, GLIM), weight history, muscle mass; baseline phosphate, K⁺, Mg²⁺.
- **Timing:** start **low-dose gastric EN within 48 h** once shock is controlled (stable or falling vasopressor dose, falling lactate); delay only in uncontrolled shock, hypoxaemia/acidosis, active upper GI bleed, bowel ischaemia, ACS, GRV > 500 mL/6 h.
- **Energy:** ≤ 70 % of EE in days 1–2, then 80–100 % of **measured EE** (indirect calorimetry; VCO₂ × 8.19 if unavailable); count propofol, citrate, glucose. NUTRIREA-3: low early intake (6 kcal/kg, low protein) shortened time to ICU discharge readiness with fewer GI complications.
- **Protein:** progressive increase towards ~1.2–1.3 g/kg/day (see D2).
- **Intolerance:** GRV > 500 mL/6 h → erythromycin ± metoclopramide 24–72 h → post-pyloric tube.
- **PN:** not early in well-nourished patients (EPaNIC); supplemental PN if EN < 60 % after day 3–7; early progressive PN only if severely malnourished and EN impossible.
- **Monitoring:** glucose (insulin > 10 mmol/L), phosphate daily (refeeding → restrict calories 48 h), triglycerides, liver tests, GI symptoms.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Summarise the key randomised trials that have shaped current recommendations on early calorie intake in the ICU.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- **EPaNIC (2011):** late (day 8) vs early (48 h) supplemental PN – late PN: fewer infections (22.8 vs 26.2 %), faster discharge, less KRT.
- **EDEN (2012)/PermiT (2015):** trophic or permissive underfeeding vs full feeding – no difference in mortality.
- **CALORIES (2014)/NUTRIREA-2 (2018):** EN vs PN – equal mortality; PN not more infectious if not overfed; full early EN in shock → more bowel ischaemia, vomiting, diarrhoea.
- **TARGET (2018):** energy-dense EN delivered more calories without benefit.
- **NUTRIREA-3 (2023):** low vs standard calorie/protein in shock – same mortality (41.3 vs 42.8 %), faster readiness for discharge, fewer GI and liver complications.
- **Synthesis:** early progressive EN, hypocaloric in the acute phase, avoid overfeeding, measure EE (ESPEN 2023).

</details>


## W21 · D2 – Protein in critical illness: EFFORT Protein, PRECISe, TARGET Protein

**1.** A 67-year-old man (80 kg) with pneumonia and septic shock is ventilated on day 4. He has KDIGO stage 2 AKI (not on KRT) with urea rising from 12 to 24 mmol/L. The dietitian proposes increasing protein to 2.0 g/kg/day. What is the most appropriate response?

- A. Agree – high protein reduces mortality in AKI
- B. Stop all protein until kidney function recovers
- C. Restrict protein to 0.6 g/kg/day to delay the need for dialysis
- D. Aim for about 1.0–1.3 g/kg/day, increased progressively, and avoid high-dose protein
- E. Start CRRT so that 2.0 g/kg/day can be given

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** In the EFFORT Protein AKI analysis, high protein in patients with AKI not on KRT was associated with higher mortality, and the 2026 synthesis of protein trials suggests a progressive increase to ~1.2 g/kg/day. ESPEN advises against restricting protein to avoid KRT.

**Why not the others:**
- A – no trial shows benefit; EFFORT suggested harm in AKI.
- B – stopping protein worsens the catabolic deficit.
- C – protein restriction to delay dialysis is not recommended.
- E – KRT is not started to allow a nutritional target.

**Ref:** Stoppe C, Crit Care 2023; Chapple LS, Crit Care 2026; Fiaccadori E, Clin Nutr 2021.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the EFFORT Protein trial:

- A. It compared prescribed protein of ≥ 2.2 g/kg/day with ≤ 1.2 g/kg/day in ventilated patients with high nutritional risk. — T / F
- B. High protein significantly shortened the time to discharge alive. — T / F
- C. Patients with acute kidney injury at baseline appeared to be harmed by higher protein. — T / F
- D. The trial was double-blinded with isocaloric formulas. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – delivered 1.6 vs 0.9 g/kg/day; 1,329 patients in 85 ICUs.
- B (False) – 46.1 vs 50.2 % discharged alive by day 60 (HR 0.91, 95 % CI 0.77–1.07).
- C (True) – the AKI and SOFA ≥ 9 subgroups had slower discharge and (AKI) higher mortality with high protein.
- D (False) – it was pragmatic, open-label and registry-based; PRECISe was the double-blind trial.

**Ref:** Heyland DK, Lancet 2023; Stoppe C, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

**3.** In the double-blind PRECISe trial, ventilated patients received isocaloric enteral nutrition with either 2.0 or 1.3 g/kg/day of protein. What was the main finding?

- A. High protein improved 6-minute walk distance and was adopted as standard
- B. High protein reduced 180-day mortality
- C. High protein reduced ICU-acquired weakness measured by MRC sum score
- D. There was no difference in any outcome, including GI tolerance
- E. High protein led to worse health-related quality of life (EQ-5D-5L) over 180 days

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** The primary outcome, EQ-5D-5L health utility over 180 days, was lower with high protein (mean difference −0.05; 95 % CI −0.10 to −0.01). GI intolerance was also more frequent (9 vs 6 %).

**Why not the others:**
- A – the 6-minute walk difference was not adjusted for multiplicity and the trial did not support high protein.
- B – 180-day mortality was 42 vs 38 % (not significant, numerically worse).
- C – muscle strength was not improved.
- D – GI intolerance and quality of life differed.

**Ref:** Bels JLM (PRECISe), Lancet 2024.

</details>

**4.** Regarding muscle wasting and protein metabolism in critical illness:

- A. Rectus femoris cross-sectional area may decrease by almost 20 % within the first 10 days. — T / F
- B. Muscle loss is greater in patients with multiorgan failure. — T / F
- C. A positive nitrogen balance can usually be achieved in the first days of septic shock by giving 2.5 g/kg/day of protein. — T / F
- D. Amino acids in excess of what can be used for synthesis are oxidised, increasing urea production. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – 17.7 % by day 10 (Puthucheary, JAMA 2013).
- B (True) – wasting was faster with more organ failures.
- C (False) – anabolic resistance means nitrogen balance remains negative in the acute phase regardless of intake.
- D (True) – rising urea and urea:creatinine ratio occur with high protein (seen in EFFORT and PRECISe).

**Ref:** Puthucheary ZA, JAMA 2013; Heyland DK, Lancet 2023.

</details>

**5.** A 55-year-old woman (90 kg) with septic shock and AKI is on continuous veno-venous haemodiafiltration with an effluent dose of 25 mL/kg/h. Her shock has resolved by day 5. What daily protein intake is best supported by the ESPEN renal guideline?

- A. 0.6 g/kg/day to limit urea generation
- B. 0.8 g/kg/day
- C. 3.0 g/kg/day
- D. Up to 1.7 g/kg/day to compensate for amino acid losses
- E. Protein should be withheld while on CRRT

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** CRRT removes ~10–20 g of amino acids per day. ESPEN recommends 1.0–1.5 g/kg/day on KRT and up to 1.7 g/kg/day for hypercatabolic patients on CRRT; in EFFORT patients already on KRT were not harmed by higher protein.

**Why not the others:**
- A, B – protein restriction is not recommended in AKI, and even less on CRRT.
- C – 3.0 g/kg exceeds any recommendation.
- E – withholding protein aggravates catabolism.

**Ref:** Fiaccadori E, Clin Nutr 2021; Stoppe C, Crit Care 2023.

</details>

**6.** A 50-year-old man (height 175 cm, weight 130 kg, BMI 42 kg/m²) is ventilated for pneumonia. Ideal body weight is ~71 kg. According to ESPEN, what is the appropriate daily protein target once he is stable?

- A. About 1.3 g/kg of adjusted body weight (~120 g/day)
- B. 1.3 g/kg of actual body weight (~170 g/day)
- C. 2.5 g/kg of actual body weight
- D. 0.5 g/kg of ideal body weight
- E. Protein should be avoided in obesity to promote fat loss

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** ESPEN uses adjusted body weight in obesity: IBW + ⅓ × (actual − IBW) = 71 + ⅓ × 59 ≈ 91 kg, and 1.3 × 91 ≈ 118 g/day – well below dosing by actual weight. ASPEN instead uses 2.0–2.5 g/kg IBW with hypocaloric energy.

**Why not the others:**
- B – actual weight overestimates lean mass and protein needs.
- C – 2.5 g/kg applies to ideal (not actual) body weight in ASPEN.
- D – too low; worsens muscle loss.
- E – obese patients lose lean mass and need protein.

**EDIC trap:** In obesity, adjust the **weight** you use, not just the dose per kg.

**Ref:** Singer P, Clin Nutr 2023; Compher C, JPEN 2022.

</details>

**7.** Regarding the TARGET Protein trial (2025):

- A. It was a cluster-randomised crossover trial in Australian and New Zealand ICUs. — T / F
- B. Augmented protein increased days alive and free of hospital at day 90. — T / F
- C. The intervention arms differed markedly in energy delivery. — T / F
- D. Augmented protein shortened ICU length of stay. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

**Ref:** Summers MJ (TARGET Protein), JAMA 2025.

</details>

**8.** A 60-year-old woman is admitted with septic shock and is ventilated. Which protein strategy for the first week best reflects the evidence available in 2026?

- A. Give 2.0–2.5 g/kg/day from day 1 to prevent muscle wasting
- B. Start low once haemodynamically stable and increase progressively over about 5 days to ~1.2–1.3 g/kg/day

- D. Give IV amino acids 2 g/kg/day for kidney protection
- E. Titrate protein to achieve a positive nitrogen balance by day 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Three RCTs (EFFORT Protein, PRECISe, TARGET Protein) found no benefit and possible harm with higher protein; ESPEN 2023 recommends 1.3 g/kg/day delivered progressively, and a 2026 synthesis suggests a ceiling of ~1.2 g/kg/day during the acute phase.

**Why not the others:**
- A – high early doses did not improve outcomes and worsened quality of life in PRECISe.
- C – prolonged protein deprivation is not recommended; NUTRIREA-3 restricted only for 7 days at 0.2–0.4 g/kg.
- D – IV amino acids reduced AKI after cardiac surgery (PROTECTION) but are not a nutrition strategy in sepsis.
- E – a positive nitrogen balance is not achievable early (anabolic resistance).

**Ref:** Singer P, Clin Nutr 2023; Chapple LS, Crit Care 2026.

**10.** Which statement about the EFFORT Protein, PRECISe and TARGET Protein trials is LEAST accurate?

**11.** A 72-year-old woman with cirrhosis (Child–Pugh C) and grade 2 hepatic encephalopathy is admitted to the ICU with variceal bleeding, now controlled. Enteral feeding is planned. What protein intake is recommended?

- A. Protein restriction to < 0.5 g/kg/day until encephalopathy resolves

- C. About 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day without restriction for encephalopathy
- D. 3 g/kg/day to reverse sarcopenia
- E. Only branched-chain amino acids parenterally

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

- E – BCAA-enriched formulas may be used in protein-intolerant patients but are not the standard route or sole source.

**Ref:** Singer P, Clin Nutr 2019; Fiaccadori E, Clin Nutr 2021.

**O1. (open)** Summarise the evidence from the large randomised trials of protein dose in critical illness and give a practical recommendation for 2026.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- **Rationale:** rapid muscle wasting (−17.7 % rectus femoris by day 10), observational associations of higher protein with survival – confounded by illness severity; anabolic resistance, ureagenesis, autophagy suppression as counter-arguments.
- **EFFORT Protein (2023, n = 1,329):** 1.6 vs 0.9 g/kg – no difference in time to discharge alive (46.1 vs 50.2 %), 60-day mortality 34.6 vs 32.1 %; harm in AKI and SOFA ≥ 9.
- **PRECISe (2024, n = 935, double-blind):** 2.0 vs 1.3 g/kg – worse EQ-5D-5L over 180 days, more GI intolerance.
- **TARGET Protein (2025, n ≈ 3,400):** 1.12 vs 0.69 g/kg IBW – no difference in days alive and out of hospital.
