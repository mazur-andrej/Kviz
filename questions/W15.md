## W15 · D1 – Traumatic brain injury: ICP, CPP, Brain Trauma Foundation, SIBICC

**1.** A 34-year-old man with severe TBI (post-resuscitation GCS 6, frontal contusions) has a parenchymal ICP monitor. He is sedated, head up 30°, normothermic, PaCO₂ 39 mmHg. ICP is 26 mmHg (sustained) and MAP, zeroed at the tragus, is 82 mmHg. Which statement is most appropriate?

- A. CPP is 108 mmHg; no further action is required
- B. CPP is 56 mmHg; ICP is above the BTF threshold and tier-1 measures (e.g. osmotherapy bolus, deeper sedation) are indicated
- C. CPP is 56 mmHg; immediate bifrontal decompressive craniectomy is the next step
- D. ICP is acceptable because the BTF treatment threshold is 25 mmHg
- E. CPP is 56 mmHg; hyperventilate to PaCO₂ 25 mmHg for the next 24 h

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** CPP = MAP − ICP = 82 − 26 = 56 mmHg, below the BTF target of 60–70 mmHg, and a sustained ICP of 26 mmHg exceeds the BTF 4th-edition treatment threshold of 22 mmHg. Tier-0 measures are already in place, so the next step is SIBICC tier 1: deeper analgesia/sedation, an osmotherapy bolus (mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg or hypertonic saline), CSF drainage if an EVD is present and CPP 60–70 mmHg.

**Why not the others:**
- A – CPP is MAP minus ICP, not the sum; 108 mmHg is an arithmetic error.
- C – early bifrontal craniectomy for diffuse injury worsened functional outcome in DECRA; DC is a tier-3 option after surgical lesions are excluded.
- D – the BTF threshold is 22 mmHg (25 mmHg was the RESCUEicp entry criterion).
- E – prolonged hyperventilation to ≤ 25 mmHg is not recommended (BTF IIB), especially in the first 24 h.

**Ref:** Carney N, Neurosurgery 2017; Hawryluk GWJ, Intensive Care Med 2019.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the Brain Trauma Foundation 4th-edition recommendations for severe TBI:

- A. Treatment of ICP above 22 mmHg is recommended because values above this level are associated with higher mortality. — T / F
- B. Corticosteroids may be considered to reduce ICP in patients with large contusions. — T / F
- C. Systolic blood pressure should be kept at or above 110 mmHg in patients aged 15–49 years or older than 70 years. — T / F
- D. Prophylactic hyperventilation to PaCO₂ ≤ 25 mmHg is recommended during the first 24 hours to prevent ICP elevation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – BTF 4th edition (Level IIB) recommends treating ICP > 22 mmHg, the value above which mortality rises (moved from 20 mmHg).
- B (False) – corticosteroids are **not recommended** in TBI (Level I): CRASH showed higher 2-week mortality (21.1 vs 17.9 %), whatever the lesion type.
- C (True) – SBP ≥ 110 mmHg at 15–49 and > 70 years; ≥ 100 mmHg at 50–69 years (Level III).
- D (False) – prolonged prophylactic hyperventilation to PaCO₂ ≤ 25 mmHg is **not recommended** (IIB) and should be avoided in the first 24 h, when CBF is often critically reduced; it is only a brief bridge for herniation.

**EDIC trap:** older MCQs quote ICP 20 mmHg – BTF 4th edition uses 22 mmHg.

**Ref:** Carney N, Neurosurgery 2017; CRASH Trial Collaborators, Lancet 2004.

</details>

**3.** A 25-year-old woman with diffuse TBI and no mass lesion has ICP > 20 mmHg for more than 15 minutes within a 1-hour period on day 1, despite first-tier therapy. The neurosurgeon proposes an early bifrontotemporoparietal decompressive craniectomy. Based on the DECRA trial, compared with continued medical therapy, this operation:

- A. halves 6-month mortality
- B. improves 6-month extended Glasgow Outcome Scale
- C. lowers ICP and shortens ICU stay, but increases the proportion with unfavourable outcome
- D. has no effect on ICP
- E. reduces the proportion of survivors in a vegetative state

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** DECRA (n = 155) randomised diffuse TBI with early, moderate ICP elevation (> 20 mmHg for > 15 min within 1 h, < 72 h) to bifrontotemporoparietal DC or medical care. Surgery lowered ICP and shortened ventilation and ICU stay, but unfavourable GOS-E at 6 months was more frequent (70 vs 51 %, OR 2.21).

**Why not the others:**
- A – mortality was similar (~19 vs 18 %); halving of mortality (26.9 vs 48.9 %) was seen in RESCUEicp, in late refractory ICP.
- B – GOS-E was worse, not better, with surgery.
- D – ICP fell significantly after DC.
- E – reducing vegetative survivors is not a DC effect; RESCUEicp actually increased the vegetative state proportion.

**EDIC trap:** DECRA = early, bifrontal, diffuse injury, worse function; RESCUEicp = late, refractory ICP, better survival with more disability.

**Ref:** Cooper DJ, N Engl J Med 2011; Hutchinson PJ, N Engl J Med 2016.

</details>

**4.** A 45-year-old man with severe TBI and an EVD has an ICP of 27 mmHg. All of the following belong to tier 1 or tier 2 of the SIBICC ICP algorithm EXCEPT:

- A. Mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg as an intermittent bolus
- B. Drainage of CSF via the external ventricular drain
- C. Mild hypocapnia with PaCO₂ 32–35 mmHg
- D. Active cooling to 33 °C
- E. A trial of neuromuscular blockade

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Active cooling to 33 °C does not belong to tiers 1–2. SIBICC places only mild hypothermia (35–36 °C) in tier 3 and lists routine cooling below 36 °C among measures not to use. Eurotherm3235 showed worse outcome when 32–35 °C hypothermia was used to treat ICP > 20 mmHg (favourable GOS-E 26 vs 37 %).

**Why not the others:**
- A – intermittent mannitol 0.25–1 g/kg is a tier-1 measure (continuous infusion is discouraged).
- B – CSF drainage via an EVD is tier 1.
- C – mild hypocapnia (PaCO₂ 32–35 mmHg) is tier 2; < 30 mmHg is discouraged.
- E – a neuromuscular blockade test dose, then infusion if effective, is tier 2.

**EDIC trap:** temperature control in tiers 0–2 means avoiding fever (36–37.5 °C), not therapeutic hypothermia.

**Ref:** Hawryluk GWJ, Intensive Care Med 2019; Andrews PJD, N Engl J Med 2015.

</details>

**5.** Regarding the RESCUEicp trial of decompressive craniectomy for traumatic intracranial hypertension:

- A. Patients were enrolled with ICP > 25 mmHg for 1–12 hours despite stage 1 and 2 measures. — T / F
- B. Six-month mortality was 26.9 % with surgery vs 48.9 % with medical care. — T / F
- C. Surgery increased the proportion of survivors in a vegetative state or with lower severe disability. — T / F
- D. Surgery significantly increased the rate of upper severe disability or better at 6 months. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – inclusion was ICP > 25 mmHg for 1–12 h despite stage 1 and 2 measures: late, refractory intracranial hypertension.
- B (True) – 6-month mortality 26.9 % with DC vs 48.9 % with medical care (including barbiturates).
- C (True) – vegetative state 8.5 vs 2.1 % and lower severe disability 21.9 vs 14.4 %: more survivors, many dependent.
- D (False) – upper severe disability or better was 42.8 vs 34.6 % at 6 months, **not significant**; the difference became significant only at 12 months (45.4 vs 32.4 %).

**EDIC trap:** RESCUEicp is a trade-off of survival for disability – discuss expected outcome and the patient's values with the family before surgery.

**Ref:** Hutchinson PJ, N Engl J Med 2016.

</details>

**6.** A 52-year-old woman with severe TBI is on day 3 in the ICU. She is not bleeding; Hb has drifted to 8.4 g/dL. ICP and PbtO₂ are not monitored. Based on current evidence, the most appropriate transfusion approach is:

- A. Transfuse to maintain Hb ≥ 9 g/dL
- B. Transfuse only if Hb falls below 7 g/dL, as in general ICU patients
- C. Transfuse to Hb ≥ 10 g/dL, which significantly improved outcome in HEMOTION
- D. Start erythropoietin instead of transfusion
- E. Transfuse only if PbtO₂ falls below 20 mmHg

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** TRAIN (n = 820 acute brain injury: TBI, SAH, ICH) compared transfusion triggers of 9 vs 7 g/dL. The liberal strategy reduced unfavourable 6-month neurological outcome (62.6 vs 73.5 %, RR 0.85) and cerebral ischaemia. Her Hb of 8.4 g/dL is below 9 g/dL, so transfusion is reasonable without waiting for neuromonitoring.

**Why not the others:**
- B – the "7 for everyone" message comes from general ICU trials (TRICC, TRISS) and does not apply to acute brain injury.
- C – HEMOTION (10 vs 7 g/dL, n = 742) showed only a non-significant trend (68.4 vs 73.5 %).
- D – erythropoietin is not a substitute for transfusion and has no role in correcting anaemia acutely.
- E – PbtO₂ is not monitored, and guiding transfusion by PbtO₂ remains investigational.

**EDIC trap:** "restrictive transfusion is safe in all ICU patients" is false for acute brain injury.

**Ref:** Taccone FS, JAMA 2024; Turgeon AF, N Engl J Med 2024.

</details>

**7.** Regarding hyperosmolar therapy in traumatic intracranial hypertension:

- A. Mannitol is best given as a continuous infusion to avoid rebound intracranial hypertension. — T / F
- B. In the COBI trial, a continuous infusion of 20 % saline improved 6-month neurological outcome. — T / F
- C. Mannitol can cause hypovolaemia through osmotic diuresis, and a rising osmolar gap suggests accumulation. — T / F
- D. Hypertonic saline is contraindicated once serum sodium exceeds 145 mmol/L. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – mannitol is given as an intermittent **bolus** (0.25–1 g/kg over 15–20 min); SIBICC explicitly discourages continuous infusion, which promotes accumulation and rebound oedema.
- B (False) – COBI (n = 370) found **no difference** in 6-month GOS-E with continuous 20 % saline targeting Na 145–155 mmol/L (adjusted OR 1.02), nor in intracranial hypertension.
- C (True) – osmotic diuresis can cause hypovolaemia and hypotension; a rising osmolar gap (> ~20 mOsm/kg) suggests mannitol accumulation.
- D (False) – sodium is commonly allowed to rise to ~155–160 mmol/L during osmotherapy; Na > 145 is not a contraindication.

**EDIC trap:** both mannitol and hypertonic saline lower ICP, but neither has proven superiority for long-term outcome.

**Ref:** Hawryluk GWJ, Intensive Care Med 2019; Roquilly A, JAMA 2021.

</details>

**8.** A 40-year-old man arrives 90 minutes after a fall. GCS is 12, pupils equal and reactive; CT shows a small subdural haematoma. Regarding tranexamic acid (TXA), the most appropriate statement is:

- A. TXA is not indicated because CRASH-3 showed benefit only when GCS ≤ 8
- B. TXA should be given only if the INR is > 1.5
- C. A single 2 g bolus is recommended at any time within 24 h
- D. TXA 1 g over 10 min followed by 1 g over 8 h is indicated because he is within 3 hours
- E. TXA is contraindicated in TBI because it increases vascular occlusive events

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** He has mild–moderate TBI (GCS 12) with intracranial blood and is within 3 hours of injury. In CRASH-3, TXA 1 g over 10 min then 1 g over 8 h reduced head-injury death in GCS 9–15 (RR 0.78), with greater benefit the earlier it was given and no excess of vascular occlusive events.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the reverse: no benefit was seen in severe TBI (GCS 3–8) or with bilateral unreactive pupils.
- B – TXA is antifibrinolytic and its indication does not depend on INR.
- C – the regimen is 1 g + 1 g over 8 h, and benefit was limited to treatment within 3 h.
- E – CRASH-3 found no increase in vascular occlusive events.

**EDIC trap:** CRASH-3 overall result in patients treated ≤ 3 h was not significant (RR 0.94); the benefit is in the mild–moderate subgroup.

**Ref:** CRASH-3 trial collaborators, Lancet 2019.

</details>

**9.** Regarding ventilation targets in patients with acute brain injury (ESICM consensus 2020):

- A. A PaO₂ target of 80–120 mmHg is recommended. — T / F
- B. PaCO₂ of 35–45 mmHg is recommended in patients without raised ICP. — T / F
- C. PEEP must be avoided in every patient with raised ICP. — T / F
- D. Brief hyperventilation is contraindicated even during transtentorial herniation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – ESICM consensus recommends PaO₂ 80–120 mmHg, avoiding both hypoxaemia and hyperoxia.
- B (True) – PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg is the target when ICP is not raised.
- C (False) – PEEP can be used in raised ICP, with monitoring; it does not usually raise ICP meaningfully if MAP and PaCO₂ are kept stable.
- D (False) – brief hyperventilation is an accepted **rescue** bridge during transtentorial herniation, while definitive treatment is arranged; only prolonged prophylactic hyperventilation is discouraged.

**EDIC trap:** lung-protective ventilation is recommended when there is no ICP concern; with raised ICP, individualise and monitor rather than avoiding PEEP.

**Ref:** Robba C, Intensive Care Med 2020.

</details>

**10.** A 22-year-old man with severe TBI has ICP persistently 25–28 mmHg on day 4 despite tier 1 and tier 2 measures; a repeat CT shows no new surgical lesion. Which of the following is LEAST appropriate?

- A. Secondary large fronto-temporo-parietal decompressive craniectomy
- B. Barbiturate coma after a test dose shows ICP response, with haemodynamic monitoring
- C. High-dose methylprednisolone
- D. Mild hypothermia to 35–36 °C
- E. A frank discussion with the family about likely survival with disability after surgery

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Corticosteroids are contraindicated in TBI (BTF Level I). In CRASH (n ≈ 10 000), methylprednisolone increased 2-week mortality (21.1 vs 17.9 %), and SIBICC lists steroids among measures not to use at any tier.

**Why not the others:**
- A – secondary large fronto-temporo-parietal DC for late refractory ICP is a tier-3 option; BTF 2020 update recommends it to reduce mortality (IIA).
- B – barbiturate coma is tier 3, used only if a test dose lowers ICP and with close haemodynamic monitoring.
- D – mild hypothermia 35–36 °C is a tier-3 option (unlike 32–35 °C in Eurotherm3235).
- E – RESCUEicp showed more survivors with severe disability after DC, so an honest discussion of expected outcome is essential.

**EDIC trap:** "LEAST appropriate" stems often hide a harmful but familiar drug among legitimate tier-3 options.

**Ref:** Carney N, Neurosurgery 2017; Hawryluk GWJ, Intensive Care Med 2019.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 30-year-old man with severe TBI (GCS 5, diffuse swelling, compressed basal cisterns) has an EVD. Despite sedation and head-up positioning, ICP is 30 mmHg with MAP 85 mmHg. Describe your stepwise management using the SIBICC tiers, including targets and the measures you would avoid.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Calculate CPP = 85 − 30 = 55 mmHg (target 60–70); treat ICP > 22 mmHg (BTF).

## W15 · D2 – Aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage: rebleeding, aneurysm securing, DCI, hydrocephalus, sodium

**1.** A 48-year-old woman presents 3 hours after a sudden severe headache that peaked within seconds during exercise. She is alert (GCS 15) without focal deficit. A non-contrast CT performed 4 hours after onset is normal. What is the most appropriate next step?

- A. Lumbar puncture immediately, regardless of timing
- B. Lumbar puncture at least 12 hours after onset to look for xanthochromia
- C. Discharge with analgesia – subarachnoid haemorrhage is effectively ruled out
- D. Repeat non-contrast CT at 24 hours
- E. Start nimodipine empirically and admit to the ICU

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In neurologically intact patients, a modern non-contrast CT performed **within 6 hours** of headache onset and read by an experienced radiologist has a sensitivity close to 100 % for SAH (Perry, BMJ 2011). AHA 2023 accepts that SAH is effectively excluded in this situation, so no LP is needed.

**Why not the others:**
- A – LP is needed only if CT is performed later than 6 h (or is of doubtful quality); early LP gives false positives from traumatic taps.
- B – waiting 12 h for xanthochromia applies when CT is negative but done > 6 h after onset.
- D – repeat CT adds nothing; sensitivity decreases with time.
- E – nimodipine is given only for confirmed aneurysmal SAH.

**EDIC trap:** the 6-hour rule applies to an alert patient with a good-quality scan; a CT at 10 h with a negative result still requires LP.

**Ref:** Perry JJ, BMJ 2011;343:d4277; Hoh BL, Stroke 2023.

</details>

**2.** Regarding grading of aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage:

- A. The WFNS scale is based on the Glasgow Coma Scale and the presence of a focal motor deficit. — T / F
- B. The modified Fisher scale is a clinical scale based on the level of consciousness. — T / F
- C. A modified Fisher grade 4 (thick cisternal blood with intraventricular haemorrhage) carries the highest risk of symptomatic vasospasm. — T / F
- D. Poor-grade patients should be graded definitively on admission, before any external ventricular drainage. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – WFNS I: GCS 15; II: 13–14 without deficit; III: 13–14 with motor deficit; IV: 7–12; V: 3–6.
- B (False) – the (modified) Fisher scale is **radiological** (CT thickness of SAH and presence of IVH).
- C (True) – grade 4 has ~40 % risk of symptomatic vasospasm/DCI.
- D (False) – grade after resuscitation and after treating hydrocephalus; poor grade on admission often improves after EVD and prognosis should be delayed.

**Ref:** Hoh BL, Stroke 2023; Treggiari MM, Neurocrit Care 2023.

</details>

**3.** A 55-year-old man with WFNS grade II aneurysmal SAH arrives at a neurovascular centre 2 hours after ictus. The anterior communicating aneurysm is suitable for both coiling and clipping. Which strategy is best supported by current evidence?

- A. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV, then 1 g every 8 hours until the aneurysm is secured
- B. Delayed clipping after day 14, when vasospasm has resolved
- C. Prophylactic hypervolaemic haemodilution before aneurysm treatment
- D. IV magnesium sulphate to prevent delayed cerebral ischaemia
- E. Endovascular coiling within 24 hours, together with oral nimodipine

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Rebleeding risk is highest in the first hours, so ESO 2026 suggests securing the aneurysm within 24 h. If both techniques are suitable in a good-grade patient, coiling is preferred (ISAT: death/dependency at 1 year 23.7 vs 30.6 %). Oral nimodipine 60 mg q4h for 21 days is the only DCI-preventive drug with high-quality evidence.

**Why not the others:**
- A – ULTRA: ultra-early TXA did not improve functional outcome (60 vs 64 % good outcome, NS); ESO recommends against routine antifibrinolytics.
- B – delayed surgery leaves the aneurysm exposed during the period of highest rebleeding risk.
- C – prophylactic hypervolaemia (triple-H) has no benefit and causes complications.
- D – MASH-2: magnesium did not improve outcome.

**Ref:** Vergouwen MDI, Eur Stroke J 2026; Molyneux A, Lancet 2002; Post R, Lancet 2021.

</details>

**4.** Regarding nimodipine in aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage:

- A. The usual regimen is 60 mg orally every 4 hours for 21 days. — T / F
- B. Its main benefit is a significant reduction in angiographic vasospasm. — T / F
- C. In the British aneurysm nimodipine trial, cerebral infarction was reduced from 33 % to 22 %. — T / F
- D. If it causes hypotension, the dose may be split into 30 mg every 2 hours. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – standard oral/NG regimen, started early, continued for 21 days.
- B (False) – nimodipine improves outcome **without** a proven effect on large-artery angiographic vasospasm; benefit is attributed to microvascular and neuroprotective effects.
- C (True) – BRANT: infarction 22 vs 33 %, poor outcome 20 vs 33 %.
- D (True) – smaller, more frequent doses limit hypotension; avoid stopping the drug.

**Ref:** Pickard JD, BMJ 1989; Vergouwen MDI, Eur Stroke J 2026.

</details>

**5.** A 60-year-old woman on day 6 after coiling of a ruptured MCA aneurysm becomes drowsy and develops new right arm weakness. All of the following are appropriate initial steps EXCEPT:

- A. Urgent CT to exclude hydrocephalus or rebleeding
- B. Check serum sodium and glucose
- C. Fluid restriction to 1 L/day because the serum sodium is 129 mmol/L
- D. Ensure euvolaemia and consider a trial of induced hypertension with noradrenaline
- E. CT angiography/perfusion and consideration of endovascular rescue if deficits persist

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** Hyponatraemia after SAH is often due to **cerebral salt wasting with hypovolaemia**; fluid restriction aggravates hypovolaemia and increases the risk of DCI. Treat with isotonic/hypertonic saline and consider fludrocortisone.

**Why not the others:**
- A – other causes of deterioration (hydrocephalus, rebleeding) must be excluded before diagnosing DCI.
- B – hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia and seizures mimic DCI.
- D – euvolaemia plus a cautious induced-hypertension trial is accepted practice in a secured aneurysm (AHA/NCS), although evidence is weak (HIMALAIA).
- E – angioplasty or intra-arterial vasodilators are rescue options for refractory symptomatic vasospasm.

**EDIC trap:** a new focal deficit between day 3 and 14 = DCI until proven otherwise, but the diagnosis requires the exclusion of other causes.

**Ref:** Hoh BL, Stroke 2023; Treggiari MM, Neurocrit Care 2023; Vergouwen MDI, Stroke 2010.

</details>

- B. Statins are recommended by the ESO 2026 guideline. — T / F
- C. Clazosentan reduced angiographic vasospasm without a convincing improvement in functional outcome. — T / F
- D. Prophylactic triple-H therapy is recommended in poor-grade patients. — T / F

- C (True) – CONSCIOUS trials: less angiographic vasospasm, but no improvement in functional outcome; REACT also negative.

**Ref:** Dorhout Mees SM, Lancet 2012; Vergouwen MDI, Eur Stroke J 2026.

</details>

**7.** A 42-year-old man with modified Fisher grade 4 aneurysmal SAH has had his aneurysm coiled on day 1. In the EARLYDRAIN trial, adding a lumbar CSF drain (5 mL/h) started within 72 hours to standard care:

- A. increased the rate of rebleeding
- B. had no effect on outcome but reduced shunt dependency
- C. reduced 6-month mortality by half
- D. reduced unfavourable outcome at 6 months (about 33 vs 45 %) and secondary infarctions
- E. was stopped early because of brain herniation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** EARLYDRAIN (JAMA Neurol 2023): unfavourable outcome (mRS 3–6) at 6 months 32.6 vs 44.8 % (RR 0.73), and secondary infarction 28.5 vs 39.9 %. Mortality was not significantly different.

**Why not the others:**
- A – lumbar drainage was started after aneurysm securing; no increase in rebleeding.
- B – the main effect was on functional outcome; shunt dependency was not the key result.
- C – mortality 13.2 vs 17.5 % (NS).
- E – the trial was completed; herniation was not a significant issue in selected patients without mass effect.

**EDIC trap:** despite this positive trial, ESO 2026 makes **no evidence-based recommendation** on lumbar drainage (single open-label trial) – know both facts.

**Ref:** Wolf S, JAMA Neurol 2023; Vergouwen MDI, Eur Stroke J 2026.

**8.** A 50-year-old woman on day 5 after SAH has an MCA mean flow velocity on transcranial Doppler of 210 cm/s with an extracranial ICA velocity of 30 cm/s.

- A. The Lindegaard ratio is about 7, consistent with severe vasospasm. — T / F
- B. An MCA velocity above 200 cm/s suggests severe vasospasm. — T / F
- C. A Lindegaard ratio below 3 with high MCA velocity suggests global hyperaemia rather than vasospasm. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

- C (True) – raised velocities in both MCA and ICA (ratio < 3) indicate hyperaemia (high flow), not narrowing.

**Ref:** Treggiari MM, Neurocrit Care 2023; Hoh BL, Stroke 2023.

**9.** A 63-year-old woman on day 4 after clipping of an aneurysm develops pulmonary oedema. Troponin is elevated, the ECG shows deep T-wave inversion, and echocardiography shows apical and mid-ventricular hypokinesia with an LVEF of 30 %. Coronary angiography is normal. The most likely diagnosis is:

- A. Neurogenic stunned myocardium (stress cardiomyopathy)
- B. Acute myocardial infarction due to plaque rupture
- C. Nimodipine-induced cardiomyopathy
- D. Fluid overload from triple-H therapy as the sole cause
- E. Myocarditis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** The catecholamine surge after SAH causes troponin release (20–40 %), ECG changes and Takotsubo-like regional wall motion abnormalities that do not follow a coronary territory. It is usually reversible within days to weeks; supportive treatment with inotropes (dobutamine or milrinone) may be needed if cardiac output falls.

**Why not the others:**
- B – normal coronary arteries argue against type 1 MI.
- C – nimodipine causes vasodilatation and hypotension, not cardiomyopathy.
- D – fluid overload may contribute to pulmonary oedema but does not explain regional wall motion abnormalities with troponin rise.
- E – no clinical context for myocarditis; the timing and pattern fit neurogenic stunning.

**Ref:** Hoh BL, Stroke 2023; Treggiari MM, Neurocrit Care 2023.

</details>

**10.** A 58-year-old woman with secured aneurysmal SAH is on day 3 with Hb 8.6 g/dL, no bleeding and no signs of DCI. Regarding red cell transfusion, which statement is most accurate?

- A. SAHaRA showed that a threshold of 10 g/dL significantly reduced 12-month unfavourable outcome
- B. A threshold of 7 g/dL has been proven superior in SAH
- C. Transfusion is contraindicated during the vasospasm window
- D. In SAHaRA, liberal (Hb < 10) vs restrictive (Hb < 8) transfusion did not significantly change 12-month unfavourable outcome (33.5 vs 37.7 %)
- E. Erythropoietin is the recommended alternative

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** SAHaRA (NEJM 2025, n = 725) found a non-significant difference (RR 0.88, 95 % CI 0.72–1.09). Together with TRAIN (which included SAH patients and favoured Hb 9 over 7 g/dL), a threshold of ~8–9 g/dL is reasonable; strict 7 g/dL is not supported in acute brain injury.

**Why not the others:**
