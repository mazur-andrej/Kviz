## W09 · D1 – ALS according to ERC 2025

**1.** A 62-year-old man in the ED develops ventricular fibrillation. He has received two biphasic shocks with 2 minutes of CPR after each; VF persists at the rhythm check. According to ERC 2025, the most appropriate next step is:

- A. Deliver the third shock, resume CPR, then give adrenaline 1 mg and amiodarone 300 mg
- B. Give lidocaine 100 mg before the third shock
- C. Give adrenaline 1 mg and amiodarone 300 mg now, then deliver the third shock
- D. Deliver three stacked shocks
- E. Stop CPR and check for a pulse for 30 seconds

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Both adrenaline and amiodarone are given **after the 3rd shock**. Stacked shocks only in a monitored arrest right at the start.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the PARAMEDIC2 trial:

- A. Among survivors, severe neurological impairment was more frequent with adrenaline. — T / F
- B. It was a double-blind placebo-controlled trial in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. — T / F
- C. 30-day survival was significantly higher with adrenaline. — T / F
- D. Survival with a favourable neurological outcome at discharge was significantly higher with adrenaline. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.** C: 3.2 vs 2.4%. D: 2.2 vs 1.9%, NS. A: 31.0 vs 17.8%.

</details>

**3.** In the ALPS trial of shock-refractory VF/pVT, compared with placebo:

- A. Neither drug significantly improved overall survival to discharge, but both were associated with higher survival in bystander-witnessed arrests
- B. Amiodarone increased mortality
- C. Lidocaine was superior to amiodarone
- D. Both drugs worsened neurological outcome
- E. Amiodarone significantly improved survival to hospital discharge in the overall population

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** 24.4% vs 23.7% vs 21.0%, NS; signal of benefit in witnessed arrest.

</details>

**4.** Regarding waveform capnography during CPR (ERC 2025):

- A. A persistent waveform helps exclude oesophageal intubation. — T / F
- B. A sudden fall in ETCO₂ may indicate ROSC. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.** B: ROSC causes a sudden rise in ETCO₂ (restored pulmonary blood flow). C, D: ETCO₂ should never be used alone for these decisions.

</details>

**10.** Which are among the "4 Hs and 4 Ts" reversible causes of cardiac arrest?

**O1. (open)** A 55-year-old man collapses in the ED; the monitor shows VF. Outline the ALS sequence for the first 10 minutes according to ERC 2025, including drug timing and what you would do if VF persists after three shocks.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- High-quality compressions with minimal interruptions; correct antero-lateral pad placement (lateral pad below the axilla, mid-axillary line).
- Single biphasic shock (first ≥ 150 J), immediately 2 min CPR without pulse check, then rhythm check; consider escalating energy if the device allows.
- Adrenaline 1 mg IV/IO after the 3rd shock, then every 3–5 min.
- Amiodarone 300 mg after the 3rd shock, 150 mg after the 5th (or lidocaine 100 mg + 50 mg).
- Refractory VF: confirm pad position, then consider vector change to antero-posterior; DSED not routinely recommended (DOSE VF small, stopped early).
- Highest FiO₂; intubation only by skilled operator with < 5 s pause, then 10 breaths/min with continuous compressions; waveform capnography.
- Actively search for 4Hs/4Ts (e.g. coronary thrombosis); consider ECPR as rescue in selected patients.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why PARAMEDIC2 is described as "positive" yet adrenaline's benefit remains controversial, and why ERC 2025 still recommends it.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Primary outcome 30-day survival was higher with adrenaline (3.2% vs 2.4%, adj. OR 1.47; NNT ~112).
- Survival to admission much higher (23.8% vs 8.0%) – adrenaline increases ROSC.
- Favourable neurological outcome (mRS ≤ 3) not significantly different (2.2% vs 1.9%).
- Severe neurological impairment among survivors more frequent (31.0% vs 17.8%).
- Mechanism: α-effect ↑ coronary perfusion pressure, but impaired cerebral microcirculation; rescues patients with severe hypoxic injury.
- ERC 2025 retains adrenaline: as early as possible in non-shockable rhythms, after the 3rd shock in VF/pVT.

</details>

**A.** Without ST elevation delay catheterisation; ERC-ESICM 2025 favours a biphasic whole-body CT.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 64-year-old woman is admitted comatose after OHCA (initial rhythm PEA, ROSC after 18 min). ECG shows no ST elevation. Outline your first-day ICU management according to ERC-ESICM 2025.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Intubate if not already; confirm with capnography; FiO₂ 1.0 until SpO₂ reliable, then SpO₂ 94–98%, PaO₂ 10–13 kPa, avoid hypoxaemia and hyperoxaemia.
- Normocapnia (PaCO₂ 4.7–6.0 kPa), Vt 6–8 mL/kg IBW; check PaCO₂ more often if hypothermic.
- MAP > 60–65 mmHg (SBP > 100 mmHg), fluids/noradrenaline/dobutamine as needed; avoid hypo- and hyperkalaemia.
- No ST elevation: delay coronary angiography unless acute occlusion strongly suspected (COACT, TOMAHAWK); biphasic whole-body CT including CTPA to look for non-cardiac causes and CPR injuries.
- Active fever prevention ≤ 37.5 °C; short-acting sedation.
- EEG for seizures; treat with levetiracetam/valproate, no prophylaxis.
- No routine corticosteroids, prophylactic antibiotics or NMB; standard glucose control.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why COACT and TOMAHAWK did not support immediate coronary angiography after OHCA without ST elevation, and how their populations differed.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- COACT: only shockable initial rhythm, unconscious, no STEMI; 90-day survival 64.5% vs 67.2%, NS.
- TOMAHAWK: any initial rhythm with presumed coronary cause; 30-day mortality 54.0% vs 46.0% (p = 0.06), death or severe neurological deficit 64.3% vs 55.6% (worse with immediate).
- Many patients without STEMI have a non-coronary cause (respiratory, neurological, PE).
- Immediate catheterisation may delay correct diagnosis and intensive care.
- Prognosis is driven largely by hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury, not the coronary lesion.
- Hence ERC-ESICM 2025: delay unless occlusion strongly suspected; STEMI still emergent.

</details>

**O1. (open)** Explain why HYPERION and TTM2 reached different conclusions about hypothermia at 33 °C, and how this is reflected in ERC-ESICM 2025.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- HYPERION: 584 patients, non-shockable rhythm, OHCA and IHCA; 33 °C vs 36.5–37.5 °C; CPC 1–2 at d90 10.2% vs 5.7% (p = 0.04), mortality no different.
- HYPERION result is fragile (fragility index 1, small absolute difference, borderline significance) → low certainty.
- TTM2: 1900 OHCA patients, cardiac/unknown cause; 33 °C vs normothermia with active fever treatment (device on at ≥ 37.8 °C); 6-month mortality 50% vs 48%, mRS 4–6 55% vs 55%.
- TTM2 showed more arrhythmias with haemodynamic compromise at 33 °C (24% vs 17%).
- Different populations: TTM2 excluded IHCA and non-cardiac causes, so HYPERION's question remains open for non-shockable/IHCA.
- ERC-ESICM 2025: "temperature control" – active fever prevention ≤ 37.5 °C for 36–72 h; routine hypothermia not recommended but not prohibited.

</details>

**O2. (open)** A comatose post-arrest patient is being maintained at 33 °C in a centre that still uses hypothermia. List the physiological complications you would anticipate and how you would manage rewarming.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Bradycardia (usually harmless) and arrhythmias.
- Hypokalaemia during cooling and hyperkalaemia during rewarming (K⁺ shifts).
- Coagulopathy and increased infection risk.
- ↓ clearance of sedatives/opioids → prolonged effect, relevant for neuroprognostication.
- Hyperglycaemia (↓ insulin secretion) and hypocapnia (↓ CO₂ production) – adjust ventilation.
- Shivering: stepwise treatment (warm hands/face, magnesium, paracetamol, opioids, sedation, NMB only as last resort).
- Rewarm slowly (max. 0.3 °C/h as in TTM2) with core temperature monitoring and a feedback device; prevent rebound fever afterwards.

</details>

- Confirm eligibility: unconscious ≥ 72 h after ROSC with GCS motor ≤ 5.
- Exclude confounders: residual sedation (↓ clearance with hypothermia, renal/hepatic failure), NMB, hypothermia, severe hypotension, hypoglycaemia, metabolic/septic disturbances.
- Clinical exam: bilateral pupillary (ideally pupillometry) and corneal reflexes; M ≤ 3 alone is not a predictor.
- SSEP (bilateral absent N20 ≥ 24 h), EEG (highly malignant pattern > 24 h), NSE at 48/72 h (> 60 µg/L; check for haemolysis), status myoclonus ≤ 72 h, CT/MRI for diffuse anoxic injury (repeat CT if first negative).
- Poor outcome likely only if ≥ 2 predictors; otherwise indeterminate → continue treatment, observe and reassess.
- Separate the WLST decision from prognosis; consider age, comorbidities, patient preferences; communicate with family; consider organ donation.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain the "self-fulfilling prophecy" in post-arrest prognostication and describe the measures that reduce it.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Most ICU deaths after OHCA follow WLST for presumed poor neurological prognosis.
- If treatment is withdrawn because of a predicted poor outcome, the patient dies and the prediction appears confirmed.
- Predictor accuracy studies are biased because treating physicians know test results → tests appear more accurate than they are.
- Mitigation: prognosticate late (≥ 72 h), after exclusion of confounders.
- Use a multimodal approach requiring ≥ 2 independent predictors.
- If criteria not met, treat as indeterminate and reassess.
- Protocolised prognostication with a blinded assessor and no WLST before 96 h without clear reasons (as in TTM2).

</details>
