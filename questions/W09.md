## W09 · D1 – ALS according to ERC 2025

**1.** A 62-year-old man in the ED develops ventricular fibrillation. He has received two biphasic shocks with 2 minutes of CPR after each; VF persists at the rhythm check. According to ERC 2025, the most appropriate next step is:

- A. Deliver the third shock, resume CPR, then give adrenaline 1 mg and amiodarone 300 mg
- B. Give lidocaine 100 mg before the third shock
- C. Give adrenaline 1 mg and amiodarone 300 mg now, then deliver the third shock
- D. Deliver three stacked shocks
- E. Stop CPR and check for a pulse for 30 seconds

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** In shockable rhythms ERC 2025 gives adrenaline 1 mg and amiodarone 300 mg only **after the 3rd shock**, during the CPR cycle that follows it. Each shock is followed straight away by 2 min of CPR with no pulse check, so pauses stay short. Giving drugs earlier has no proven benefit and may delay the shock.

**Why not the others:**
- B – lidocaine 100 mg is an alternative to amiodarone, and it is also given only after the 3rd shock.
- C – drugs come after the 3rd shock, not before it.
- D – stacked shocks are only for a witnessed, monitored arrest with a defibrillator at hand, and only as the first shocks.
- E – a 30-s pulse check stops perfusion; rhythm checks should take < 10 s.

**EDIC trap:** "after the 3rd shock" applies to **both** drugs.

**Ref:** Soar J, et al. ERC 2025 ALS, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the PARAMEDIC2 trial:

- A. Among survivors, severe neurological impairment was more frequent with adrenaline. — T / F
- B. It was a double-blind placebo-controlled trial in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. — T / F
- C. 30-day survival was significantly higher with adrenaline. — T / F
- D. Survival with a favourable neurological outcome at discharge was significantly higher with adrenaline. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – severe neurological impairment (mRS 4–5) among survivors was 31.0% with adrenaline vs 17.8% with placebo.
- B (True) – double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT of 8014 adults with OHCA in 5 UK ambulance services.
- C (True) – 30-day survival (the primary outcome) was 3.2% vs 2.4% (adjusted OR 1.47; NNT ~112).
- D (False) – favourable neurological outcome (mRS ≤ 3) at discharge was 2.2% vs 1.9%, not significant. Adrenaline raises coronary perfusion pressure and ROSC, but may impair cerebral microcirculation.

**EDIC trap:** the primary outcome was positive. The neurological outcome was neutral.

**Ref:** Perkins GD, et al. PARAMEDIC2, N Engl J Med 2018.

</details>

**3.** In the ALPS trial of shock-refractory VF/pVT, compared with placebo:

- A. Neither drug significantly improved overall survival to discharge, but both were associated with higher survival in bystander-witnessed arrests
- B. Amiodarone increased mortality
- C. Lidocaine was superior to amiodarone
- D. Both drugs worsened neurological outcome
- E. Amiodarone significantly improved survival to hospital discharge in the overall population

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** ALPS (3026 patients, shock-refractory VF/pVT in OHCA) found survival to discharge of 24.4% with amiodarone, 23.7% with lidocaine and 21.0% with placebo. None of these differences was significant (amiodarone vs placebo p = 0.08). In bystander-witnessed arrests both drugs improved survival compared with placebo, which suggests that time to drug matters.

**Why not the others:**
- B – amiodarone did not raise mortality; survival was numerically higher.
- C – amiodarone and lidocaine had almost the same outcomes, so lidocaine is an equal alternative.
- D – good neurological outcome was similar in all arms (~17–19%).
- E – the 3.4-point gain over placebo was not statistically significant.

**Ref:** Kudenchuk PJ, et al. ALPS, N Engl J Med 2016.

</details>

**4.** Regarding waveform capnography during CPR (ERC 2025):

- A. A persistent waveform helps exclude oesophageal intubation. — T / F
- B. A sudden fall in ETCO₂ may indicate ROSC. — T / F
- C. A low ETCO₂ value alone is sufficient to decide to terminate resuscitation. — T / F
- D. A rise in ETCO₂ alone justifies stopping compressions to check for a pulse. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – a persistent waveform over several breaths confirms the tube is in the trachea and helps exclude oesophageal intubation, though it does not exclude endobronchial placement.
- B (False) – at ROSC, ETCO₂ **rises** suddenly because pulmonary blood flow returns. A sudden fall suggests tube displacement or loss of output.
- C (False) – a low ETCO₂ alone must not be used to stop CPR; values depend on CPR quality, ventilation and the cause of arrest.
- D (False) – a rise in ETCO₂ may signal ROSC, but compressions should not be stopped on this sign alone. Wait for the planned rhythm check.

**Ref:** Soar J, et al. ERC 2025 ALS, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**5.** A patient in cardiac surgery ICU with an arterial line suffers PEA. According to ERC 2025, a reasonable initial approach to adrenaline is:

- A. 5 mg IV bolus
- B. Vasopressin 40 U instead of adrenaline
- C. Wait until after the third shock
- D. Small IV increments of 50–100 µg rather than an immediate 1 mg bolus
- E. Adrenaline is contraindicated with an arterial line

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** In a monitored arrest with an arterial line (e.g. after cardiac surgery), ERC 2025 allows small IV adrenaline doses of **50–100 µg**, titrated to arterial pressure. A full 1 mg bolus can cause severe rebound hypertension and arrhythmias after ROSC. If small doses fail, check for extravasation and move to 1 mg every 3–5 min.

**Why not the others:**
- A – high-dose adrenaline (5 mg) does not improve survival.
- B – vasopressin is not recommended instead of adrenaline in ALS.
- C – PEA is non-shockable, so there is no shock; adrenaline is given as soon as possible.
- E – an arterial line is a reason to titrate adrenaline, not a contraindication.

**Ref:** Soar J, et al. ERC 2025 ALS, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**6.** Regarding defibrillation according to ERC 2025:

- A. Higher energy should be used routinely in obese patients. — T / F
- B. Pads may be placed directly over an implanted device. — T / F
- C. The first biphasic (rectilinear or truncated exponential) shock should be at least 150 J. — T / F
- D. In VF persisting after three shocks, changing to an antero-posterior pad position may be considered. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – obese patients get standard shock energies. Transthoracic impedance makes little difference with modern biphasic devices.
- B (False) – place pads **> 8 cm** from an ICD or pacemaker. The device can block current and be damaged.
- C (True) – the first biphasic shock (rectilinear or truncated exponential) is **≥ 150 J**; for pulsed biphasic it is 130–150 J. Escalate energy if the device allows.
- D (True) – in refractory VF, first confirm the antero-lateral pads are placed correctly, then consider switching to antero-posterior (vector change).

**EDIC trap:** if you do not know the device, use the highest energy available.

**Ref:** Soar J, et al. ERC 2025 ALS, Resuscitation 2025; Cheskes S, et al. DOSE VF, N Engl J Med 2022.

</details>

**7.** In the DOSE VF trial, survival to hospital discharge in refractory VF was highest with:

- A. Standard defibrillation
- B. Mechanical CPR
- C. Double sequential external defibrillation
- D. Adding lidocaine
- E. Vector-change defibrillation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** DOSE VF randomised 405 OHCA patients in refractory VF (Ontario) to three strategies. Survival to discharge was **30.4% with double sequential external defibrillation (DSED)**, 21.7% with vector change and 13.3% with standard shocks. The trial stopped early (partly because of COVID-19) and the numbers are small. ERC 2025 therefore does not recommend DSED routinely.

**Why not the others:**
- A – standard defibrillation was the control arm and had the lowest survival.
- B – mechanical CPR was not an intervention in DOSE VF.
- D – lidocaine was not studied in DOSE VF; ALPS tested antiarrhythmics.
- E – vector change improved survival, but less than DSED.

**EDIC trap:** DSED was best in the trial, but the guideline recommends considering vector change, the simpler option.

**Ref:** Cheskes S, et al. DOSE VF, N Engl J Med 2022.

</details>

**8.** Regarding point-of-care ultrasound during cardiac arrest:

- A. It cannot identify cardiac tamponade during CPR. — T / F
- B. Absence of myocardial contractility alone is a reason to stop CPR. — T / F
- C. Isolated right ventricular dilatation confirms pulmonary embolism. — T / F
- D. Prolonged pauses in chest compressions are acceptable to obtain diagnostic images. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – finding **tamponade** (and tension pneumothorax) is one of the main uses of POCUS in arrest, and both are treatable.
- B (False) – absent myocardial contraction alone does not justify stopping CPR. Survival is poor, but the finding varies between operators and timing.
- C (False) – the RV dilates in every arrest, so isolated RV dilatation **does not** diagnose PE.
- D (False) – images must be taken by a skilled operator within the normal rhythm-check pause (< 10 s), ideally recorded and reviewed afterwards.

**EDIC trap:** ERC states explicitly that a dilated RV is not proof of PE.

**Ref:** Soar J, et al. ERC 2025 ALS, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**9.** After tracheal intubation during CPR, ventilation should be delivered:

- A. 30:2 with pauses for breaths
- B. At 10 breaths/min with continuous chest compressions
- C. At 20 breaths/min to correct acidosis
- D. At 6 breaths/min to avoid hyperventilation, with pauses
- E. Only when ETCO₂ falls below 1 kPa

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Once a tracheal tube or supraglottic airway is in place, ERC 2025 recommends **continuous compressions with 10 breaths/min** (each ~1 s, visible chest rise, highest FiO₂). Stopping for breaths lowers coronary perfusion pressure. Hyperventilation raises intrathoracic pressure and reduces venous return.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 30:2 is for an unsecured airway (bag-mask).
- C – 20/min is hyperventilation: less venous return and cerebral vasoconstriction; it does not correct acidosis during CPR.
- D – pauses are not needed after intubation, and 6/min risks hypoxaemia.
- E – ventilation is not titrated to ETCO₂ during CPR.

**Ref:** Soar J, et al. ERC 2025 ALS, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**10.** Which are among the "4 Hs and 4 Ts" reversible causes of cardiac arrest?

- A. Hypothermia — T / F
- B. Toxins — T / F
- C. Thrombosis (coronary or pulmonary) — T / F
- D. Hypo/hyperkalaemia — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – hypothermia (and hyperthermia) is one of the 4 Hs; in accidental hypothermia, adrenaline and shocks are modified.
- B (True) – toxins are one of the 4 Ts; give antidotes early and be ready for prolonged CPR or ECPR.
- C (True) – thrombosis, coronary or pulmonary, is one of the 4 Ts (PCI, or thrombolysis for PE).
- D (True) – hypo/hyperkalaemia and other metabolic disorders are one of the 4 Hs.

The full list: 4 Hs are hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hypo/hyperkalaemia/metabolic, hypo/hyperthermia; 4 Ts are thrombosis, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins.

**EDIC trap:** "hypoglycaemia" and "hydrogen ions" come from older or American lists, not the ERC 4 Hs.

**Ref:** Soar J, et al. ERC 2025 ALS, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 55-year-old man collapses in the ED; the monitor shows VF. Outline the ALS sequence for the first 10 minutes according to ERC 2025, including drug timing and what you would do if VF persists after three shocks.

Key points expected:
- High-quality compressions with minimal interruptions; correct antero-lateral pad placement (lateral pad below the axilla, mid-axillary line).

**O2. (open)** Explain why PARAMEDIC2 is described as "positive" yet adrenaline's benefit remains controversial, and why ERC 2025 still recommends it.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Primary outcome 30-day survival was higher with adrenaline (3.2% vs 2.4%, adj. OR 1.47; NNT ~112).

## W09 · D2 – Post-ROSC care: oxygenation, MAP, coronary angiography

**1.** A 58-year-old is admitted after OHCA with ROSC after 15 minutes; he remains comatose. ECG shows sinus tachycardia with no ST elevation, he is haemodynamically stable on low-dose noradrenaline. According to ERC-ESICM 2025, the most appropriate approach is:

- A. Delay catheterisation unless acute coronary occlusion is strongly suspected, and consider a whole-body CT
- B. Coronary CT angiography only after 72 hours
- C. Systemic thrombolysis
- D. Coronary angiography is contraindicated after OHCA
- E. Immediate coronary angiography in all patients after OHCA

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** With no ST elevation, ERC-ESICM 2025 recommends **delaying coronary angiography** unless acute coronary occlusion is strongly suspected (haemodynamic or electrical instability, ongoing ischaemia). Many such patients have a non-coronary cause. A **biphasic whole-body CT** (head to pelvis, including CT pulmonary angiography) can find intracranial haemorrhage, PE, aortic dissection and injuries from CPR. COACT and TOMAHAWK showed no benefit from immediate angiography.

**Why not the others:**
- B – no guideline sets a fixed 72-h CT coronary angiography.
- C – systemic thrombolysis has no role without a diagnosis such as PE.
- D – angiography is not contraindicated; it is emergent in STEMI and delayed without it.
- E – routine immediate angiography for all patients was not beneficial (COACT, TOMAHAWK).

**Ref:** Nolan JP, et al. ERC-ESICM 2025 Post-Resuscitation Care, Resuscitation/Intensive Care Med 2025.

</details>

**2.** Regarding oxygenation and ventilation after ROSC (ERC-ESICM 2025):

- A. Pulse oximetry may overestimate saturation in patients with darker skin. — T / F
- B. Titrate SpO₂ to 94–98 % once reliably measured. — T / F
- C. Target PaO₂ 20–25 kPa. — T / F
- D. Target mild hypocapnia (PaCO₂ 3.5–4.0 kPa) to reduce intracranial pressure. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – new in 2025: pulse oximetry can overestimate SpO₂ in people with darker skin, and it is unreliable in low-flow states. Use arterial gases.
- B (True) – give FiO₂ 1.0 until SpO₂ can be measured reliably, then titrate to SpO₂ 94–98%.
- C (False) – the target is PaO₂ **10–13 kPa** (75–100 mmHg). Avoid both hypoxaemia (< 8 kPa) and hyperoxaemia.
- D (False) – aim for **normocapnia, PaCO₂ 4.7–6.0 kPa**. Hypocapnia causes cerebral vasoconstriction and worsens ischaemia.

**EDIC trap:** the post-ROSC SpO₂ target is 94–98%, not 88–92% and not 100%.

**Ref:** Nolan JP, et al. ERC-ESICM 2025 Post-Resuscitation Care, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**3.** In the BOX trial, comatose OHCA survivors were randomised to mean arterial pressure targets of:

- A. 65 vs 85 mmHg
- B. 80–85 vs 65–70 mmHg
- C. 70 vs 90 mmHg
- D. 55 vs 65 mmHg
- E. 63 vs 77 mmHg

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** BOX (789 comatose OHCA patients, two Danish centres, factorial design) compared **MAP 63 vs 77 mmHg**, blinded with a modified arterial pressure module. The primary outcome (death or severe disability or coma at 90 days) was **32% vs 34%** (HR 1.08), with no difference. ERC-ESICM 2025 therefore sets MAP > 60–65 mmHg.

**Why not the others:**
- A – 65 vs 85 mmHg were not the BOX arms; smaller trials (COMACARE, Neuroprotect) tested higher targets of roughly 80–100 mmHg.
- B – BOX did not use target ranges like 80–85 vs 65–70 mmHg; its arms were single values, 63 vs 77 mmHg.
- C – 70 vs 90 mmHg was not a BOX arm.
- D – 55 mmHg would be below any accepted post-ROSC target.

**Ref:** Kjaergaard J, et al. BOX, N Engl J Med 2022.

</details>

**4.** Regarding COACT and TOMAHAWK:

- A. Both showed significantly improved survival with immediate angiography. — T / F
- B. TOMAHAWK enrolled patients regardless of initial rhythm. — T / F
- C. In TOMAHAWK, death or severe neurological deficit was numerically more frequent with immediate angiography. — T / F
- D. COACT enrolled only patients with ST-segment elevation. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – neither trial showed better survival. In COACT, 90-day survival was 64.5% vs 67.2%; in TOMAHAWK, 30-day mortality was 54.0% vs 46.0%, which was worse with immediate angiography (p = 0.06).
- B (True) – TOMAHAWK enrolled OHCA of presumed coronary cause with **any initial rhythm** and no ST elevation.
- C (True) – death or severe neurological deficit was 64.3% vs 55.6%, numerically higher with immediate angiography.
- D (False) – COACT enrolled unconscious patients with a **shockable** rhythm and **no** ST elevation.

**EDIC trap:** COACT is shockable rhythms only; TOMAHAWK is all rhythms. STEMI still needs emergent angiography.

**Ref:** Lemkes JS, et al. COACT, N Engl J Med 2019; Desch S, et al. TOMAHAWK, N Engl J Med 2021.

</details>

**5.** Which post-ROSC intervention is recommended by ERC-ESICM 2025?

- A. Routine prophylactic antibiotics
- B. Routine hydrocortisone
- C. MAP > 60–65 mmHg and avoidance of hypotension
- D. Routine seizure prophylaxis with levetiracetam
- E. Routine neuromuscular blockade for 24 h

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** ERC-ESICM 2025 gives an explicit target of **MAP > 60–65 mmHg** and says to avoid hypotension, using fluids, noradrenaline and/or dobutamine as needed. BOX showed no benefit from a higher target (77 vs 63 mmHg). Hypotension after ROSC is linked to worse neurological outcome.

**Why not the others:**
- A – prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended. ANTHARTIC reduced early VAP but did not change mortality; keep a low threshold to treat pneumonia.
- B – routine corticosteroids are not recommended.
- D – seizure prophylaxis is not recommended; seizures are diagnosed by EEG and then treated.
- E – routine NMB is not recommended; it may be considered in severe ARDS or shivering.

**Ref:** Nolan JP, et al. ERC-ESICM 2025 Post-Resuscitation Care, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**6.** Components of the post-cardiac arrest syndrome include:

- A. The persisting precipitating pathology — T / F
- B. Post-arrest myocardial dysfunction — T / F
- C. A systemic ischaemia–reperfusion response — T / F
- D. Hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – the persisting precipitating cause (ACS, PE, haemorrhage, sepsis) needs targeted treatment.
- B (True) – post-arrest myocardial dysfunction (stunning) is usually reversible within 48–72 h; it may need dobutamine or mechanical support.
- C (True) – the systemic ischaemia–reperfusion response is "sepsis-like", with vasoplegia, capillary leak, coagulopathy and adrenal dysfunction.
- D (True) – hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury is the leading cause of death after OHCA, mostly through withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy.

**EDIC trap:** the syndrome has **four** components. Do not forget the precipitating cause.

**Ref:** Nolan JP, et al. ERC-ESICM 2025 Post-Resuscitation Care, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**7.** In the oxygen part of the BOX trial (PaO₂ 9–10 vs 13–14 kPa), the primary outcome:

- A. Was significantly better with the liberal target
- B. Was only reported for in-hospital arrests
- C. Did not differ significantly between groups
- D. Could not be assessed because the trial stopped early
- E. Was significantly better with the restrictive target

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** In the BOX oxygen comparison (789 patients), the primary outcome (death or severe disability or coma at 90 days) was **32.0% with the restrictive target vs 33.9% with the liberal target** (HR 0.95), which was not significant. This supports the moderate ERC-ESICM range of PaO₂ 10–13 kPa, avoiding both extremes.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the liberal target was not better.
- B – BOX enrolled OHCA only.
- D – BOX finished recruitment and reported its primary outcome.
- E – the restrictive target was not significantly better.

**Ref:** Schmidt H, et al. BOX oxygen, N Engl J Med 2022.

</details>

**8.** A comatose patient after ROSC is being cooled after spontaneous mild hypothermia. Regarding ventilation:

- A. Hypothermia increases CO₂ production, so minute ventilation usually needs to be increased. — T / F
- B. Hypoxaemia with PaO₂ < 8 kPa should be avoided. — T / F
- C. Hyperoxaemia should be actively targeted to protect the brain. — T / F
- D. Tidal volumes of 10–12 mL/kg ideal body weight are recommended to avoid hypercapnia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – hypothermia **reduces** metabolic CO₂ production (VCO₂). If ventilation is unchanged, PaCO₂ falls, causing hypocapnia and cerebral vasoconstriction. Check PaCO₂ more often (new in 2025).
- B (True) – avoid hypoxaemia (PaO₂ < 8 kPa / 60 mmHg); the target is 10–13 kPa.
- C (False) – hyperoxaemia should be avoided; it may worsen reperfusion injury and has no proven benefit.
- D (False) – lung-protective ventilation with Vt **6–8 mL/kg IBW** is recommended. Correct CO₂ by changing rate, not by large tidal volumes.

**EDIC trap:** in hypothermia the danger is **hypocapnia**, not hypercapnia.

**Ref:** Nolan JP, et al. ERC-ESICM 2025 Post-Resuscitation Care, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**9.** A patient after OHCA has persistent ST elevation in leads V1–V4 after ROSC. The most appropriate step is:

- A. Emergent cardiac catheterisation with PPCI if needed
- B. Thrombolysis only if the patient wakes up
- C. Conservative management because the patient is comatose
- D. Delayed angiography after neuroprognostication at 72 h
- E. Whole-body CT and angiography only if troponin rises

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Persistent ST elevation after ROSC (here anterior V1–V4) strongly suggests acute coronary occlusion. ERC-ESICM 2025 recommends **emergent cardiac catheterisation with PPCI if needed**, as in any STEMI. Coma is not a contraindication, because prognosis cannot be judged reliably at this stage.

**Why not the others:**
- B – PPCI is preferred over thrombolysis, and treatment must not wait for awakening.
- C – coma alone is not a reason to withhold reperfusion.
- D – waiting 72 h for prognostication would lose the myocardium.
- E – troponin always rises after CPR and defibrillation, so it does not help with timing; CT is mainly for patients without ST elevation.

**Ref:** Nolan JP, et al. ERC-ESICM 2025 Post-Resuscitation Care, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

**10.** Regarding seizures and sedation after cardiac arrest (ERC-ESICM 2025):

- A. Seizure prophylaxis is recommended for all comatose patients. — T / F
- B. Levetiracetam or sodium valproate are suggested first-line antiseizure drugs. — T / F
- C. EEG should be used to diagnose electrographic seizures. — T / F
- D. Short-acting sedatives facilitate neurological assessment. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (False) – seizure prophylaxis is **not** recommended. Treat clinical or electrographic seizures once diagnosed.
- B (True) – ERC-ESICM 2025 suggests **levetiracetam or sodium valproate** first line, together with sedatives.
- C (True) – use EEG to diagnose and monitor seizures, since many seizures in sedated patients are non-convulsive.
- D (True) – short-acting sedatives (e.g. propofol) and daily interruptions allow neurological examination and reduce confounding in prognostication.

**EDIC trap:** status myoclonus is a prognostic sign. It is not automatically a seizure, so record an EEG.

**Ref:** Nolan JP, et al. ERC-ESICM 2025 Post-Resuscitation Care, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>

## W09 · D3 – Temperature management and fever after cardiac arrest

**1.** In the TTM2 trial, compared with normothermia with early treatment of fever, targeted hypothermia at 33 °C:

- A. Reduced the incidence of pneumonia

- E. Poor outcome is certain because NSE > 60 µg/L

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** NSE is present in erythrocytes, so **visible haemolysis on ECMO falsely raises it**, and 80 µg/L cannot be taken at face value. Present N20 and a continuous EEG background are not predictors of poor outcome. With **no valid second predictor**, prognosis is **indeterminate**: continue treatment, repeat tests and watch the trend.

**Why not the others:**
- B – present N20 is common in patients who do badly (low sensitivity) and does not guarantee recovery.
- C – serial NSE and haemolysis indices help interpret it.
- D – ECMO dependence on its own says nothing about brain prognosis.
- E – a single confounded predictor cannot make poor outcome certain; ≥ 2 valid predictors are required.

**Ref:** Nolan JP, et al. ERC-ESICM 2025 Post-Resuscitation Care, Resuscitation 2025.

**14.** Regarding coronary angiography and diagnostics after ROSC (ERC-ESICM 2025):

- A. Persistent ST elevation after ROSC warrants emergent catheterisation. — T / F
- B. A dual-phase whole-body CT is prioritised in patients without ST elevation. — T / F
- C. Coma is a contraindication to emergent angiography in STEMI. — T / F
- D. In OHCA without ST elevation, catheterisation should be delayed unless acute coronary occlusion is strongly suspected. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – persistent ST elevation after ROSC means emergent catheterisation, with PPCI if needed.
- B (True) – new in 2025: without ST elevation, a dual-phase (biphasic) whole-body CT, head to pelvis including CTPA, is prioritised to find non-coronary causes and CPR injuries.
- C (False) – coma is **not** a contraindication to emergent angiography in STEMI, because early neurological prognosis is unreliable.
- D (True) – without ST elevation, catheterisation is delayed unless acute occlusion is strongly suspected (COACT, TOMAHAWK).

**Ref:** Nolan JP, et al. ERC-ESICM 2025 Post-Resuscitation Care, Resuscitation 2025.

**15.** A 30-year-old woman develops urticaria, wheeze and BP 70/40 mmHg minutes after an IV antibiotic; she has a palpable pulse. The first drug to give is:

- A. Chlorphenamine 10 mg IV
- B. Adrenaline 1 mg IV bolus
- C. Lipid emulsion 1.5 mL/kg
- D. Adrenaline 500 µg IM, repeated after 5 minutes if no improvement
- E. Hydrocortisone 200 mg IV

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Urticaria, bronchospasm and hypotension after a drug exposure mean anaphylaxis. The first drug is **IM adrenaline 500 µg** (anterolateral thigh), repeated after 5 min if there is no improvement, together with an IV crystalloid bolus and high-flow oxygen. Its α- and β-effects reverse vasodilatation, bronchospasm and mediator release.

**Why not the others:**
- A – antihistamines treat skin symptoms only and are not first line.
- B – an IV 1 mg bolus is for cardiac arrest only; with a pulse it risks arrhythmia and severe hypertension.
- C – lipid emulsion treats local anaesthetic toxicity.
- E – corticosteroids act slowly, have no proven acute benefit and are not first line.

**Ref:** Lott C, et al. ERC 2025 Special Circumstances, Resuscitation 2025.

</details>
