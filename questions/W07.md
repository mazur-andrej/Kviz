## W07 · D1 – Cardiogenic shock: definition, SCAI SHOCK, diagnosis, recommendations

**1.** According to the 2026 ESC heart failure guidelines, which statement about the definition of cardiogenic shock is correct?

- A. A cardiac index < 1.8 L/min/m² must be documented
- B. Pulmonary congestion on chest X-ray is required
- C. It is critical end-organ hypoperfusion due to primary cardiac dysfunction, without a fixed blood pressure cut-off
- D. Lactate must exceed 4 mmol/L
- E. Systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg for ≥ 30 minutes is mandatory

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.** ESC 2026 defines CS as critical hypoperfusion due to primary cardiac dysfunction; lactate > 2 mmol/L supports the diagnosis, no fixed BP cut-off is set.

</details>

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 58-year-old man with anterior STEMI arrives with BP 92/58 mmHg, cool mottled extremities, oliguria and lactate 4.1 mmol/L. Outline your initial diagnostic work-up and management during the first hours.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Recognise CS (hypoperfusion from a primary cardiac cause; lactate > 2 supports it, no fixed BP cut-off) and stage it – hypoperfusion needing intervention = at least SCAI C; reassess for deterioration (D).
- ECG, troponin, lactate, ABG, creatinine, liver tests, NT-proBNP; echocardiography as first-line tool – LVEF, regional wall motion, mechanical complications (VSD, acute MR, tamponade), RV function.
- Immediate coronary angiography and PCI of the infarct-related artery (SHOCK trial: ↓ 6-month mortality); emergency CABG if PCI not feasible; culprit-only PCI in multivessel disease.
- Arterial line; consider PAC/TPTD if CO monitoring needed (CPO, PAPi, SvO₂).
- Norepinephrine as preferred vasopressor, initial MAP target ≥ 65 mmHg individualised to perfusion; avoid dopamine and routine epinephrine.
- Inotrope (e.g. dobutamine) if hypoperfusion persists despite standard treatment incl. fluid challenge – lowest dose, shortest time.
- Activate the shock team (I, C); consider microaxial pump only in selected STEMI-CS without risk of hypoxic brain injury (IIa); no routine IABP or unselected MCS (III).
- Monitor perfusion targets: lactate clearance, CRT, urine output, mental status.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Explain why mixed venous saturation helps distinguish cardiogenic from septic shock, and how PAC-derived indices help identify right ventricular failure.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- In CS, low CO leads to increased O₂ extraction → SvO₂/ScvO₂ typically low.
- In septic shock ScvO₂ is often normal or high.
- Low SvO₂ + high PCWP + low CI = classic CS profile.
- PAPi = (sPAP − dPAP) / RAP; a low value signals RV failure.
- High RAP/PCWP ratio = predominant RV failure; isolated RV failure shows ↑ CVP/RAP with normal or low PCWP.
- CPO = MAP × CO / 451 integrates pressure and flow and is a strong predictor of mortality.

</details>

**5.** A patient on femoral VA-ECMO for cardiogenic shock with severe ARDS shows recovering LV function. Right radial PaO₂ is 6 kPa, femoral arterial PaO₂ 35 kPa. The most appropriate intervention is:

- A. Improve native lung gas exchange and consider conversion to V-AV ECMO
- B. Increase FiO₂ on the oxygenator
- C. Increase sweep gas flow
- D. Reduce ECMO flow to the minimum
- E. Insert a distal perfusion cannula

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.** Harlequin syndrome – the heart ejects hypoxic blood from the failing lungs into the aortic root; oxygenator FiO₂ does not change that. Reducing flow would move the mixing zone even more distally.

</details>

**7.** The haemodynamic effect of a transvalvular microaxial flow pump (Impella) on the left ventricle is:

**O1. (open)** Explain why ECLS-SHOCK was negative while DanGer Shock showed a mortality benefit, and what this means for device selection in AMI-related cardiogenic shock.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Different devices and physiology: VA-ECMO gives full circulatory support but increases LV afterload; Impella directly unloads the LV.
- ECLS-SHOCK: unselected AMI-CS (STEMI and NSTEMI), lactate > 3, large proportion after CPR; 30-d mortality 47.8 vs 49.0% (NS).
- ECLS-SHOCK: more BARC 3–5 bleeding (23.4 vs 9.6%) and vascular complications (11.0 vs 3.8%); active LV unloading in only ~6%.
- DanGer Shock: carefully selected STEMI-CS, LVEF < 45%, lactate ≥ 2.5, comatose OHCA patients excluded; 180-d mortality 45.8 vs 58.5% (HR 0.74).
- DanGer Shock benefit came at the cost of more adverse events (24.0 vs 6.2%), limb ischaemia and RRT.
- Implication (ESC 2026): microaxial pump IIa only in selected STEMI-CS without risk of hypoxic brain injury; unselected MCS and routine IABP III; shock team I.
- Rescue/individualised VA-ECMO still has a role (refractory CS, ECPR, myocarditis, bridge to transplant/VAD).

</details>

**O2. (open)** A patient on femoral VA-ECMO shows loss of arterial pulse pressure and worsening pulmonary oedema. Explain the mechanism and outline your management.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Retrograde femoral return increases LV afterload; a weak LV cannot open the aortic valve.
- Result: ↑ LVEDP and LA pressure → pulmonary oedema, LV/aortic root stasis and thrombus, subendocardial ischaemia.
- Confirm with echo: aortic valve not opening, "smoke" in the LV; rising PCWP.
- Reduce ECMO flow to the minimum that maintains perfusion.
- Inotrope to open the aortic valve; vasodilation to reduce afterload.
- Unload with IABP or Impella (ECPELLA), or surgical/percutaneous vent (transseptal LA cannula, septostomy).
- Note: EARLY-UNLOAD showed no benefit of early routine unloading (46.6 vs 44.8%).

</details>

**O1. (open)** Explain why CULPRIT-SHOCK and COMPLETE reached different conclusions about multivessel PCI, and how you would apply them in practice.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Different populations: CULPRIT-SHOCK = AMI-CS with multivessel CAD (n = 706); COMPLETE = haemodynamically stable STEMI after successful culprit PCI (n = 4041).
- CULPRIT-SHOCK: culprit-only reduced death or RRT at 30 d (45.9 vs 55.4%, RR 0.83); 1-year mortality 50.0 vs 56.9%.

- In shock, extra lesions add contrast load, procedure time and ischaemia during further interventions.
- The trials do not conflict – presence of shock decides.

- If anatomy unsuitable for PCI in CS → emergency CABG.

</details>

**O2. (open)** Four days after a late-presenting MI, a patient becomes acutely hypotensive. Outline how you would differentiate the possible mechanical complications at the bedside and their management.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Urgent echocardiography for any sudden deterioration or new murmur.
- VSR: new holosystolic murmur with thrill, biventricular failure, left-to-right shunt, O₂ saturation step-up RA→PA, Qp/Qs > 1.
- Papillary muscle rupture (posteromedial, inferior MI, single PDA supply): sudden pulmonary oedema, murmur may be absent, flail leaflet, large v waves on PCWP.
- Free wall rupture: tamponade, PEA, sudden death → pericardiocentesis as bridge and emergency surgery.
- Also consider RV infarction (hypotension, raised JVP, clear lungs, V4R) – preserve preload, avoid nitrates.
- Surgery is the treatment of choice, decided by the Heart Team; CABG at the same time if needed.
- Bridge with afterload reduction, IABP or temporary MCS (ESC 2026 IIa, C).

</details>

**O1. (open)** A patient with known PAH is admitted with pneumonia, BP 82/50 mmHg, CVP 18 mmHg, lactate 3.5 mmol/L and a dilated RV with D-sign. Outline your management and explain the physiological rationale.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Vicious circle: ↑ RV afterload → dilatation, TR → ↓ LV filling → hypotension → ↓ RV coronary perfusion → ischaemia.
- Treat the trigger (pneumonia – antibiotics; check arrhythmia, anaemia, interrupted PAH therapy).
- Restore systemic pressure – MAP > 60 mmHg with norepinephrine or vasopressin (vasopressin raises SVR without marked rise in PVR).
- Avoid fluid boluses – dilated RV compresses the LV; most need negative balance (diuretics, RRT/ultrafiltration).
- Inotrope for low output (dobutamine, milrinone; levosimendan alternative), with vasopressor if inodilator causes hypotension.
- Reduce RV afterload: correct hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, acidosis; inhaled pulmonary vasodilators (iNO, inhaled prostacyclins).
- Maintain sinus rhythm/AV synchrony; avoid intubation if possible (if needed: vasopressor running before induction, gentle induction).
- Consider VA-ECMO as a bridge to transplantation or recovery.

</details>

**O2. (open)** A ventilated ARDS patient has driving pressure 20 cmH₂O, PaCO₂ 55 mmHg and a D-sign on echo. Explain why the RV is failing and describe RV-protective ventilation.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Acute cor pulmonale occurs in ~22% of moderate/severe ARDS; this patient has risk factors (DP ≥ 18, PaCO₂ ≥ 48).
- Positive pressure ↑ RV afterload and ↓ preload; hypercapnia and acidosis ↑ PVR.
- PVR–lung volume relationship is U-shaped – lowest at FRC; atelectasis and hyperinflation both raise PVR.
- Limit plateau and driving pressure (target < 18 cmH₂O).
- Keep PaCO₂ < 48 mmHg; correct hypoxaemia.
- Titrate PEEP – enough to recruit, avoid overdistension; do not simply increase PEEP or give fluids.
- Prone positioning unloads the RV; consider VV-ECMO for refractory ACP; vasopressor to maintain RV perfusion.

</details>
