## W07 · D1 – Cardiogenic shock: definition, SCAI SHOCK, diagnosis, recommendations

**1.** According to the 2026 ESC heart failure guidelines, which statement about the definition of cardiogenic shock is correct?

- A. A cardiac index < 1.8 L/min/m² must be documented
- B. Pulmonary congestion on chest X-ray is required
- C. It is critical end-organ hypoperfusion due to primary cardiac dysfunction, without a fixed blood pressure cut-off
- D. Lactate must exceed 4 mmol/L
- E. Systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg for ≥ 30 minutes is mandatory

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The 2026 ESC heart failure guidelines define cardiogenic shock as critical end-organ hypoperfusion caused by a primary cardiac disorder. Lactate > 2 mmol/L supports the diagnosis, but no blood pressure threshold is required: vasoconstriction can keep BP normal while perfusion fails ("normotensive CS").

**Why not the others:**
- A – a cardiac index ≤ 2.2 L/min/m² was a supportive haemodynamic criterion in trial definitions; the guideline does not require a CI of 1.8.
- B – congestion is common ("cold and wet") but not required; "cold and dry" CS exists, e.g. in RV infarction.
- D – lactate > 2 mmol/L supports the diagnosis; 4 mmol/L is not a diagnostic cut-off.
- E – SBP < 90 mmHg for ≥ 30 min is the classic trial definition (SHOCK, IABP-SHOCK II), not the ESC 2026 one.

**EDIC trap:** a normotensive patient with hypoperfusion from a cardiac cause still has CS.

**Ref:** Køber L et al., 2026 ESC Guidelines for the management of heart failure, Eur Heart J 2026;47:5346–5457; Hochman JS et al., SHOCK, N Engl J Med 1999;341:625–634.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the SCAI SHOCK classification (2022 update):

- A. Stage C can be assigned without hypotension if hypoperfusion requires intervention. — T / F
- B. A lactate ≥ 8 mmol/L is typical of stage E. — T / F
- C. Stage B describes haemodynamic instability (relative hypotension or tachycardia) without hypoperfusion. — T / F
- D. The cardiac arrest modifier applies to patients who are comatose after cardiac arrest. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – stage C is defined by hypoperfusion that needs one intervention beyond fluids (a drug or a device). Hypotension is typical but not mandatory.
- B (True) – lactate ≥ 8 mmol/L, often with CPR, ECMO or profound hypotension despite maximal support, is typical of stage E (Extremis).
- C (True) – stage B (Beginning) means relative hypotension (SBP < 90, MAP < 60 or a fall > 30 mmHg) or tachycardia, with normal lactate and preserved perfusion.
- D (True) – the cardiac arrest modifier applies to comatose patients after arrest (typically GCS < 9), because the risk of hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury independently worsens prognosis.

**EDIC trap:** B = hypotension without hypoperfusion; C = hypoperfusion with or without hypotension.

**Ref:** Naidu SS et al., SCAI SHOCK Stage Classification Expert Consensus Update, J Am Coll Cardiol 2022;79:933–946.

</details>

**3.** A 64-year-old man with anterior STEMI has BP 96/60 mmHg, cool extremities and lactate 3.4 mmol/L, requiring dobutamine to maintain urine output. One hour later he is stable on the same dose. His SCAI stage is:

- A. A
- B. B
- C. D
- D. C
- E. E

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** The patient has hypoperfusion (cool extremities, lactate 3.4 mmol/L) that needs one intervention beyond fluids (dobutamine), and he remains stable on the same dose after an hour. That is SCAI stage C (Classic), which is option D.

**Why not the others:**
- A – stage A (At risk) has neither haemodynamic instability nor hypoperfusion; a large STEMI alone would be A.
- B – stage B needs normal lactate and preserved perfusion, which a lactate of 3.4 and cold extremities rule out.
- C – stage D (Deteriorating) needs initial support to fail, with rising lactate or escalation of drugs or MCS, and this has not happened.
- E – stage E means circulatory collapse, CPR or ECMO, or lactate ≥ 8 mmol/L.

**EDIC trap:** the option letters do not match the SCAI letters. Read the label, not the position.

**Ref:** Naidu SS et al., SCAI SHOCK Stage Classification Expert Consensus Update, J Am Coll Cardiol 2022;79:933–946.

</details>

**4.** Regarding haemodynamic assessment in cardiogenic shock:

- A. Cardiac power output is calculated as MAP × CO / 451. — T / F
- B. The ESICM 2025 guidelines consider echocardiography the first-line tool to determine the type of shock. — T / F
- C. Mixed venous oxygen saturation is typically high in cardiogenic shock. — T / F
- D. A high pulmonary artery pulsatility index (PAPi) suggests right ventricular failure. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – F.**

- A (True) – CPO (W) = MAP × CO / 451. It combines pressure and flow, and a low CPO (< ~0.6 W) is one of the strongest haemodynamic predictors of mortality in CS.
- B (True) – ESICM 2025 recommends echocardiography as the first-line tool to identify the type of shock, repeated even when CO is monitored invasively.
- C (False) – SvO₂ is typically **low** in CS, because low CO increases O₂ extraction. Normal or high values are more typical of septic shock.
- D (False) – PAPi = (sPAP − dPAP) / RAP. It is a **low** PAPi (roughly < 1 in AMI) that signals RV failure.

**EDIC trap:** low SvO₂ + high PCWP + low CI = classic CS profile.

**Ref:** Monnet X et al., ESICM guidelines on circulatory shock 2025, Intensive Care Med 2025;51:1971–2012; Fincke R et al., J Am Coll Cardiol 2004;44:340–348.

</details>

**5.** In the SHOCK trial (NEJM 1999), emergency revascularisation compared with initial medical stabilisation in AMI-related cardiogenic shock:

- A. Was stopped early for harm
- B. Increased mortality at 6 months
- C. Showed benefit only with fibrinolysis
- D. Did not significantly reduce 30-day mortality but reduced 6-month mortality
- E. Significantly reduced 30-day mortality

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** SHOCK randomised 302 patients with AMI-related CS to emergency revascularisation (PCI or CABG) or initial medical stabilisation. The primary endpoint, 30-day mortality, was not significantly lower (46.7% vs 56.0%, p = 0.11), but 6-month mortality was (50.3% vs 63.1%, p = 0.027). The benefit persisted long-term and underpins the class I recommendation for immediate revascularisation.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the trial completed enrolment and was not stopped for harm.
- B – 6-month mortality was lower, not higher, with revascularisation.
- C – the benefit came from mechanical revascularisation; fibrinolysis was part of the medical stabilisation arm.
- E – the 30-day difference was not statistically significant.

**EDIC trap:** SHOCK was "negative" for its primary endpoint but positive at 6 months.

**Ref:** Hochman JS et al., N Engl J Med 1999;341:625–634; Byrne RA et al., 2023 ESC ACS Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2023;44:3720–3826.

</details>

**6.** Regarding vasoactive drugs in cardiogenic shock:

- A. In SOAP II, dopamine was associated with more arrhythmic events than norepinephrine. — T / F
- B. In the cardiogenic shock subgroup of SOAP II, dopamine was associated with higher 28-day mortality. — T / F
- C. The 2026 ESC guidelines give a class I recommendation for norepinephrine in cardiogenic shock. — T / F
- D. An individual-patient meta-analysis associated epinephrine with higher short-term mortality in cardiogenic shock. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – SOAP II (1679 patients with all types of shock) found arrhythmias in 24.1% with dopamine vs 12.4% with norepinephrine, mostly AF.
- B (True) – in the prespecified CS subgroup (n = 280), dopamine was associated with higher 28-day mortality, although overall mortality did not differ (52.5% vs 48.5%).
- C (False) – ESC 2026 gives norepinephrine **IIb, C** as the preferred vasopressor. No vasopressor carries class I in CS.
- D (True) – Léopold et al. (individual-patient data, 2583 patients) linked epinephrine to higher short-term mortality. The data are observational and cannot prove causality.

**EDIC trap:** "preferred" does not mean class I; read the class carefully.

**Ref:** De Backer D et al., N Engl J Med 2010;362:779–789; Léopold V et al., Intensive Care Med 2018;44:847–856; Køber L et al., 2026 ESC Guidelines for the management of heart failure, Eur Heart J 2026;47:5346–5457.

</details>

**7.** According to the 2026 ESC heart failure guidelines, which recommendation regarding temporary mechanical circulatory support carries class I?

- A. Routine IABP in all cardiogenic shock
- B. Microaxial flow pump in all AMI-related cardiogenic shock
- C. A multidisciplinary Shock Team to guide device selection
- D. VA-ECMO in all patients with lactate > 4 mmol/L
- E. Temporary MCS in unselected AMI-CS

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** ESC 2026 recommends a multidisciplinary shock team (interventional cardiologist, intensivist, cardiac surgeon, HF specialist) for patients who are candidates for MCS, with class **I, C**. Team-based care in hub-and-spoke networks aims to match the right device to the right patient at the right time.

**Why not the others:**
- A – routine IABP in unselected CS is class III, B1 (IABP-SHOCK II showed no mortality benefit).
- B – the microaxial pump is IIa, B1 only in selected STEMI-CS with LV dysfunction and no risk of hypoxic brain injury (DanGer Shock), not in all AMI-CS.
- D – no lactate threshold triggers routine VA-ECMO; ECLS-SHOCK (lactate > 3) showed no benefit and more complications.
- E – temporary MCS in unselected AMI-CS is class III, B1 because of the risk of harm.

**Ref:** Køber L et al., 2026 ESC Guidelines for the management of heart failure, Eur Heart J 2026;47:5346–5457.

</details>

**8.** Haemodynamic profiles of cardiogenic shock:

- A. The "cold and wet" profile is the most common. — T / F
- B. Results of AMI-CS device trials can be directly extrapolated to non-ischaemic HF-related shock. — T / F
- C. Isolated right ventricular failure typically shows low right atrial pressure with a high PCWP. — T / F
- D. A "warm and wet" profile suggests low systemic vascular resistance, e.g. a systemic inflammatory response after MI. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – "cold and wet" (low CI, high PCWP) is the classic and most common CS profile.
- B (False) – IABP-SHOCK II, ECLS-SHOCK and DanGer Shock all enrolled AMI-CS only. HF-related shock differs in pathophysiology and course, so their results should not be extrapolated automatically.
- C (False) – isolated RV failure shows **high** RAP/CVP with a normal or low PCWP, i.e. a high RAP/PCWP ratio (> ~0.8).
- D (True) – "warm and wet" (relatively preserved CI, low SVR, high PCWP) is the "mixed" phenotype, e.g. SIRS or sepsis after MI. Look for infection.

**EDIC trap:** "cold and dry" can be RV infarction or hypovolaemia with LV dysfunction, so assess preload before giving diuretics.

**Ref:** Naidu SS et al., SCAI SHOCK Stage Classification Expert Consensus Update, J Am Coll Cardiol 2022;79:933–946; Køber L et al., 2026 ESC Guidelines for the management of heart failure, Eur Heart J 2026;47:5346–5457.

</details>

**9.** A patient with NSTEMI develops cardiogenic shock. According to the 2023 ESC ACS guidelines, the appropriate invasive strategy is:

- A. Fibrinolysis first, then angiography within 24 hours
- B. Invasive strategy within 72 hours
- C. Early invasive strategy within 24 hours
- D. Immediate invasive strategy
- E. Selective invasive strategy after non-invasive testing

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** In NSTE-ACS, haemodynamic instability or cardiogenic shock is a very-high-risk criterion. ESC 2023 recommends an **immediate** invasive strategy (< 2 h), as in STEMI (I, C), with PCI of the culprit lesion or emergency CABG if the anatomy is unsuitable.

**Why not the others:**
- A – fibrinolysis has no role in NSTE-ACS (no benefit, more bleeding).
- B – the 72-hour window came from older guidelines and does not apply to very-high-risk patients.
- C – the 24-hour window is for high-risk patients (confirmed NSTEMI, dynamic ST changes, GRACE > 140, transient ST elevation).
- E – a selective strategy is for patients without high-risk features, the opposite of shock.

**EDIC trap:** a raised troponin without ST elevation does not mean "within 24 h" when the patient is in shock.

**Ref:** Byrne RA et al., 2023 ESC ACS Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2023;44:3720–3826.

</details>

**10.** Regarding the 2022 SCAI SHOCK update:

- A. Mortality increases progressively across stages A to E in validation studies. — T / F
- B. It was endorsed by the ESC Association for Acute CardioVascular Care. — T / F
- C. Age and frailty are considered modifiable risk factors. — T / F
- D. It introduced a three-axis model including shock severity, phenotype/aetiology and risk modifiers. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – large validation cohorts (e.g. Mayo Clinic CICU) showed a stepwise rise in mortality from stage A to E, which gives the scheme prognostic value.
- B (True) – the 2022 update was endorsed by the ESC Association for Acute CardioVascular Care (ACVC) and by SCCM, among others.
- C (False) – age, frailty and comorbidity are **non-modifiable** risk modifiers. They change prognosis but not the stage.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 58-year-old man with anterior STEMI arrives with BP 92/58 mmHg, cool mottled extremities, oliguria and lactate 4.1 mmol/L. Outline your initial diagnostic work-up and management during the first hours.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- Recognise CS (hypoperfusion from a primary cardiac cause; lactate > 2 supports it, no fixed BP cut-off) and stage it – hypoperfusion needing intervention = at least SCAI C; reassess for deterioration (D).

- Immediate coronary angiography and PCI of the infarct-related artery (SHOCK trial: ↓ 6-month mortality); emergency CABG if PCI not feasible; culprit-only PCI in multivessel disease.
- Arterial line; consider PAC/TPTD if CO monitoring needed (CPO, PAPi, SvO₂).
- Norepinephrine as preferred vasopressor, initial MAP target ≥ 65 mmHg individualised to perfusion; avoid dopamine and routine epinephrine.
- Inotrope (e.g. dobutamine) if hypoperfusion persists despite standard treatment incl. fluid challenge – lowest dose, shortest time.
- Activate the shock team (I, C); consider microaxial pump only in selected STEMI-CS without risk of hypoxic brain injury (IIa); no routine IABP or unselected MCS (III).
- Monitor perfusion targets: lactate clearance, CRT, urine output, mental status.

**O2. (open)** Explain why mixed venous saturation helps distinguish cardiogenic from septic shock, and how PAC-derived indices help identify right ventricular failure.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

## W07 · D2 – Mechanical circulatory support in cardiogenic shock

**1.** In IABP-SHOCK II, intra-aortic balloon pump support in AMI-related cardiogenic shock:

- A. Reduced 30-day mortality by 25 %
- B. Did not reduce 30-day mortality
- C. Was only tested in patients not undergoing revascularisation
- D. Increased the stroke rate significantly
- E. Increased major bleeding significantly

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** IABP-SHOCK II randomised 600 patients with AMI-CS who were planned for early revascularisation to IABP or no IABP. 30-day mortality was 39.7% vs 41.3% (RR 0.96; 95% CI 0.79–1.17; p = 0.69), and there was still no difference at 1 and 6 years. As a result, routine IABP in CS is class III (ESC 2023 ACS; ESC 2026 HF III, B1).

**Why not the others:**
- A – there was no reduction; the confidence interval spans 1.
- C – all patients were planned for early revascularisation, mostly PCI.
- D – stroke rates were low and did not differ significantly.
- E – major bleeding was 3.3% vs 4.4%, not significantly different.

**EDIC trap:** IABP still has a role as a bridge in mechanical complications (acute MR, VSR).

**Ref:** Thiele H et al., N Engl J Med 2012;367:1287–1296; Thiele H et al., Circulation 2019;139:395–403.

</details>

**2.** Regarding the ECLS-SHOCK trial:

- A. Peripheral ischaemic vascular complications were more frequent with ECLS. — T / F
- B. 30-day mortality was significantly lower with early ECLS. — T / F
- C. It enrolled patients with AMI-related cardiogenic shock with lactate > 3 mmol/L planned for revascularisation. — T / F
- D. Moderate or severe bleeding was less frequent with ECLS. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – peripheral ischaemic vascular complications needing surgical or interventional treatment occurred in 11.0% with ECLS vs 3.8% without.
- B (False) – 30-day mortality was 47.8% vs 49.0% (RR 0.98; 0.80–1.19), with no difference.
- C (True) – 420 patients with AMI-CS (STEMI or NSTEMI), planned early revascularisation and lactate > 3 mmol/L; most had been resuscitated before randomisation.
- D (False) – moderate or severe bleeding (BARC 3–5) was **more** frequent with ECLS: 23.4% vs 9.6%.

**EDIC trap:** ECLS-SHOCK argues against *routine early* ECMO, not against individualised rescue ECMO in refractory shock.

**Ref:** Thiele H et al., ECLS-SHOCK, N Engl J Med 2023;389:1286–1297.

</details>

**3.** Which patient best matches the population in which DanGer Shock showed a mortality benefit?

- A. 62-year-old comatose after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest with STEMI and shock
- B. 66-year-old with isolated right ventricular infarction
- C. 45-year-old with fulminant myocarditis and biventricular failure
- D. 70-year-old with NSTEMI, LVEF 55 %, lactate 1.5 mmol/L
- E. 58-year-old with anterior STEMI, LVEF 25 %, lactate 4 mmol/L, awake, on norepinephrine

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** DanGer Shock enrolled STEMI-CS (SBP < 100 mmHg or vasoactive support, lactate ≥ 2.5 mmol/L, LVEF < 45%) and excluded patients who were comatose after OHCA. Impella CP lowered 180-day mortality from 58.5% to 45.8% (HR 0.74). An awake patient with anterior STEMI, EF 25%, lactate 4 and on norepinephrine matches this population exactly.

**Why not the others:**
- A – comatose OHCA survivors were excluded, because hypoxic brain injury drives their outcome; ESC 2026 restricts IIa to patients without this risk.
- B – a left-sided microaxial pump does not support a failing RV; the trial studied LV failure.
- C – myocarditis is non-ischaemic HF-CS, outside the trial population, although MCS may be used on an individual basis.
- D – EF 55% with lactate 1.5 is not CS by trial criteria, and the trial did not include NSTEMI.

**Ref:** Møller JE et al., DanGer Shock, N Engl J Med 2024;390:1382–1393; Køber L et al., 2026 ESC HF Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2026;47:5346–5457.

</details>

**4.** Regarding DanGer Shock (NEJM 2024):

- A. The composite of adverse events was more frequent with the pump. — T / F
- B. Limb ischaemia requiring intervention was more frequent with the pump. — T / F
- C. Renal replacement therapy was less frequent in the pump group. — T / F
- D. All-cause death at 180 days was 45.8 % with the microaxial flow pump vs 58.5 % with standard care. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – the composite safety endpoint (severe bleeding, limb ischaemia, haemolysis, device failure, worsening aortic regurgitation) occurred in 24.0% vs 6.2%.
- B (True) – limb ischaemia needing intervention occurred in 5.6% vs 1.1%.
- C (False) – RRT was **more** frequent with the pump: 41.9% vs 26.7%.
- D (True) – 45.8% vs 58.5% (HR 0.74; 0.55–0.99; p = 0.04). This was the first RCT of MCS in AMI-CS to show a mortality benefit.

**Ref:** Møller JE et al., N Engl J Med 2024;390:1382–1393.

</details>

**5.** A patient on femoral VA-ECMO for cardiogenic shock with severe ARDS shows recovering LV function. Right radial PaO₂ is 6 kPa, femoral arterial PaO₂ 35 kPa. The most appropriate intervention is:

- A. Improve native lung gas exchange and consider conversion to V-AV ECMO
- B. Increase FiO₂ on the oxygenator
- C. Increase sweep gas flow
- D. Reduce ECMO flow to the minimum
- E. Insert a distal perfusion cannula

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** With femoral VA-ECMO, oxygenated blood returns retrogradely up the aorta. The recovering LV ejects deoxygenated blood from the failing lungs into the aortic root, which supplies the coronaries, carotids and right subclavian artery. A right radial PaO₂ of 6 kPa against a femoral PaO₂ of 35 kPa is Harlequin (differential) hypoxaemia. The fix is to treat the source: optimise ventilation (PEEP, FiO₂, recruitment) and/or add a jugular return cannula (V-AV ECMO).

**Why not the others:**
- B – oxygenator FiO₂ only raises the saturation of ECMO blood, which already goes to the lower body.
- C – sweep gas mainly removes CO₂ from ECMO blood and does nothing for blood leaving the native lungs.
- D – lower ECMO flow lets more hypoxic native output into the aorta and moves the mixing zone distally.
- E – a distal perfusion cannula prevents limb ischaemia and does not affect upper-body oxygenation.

**Ref:** Lorusso R et al., ELSO interim guidelines for VA-ECMO in adult cardiac patients, ASAIO J 2021;67:827–844.

</details>

**6.** Signs suggesting left ventricular distension on peripheral VA-ECMO include:

- A. Aortic valve remaining closed on echocardiography — T / F
- B. Loss of arterial pulse pressure — T / F
- C. Spontaneous echo contrast ("smoke") in the left ventricle — T / F
- D. Rapid resolution of pulmonary oedema — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – the weak LV cannot generate a pressure above the aortic pressure raised by retrograde flow, so the aortic valve stays closed.
- B (True) – a pulse pressure falling towards 0 (flat arterial trace) means the LV is no longer ejecting.
- C (True) – spontaneous echo contrast ("smoke") in the LV or aortic root shows stasis and a high risk of thrombus.
- D (False) – LV distension raises LVEDP, LA pressure and PCWP, so pulmonary oedema **progresses**.

**EDIC trap:** treat promptly: lower ECMO flow, start an inotrope, reduce afterload, then IABP, Impella or a vent.

**Ref:** Lorusso R et al., ELSO interim guidelines for VA-ECMO in adult cardiac patients, ASAIO J 2021;67:827–844; Kim MC et al., EARLY-UNLOAD, Circulation 2023;148:1570–1581.

</details>

**7.** The haemodynamic effect of a transvalvular microaxial flow pump (Impella) on the left ventricle is:

- A. Increase in LV afterload
- B. Right ventricular unloading
- C. Direct LV unloading with reduced end-diastolic pressure
- D. Full biventricular and respiratory support
- E. Diastolic augmentation only

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The Impella aspirates blood from the LV through a cannula across the aortic valve and ejects it into the ascending aorta, so it works in series with the LV. This directly unloads the ventricle, lowering LVEDP, wall stress and myocardial O₂ consumption while increasing forward flow (Impella CP about 3–4 L/min).

**Why not the others:**
- A – a rise in LV afterload is the problem with peripheral VA-ECMO (retrograde flow).
- B – a left-sided pump does not unload the RV and may increase RV preload; RV support needs Impella RP or an RVAD.
- D – full biventricular and respiratory support is provided by VA-ECMO.
- E – diastolic augmentation is how the IABP works.

**EDIC trap:** contraindications include LV thrombus, a mechanical aortic valve, severe AS/AR and severe PAD.

**Ref:** Møller JE et al., N Engl J Med 2024;390:1382–1393; Køber L et al., 2026 ESC HF Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2026;47:5346–5457.

</details>

**8.** Regarding left ventricular unloading during VA-ECMO:

- A. Inotropes are contraindicated because they prevent aortic valve opening. — T / F
- B. In EARLY-UNLOAD, early routine transseptal left atrial venting reduced 30-day mortality. — T / F
- C. In ECLS-SHOCK, active LV unloading was used in the majority of ECLS patients. — T / F
- D. An IABP or microaxial flow pump cannot be used to unload the LV. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – F.**

- A (False) – inotropes (e.g. dobutamine) increase contractility so the aortic valve opens, and are a first step against LV distension together with reducing ECMO flow.
- B (False) – EARLY-UNLOAD (116 patients) found 30-day mortality of 46.6% with early routine transseptal LA venting vs 44.8% (NS); the control arm allowed rescue unloading.
- C (False) – only ~6% of ECLS patients received active LV unloading, a frequent criticism of the trial.
- D (False) – both are used: the IABP reduces afterload and the microaxial pump ("ECPELLA") unloads the LV directly; septostomy or an LA vent are alternatives.

**EDIC trap:** no RCT shows a benefit from routine early unloading; unloading is reactive, guided by echo and pulse pressure.

**Ref:** Kim MC et al., Circulation 2023;148:1570–1581; Thiele H et al., N Engl J Med 2023;389:1286–1297.

</details>

**9.** Which statement about the IPD meta-analysis of VA-ECMO trials in infarct-related cardiogenic shock (Lancet 2023) is correct?

- A. It included only observational studies
- B. It showed benefit only in patients older than 75 years
- C. VA-ECMO reduced 30-day mortality by about one third
- D. VA-ECMO did not reduce 30-day mortality and increased major bleeding and vascular complications
- E. It found fewer vascular complications with VA-ECMO

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** The individual-patient-data meta-analysis pooled 4 RCTs (ECLS-SHOCK, ECMO-CS, EURO SHOCK and the ECLS-SHOCK pilot), 567 patients with AMI-CS. VA-ECMO did not reduce 30-day mortality (OR 0.93; 0.66–1.29), but it increased major bleeding (OR 2.44) and peripheral vascular complications (OR 3.53).

**Why not the others:**
- A – only randomised trials were included.
- B – no subgroup, including age, showed a significant benefit.
- C – mortality was not reduced.

**7.** In a mechanically ventilated patient, pulmonary vascular resistance is lowest at:

- A. Lung volumes associated with atelectasis
- B. Maximal PEEP
- C. Functional residual capacity
- D. Total lung capacity
- E. Residual volume

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

- D – at TLC, compression of alveolar vessels is maximal.

- C (False) – monitor from the **right radial** artery (ABG, right-hand SpO₂) and with cerebral NIRS. Femoral blood mainly reflects the ECMO circuit.
- D (True) – depending on the mixing zone, the coronaries, carotids and right subclavian artery receive poorly oxygenated native output.

**Ref:** Lorusso R et al., ELSO VA-ECMO interim guidelines, ASAIO J 2021;67:827–844.

**7.** A 70-year-old on long-term bisoprolol is admitted with low-output heart failure (SBP 92 mmHg, cold extremities, lactate 3 mmol/L) and remains hypoperfused after cautious fluids. According to ESC 2026, which inotrope may be preferred over dobutamine?

- A. Isoprenaline
- B. Dopamine
- C. Vasopressin
- D. Phenylephrine
- E. Levosimendan or a PDE-3 inhibitor

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Long-term β-blockade (bisoprolol) blunts β1-agonists such as dobutamine. Levosimendan (troponin C sensitisation plus K-ATP vasodilation) and PDE-3 inhibitors such as milrinone (cAMP rise downstream of the receptor) work independently of the β-receptor, and ESC 2026 notes they may be preferred. Both are inodilators, so with SBP 92 mmHg norepinephrine is often needed and a bolus is usually omitted.

**Why not the others:**
- A – isoprenaline is a non-selective β-agonist that β-blockade blunts just as much, and it causes tachycardia and vasodilation.
- B – dopamine also depends on the β-receptor and caused more arrhythmias, with higher mortality in the CS subgroup of SOAP II.
- C – vasopressin is a vasopressor with no inotropic effect.
- D – phenylephrine is a pure α1-agonist that raises afterload and can lower CO.

**Ref:** Køber L et al., 2026 ESC HF Guidelines, Eur Heart J 2026;47:5346–5457; Mebazaa A et al., JAMA 2007;297:1883–1891.

**8.** Regarding trials of inotropes:

- A. In SURVIVE, 180-day mortality did not differ significantly. — T / F
- B. DOREMI compared milrinone with dobutamine in cardiogenic shock. — T / F
- C. SURVIVE compared levosimendan with dobutamine in acute decompensated heart failure. — T / F
- D. DOREMI found a significant mortality benefit with milrinone. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – 180-day mortality was 26% vs 28% (HR 0.91), not significant.
- B (True) – DOREMI was a double-blind trial in 192 CS patients (SCAI B–E).
- C (True) – SURVIVE enrolled 1327 patients with ADHF who needed inotropic support.
- D (False) – in-hospital mortality was 37% vs 43% (RR 0.85, NS), and the primary composite was 49% vs 54% (NS).

**9.** A 76-year-old woman presents with acute pulmonary oedema, BP 200/110 mmHg, RR 34/min, SpO₂ 84 %, pH 7.24, PaCO₂ 7.5 kPa, alert and cooperative. The best initial respiratory support is:

- A. Heliox
- B. Non-invasive ventilation (CPAP or NIPPV)
- C. Intravenous morphine and oxygen
- D. Immediate intubation
- E. Low-flow oxygen 2 L/min only

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** This is hypertensive APO with hypoxaemia, tachypnoea and respiratory acidosis (pH 7.24, PaCO₂ 7.5 kPa) in an alert, cooperative patient, so NIV (CPAP or NIPPV) is indicated. **11.** A patient with ARDS from pneumonia has driving pressure 19 cmH₂O, PaO₂/FiO₂ 110 mmHg and PaCO₂ 52 mmHg (7 kPa). Echo shows a dilated RV with paradoxical septal motion. Which measure is most likely to help the right ventricle?

- A. Fluid bolus of 1 L
- B. Increase PEEP to maximal tolerated values
- C. Start a beta-blocker
- D. Prone positioning with reduction of driving pressure and PaCO₂
- E. Permissive hypercapnia with PaCO₂ target 8 kPa

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** All four ACP risk factors are present: pneumonia, driving pressure ≥ 18 (19 cmH₂O), PaO₂/FiO₂ < 150 (110 mmHg) and PaCO₂ ≥ 48 (52 mmHg). A dilated RV with paradoxical septal motion confirms acute cor pulmonale. Prone positioning recruits lung, lowers driving pressure and PaCO₂ and unloads the RV; combine it with a lower tidal volume and correction of hypercapnia (e.g. less dead space). Consider VV-ECMO if this is not enough.

**Why not the others:**
- A – fluids worsen RV dilatation and septal shift.
- B – maximal PEEP overdistends the lung and raises PVR.
- C – a β-blocker lowers contractility and heart rate, and so CO in RV failure.
- E – a PaCO₂ of 8 kPa (60 mmHg) worsens pulmonary vasoconstriction; the target is < 48 mmHg (~6.4 kPa).

**13.** A 66-year-old with inferior STEMI is hypotensive (82/50 mmHg) with distended neck veins and clear lungs. ST elevation is present in V4R. Which initial treatment should be avoided?

- A. Temporary pacing for complete AV block
- B. Cautious fluid challenge
- C. Primary PCI of the right coronary artery
- D. Dobutamine if hypoperfusion persists
- E. Intravenous nitrates

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Inferior STEMI with hypotension, raised JVP, clear lungs and ST elevation in V4R is RV infarction. The ischaemic RV depends on preload, so nitrates (and diuretics), which dilate veins, can cause profound hypotension and should be avoided.

**Why not the others:**
- A – AV block is common with proximal RCA occlusion; pacing (ideally AV-sequential) restores rate and atrial kick.
- B – a cautious fluid challenge is reasonable if the patient is not volume-overloaded; avoid large volumes.
- C – primary PCI of the RCA is the key treatment of the cause.
- D – dobutamine supports RV contractility if hypoperfusion persists after preload is optimised.

**EDIC trap:** record right-sided leads in every inferior STEMI before giving nitrates.

**15.** A 52-year-old with anterior STEMI and cardiogenic shock (LVEF 25 %, lactate 5 mmol/L) is awake and was not resuscitated. He remains hypoperfused despite norepinephrine after primary PCI. According to ESC 2026, which option has class IIa support?

- A. Dopamine as first-line vasopressor
- B. No mechanical support should be considered
- C. Routine VA-ECMO for all AMI-CS
- D. Microaxial flow pump
- E. Routine IABP

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** An awake, non-resuscitated patient with anterior STEMI-CS, LVEF 25% and lactate 5 mmol/L despite norepinephrine after PCI matches the DanGer Shock population (180-day mortality 45.8% vs 58.5%). ESC 2026 gives a microaxial flow pump IIa, B1 in selected STEMI-CS with LV dysfunction and no risk of hypoxic brain injury. **Why not the others:**
