## W25 · D1 – Pre-eclampsia, eclampsia and HELLP syndrome

**1.** A 31-year-old primigravida at 35 weeks presents with severe headache. BP 172/114 mmHg on repeated measurements, platelets 128 × 10⁹/L, uPCR 85 mg/mmol, brisk reflexes with 2 beats of ankle clonus. Which is the most appropriate immediate management?

- A. Sublingual nifedipine 10 mg and immediate caesarean section
- B. Oral methyldopa 500 mg and repeat BP in 4 hours
- C. IV enalapril 1.25 mg and fluid bolus 1 L
- D. IV labetalol titrated to BP, magnesium sulphate 4 g IV load then 1 g/h, and planning of delivery once stable
- E. Diazepam 10 mg IV for seizure prophylaxis and fluid restriction

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** This is pre-eclampsia with severe hypertension and neurological features. Systolic BP ≥ 160 mmHg must be lowered within 30–60 min (stroke risk), MgSO₄ prevents eclampsia (Magpie), and at 35 weeks delivery is planned once the mother is stabilised.

**Why not the others:**
- A – sublingual nifedipine causes abrupt hypotension and fetal distress; caesarean section before maternal stabilisation is unsafe.
- B – methyldopa is a slow maintenance drug, not for acute severe hypertension.
- C – ACE inhibitors are contraindicated before delivery, and fluid boluses risk pulmonary oedema.
- E – diazepam is inferior to MgSO₄ for preventing and treating eclamptic seizures.

**Ref:** Magee LA (ISSHP), Pregnancy Hypertens 2022; Magpie Trial, Lancet 2002.

</details>

**2.** According to the ISSHP 2021 definitions of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy:

- A. Pre-eclampsia can be diagnosed without proteinuria if there is new maternal organ dysfunction. — T / F
- B. Proteinuria above 5 g/day is an indication for immediate delivery. — T / F
- C. New-onset hypertension at 16 weeks of gestation is classified as gestational hypertension. — T / F
- D. Fetal growth restriction in a woman with gestational hypertension fulfils the definition of pre-eclampsia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – AKI, liver involvement, neurological or haematological complications define pre-eclampsia even without proteinuria.
- B (False) – the amount of proteinuria neither grades severity nor determines timing of delivery.
- C (False) – hypertension before 20 weeks is chronic (pre-existing) hypertension; gestational hypertension starts at ≥ 20 weeks.
- D (True) – uteroplacental dysfunction (growth restriction, abnormal Doppler, stillbirth) is one of the defining features.

**Ref:** Magee LA (ISSHP), Pregnancy Hypertens 2022.

</details>

**3.** A 24-year-old woman at 37 weeks with known pre-eclampsia has a generalised tonic-clonic seizure on the labour ward. She is placed in the left lateral position and given oxygen; the seizure is still ongoing after 1 minute. What is the drug treatment of choice?

- A. Lorazepam 4 mg IV, then levetiracetam 60 mg/kg IV
- B. Magnesium sulphate 4 g IV over 5–15 min, then 1 g/h for 24 h
- C. Phenytoin 20 mg/kg IV
- D. Propofol 2 mg/kg IV and intubation
- E. Diazepam 10 mg IV followed by a diazepam infusion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** Magnesium sulphate is the anticonvulsant of choice for eclampsia; in the Collaborative Eclampsia Trial it reduced recurrent seizures by 52 % vs diazepam and 67 % vs phenytoin. Continue 1 g/h for 24 h after the last seizure or delivery; give a further 2–4 g bolus for recurrence.

**Why not the others:**
- A – benzodiazepines and levetiracetam are second-line in eclampsia.
- C – phenytoin was clearly inferior to MgSO₄.
- D – anaesthetic induction is reserved for seizures refractory to MgSO₄ or airway compromise.
- E – diazepam was inferior and causes neonatal sedation.

**Ref:** Eclampsia Trial Collaborative Group, Lancet 1995.

</details>

**4.** A 29-year-old woman with severe pre-eclampsia receives magnesium sulphate 1 g/h for 20 hours after caesarean section. Urine output has been 15 mL/h for 6 hours. She is drowsy, RR 8/min, SpO₂ 91 % on 4 L/min oxygen, and patellar reflexes are absent. What is the most appropriate immediate action?

- A. Fluid challenge of 1,000 mL crystalloid
- B. Reduce the infusion to 0.5 g/h and recheck reflexes in 1 hour
- C. Naloxone 0.4 mg IV
- D. Furosemide 40 mg IV and continue magnesium
- E. Stop the infusion, give calcium gluconate 10 % 10 mL IV and support ventilation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Areflexia and respiratory depression in a woman with oliguria indicate magnesium toxicity (levels likely > 5 mmol/L). Stop the infusion, give calcium gluconate 1 g IV over ~10 min and support breathing (bag-mask, intubation if needed); measure serum Mg.

**Why not the others:**
- A – large fluid boluses risk pulmonary oedema in pre-eclampsia and do not treat toxicity.
- B – reducing the dose is inadequate with signs of respiratory depression.
- C – opioids are not the cause of areflexia.
- D – furosemide may increase Mg excretion but continuing magnesium is wrong.

**EDIC trap:** Loss of patellar reflexes is the first warning sign – check them hourly.

**Ref:** Eclampsia Trial Collaborative Group, Lancet 1995; NICE NG133.

</details>

**5.** A 33-year-old woman develops epigastric pain 12 hours after a vaginal delivery at 38 weeks. BP 150/95 mmHg, platelets 62 × 10⁹/L, AST 310 U/L, LDH 980 U/L, schistocytes on the film. Regarding her condition:

- A. Up to one-third of cases of this syndrome present after delivery. — T / F
- B. High-dose dexamethasone is recommended to improve maternal outcome. — T / F
- C. Sudden shoulder-tip pain with hypotension should prompt urgent imaging for a subcapsular hepatic haematoma. — T / F
- D. Platelet count may continue to fall for 24–48 hours after delivery. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – T.**

- A (True) – about 30 % of HELLP syndrome appears post-partum.
- B (False) – RCTs showed faster laboratory recovery but no clinical benefit; corticosteroids are given only for fetal lung maturity.
- C (True) – hepatic haematoma/rupture is a life-threatening complication: CT, angio-embolisation or surgery.
- D (True) – the nadir is typically at 24–48 h; failure to recover after 72–96 h suggests TTP, aHUS or another diagnosis.

**Ref:** Magee LA (ISSHP), Pregnancy Hypertens 2022.

</details>

**6.** A 34-year-old woman at 32 weeks with pre-eclampsia is managed expectantly after antenatal corticosteroids. All of the following are indications to proceed to delivery EXCEPT:

- A. Pulmonary oedema
- B. BP persistently ≥ 160/110 mmHg despite three antihypertensive agents
- C. Increase in proteinuria from 1.2 to 5.5 g/day
- D. Platelets falling from 140 to 70 × 10⁹/L over 24 hours
- E. Eclamptic seizure

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The quantity of proteinuria has no prognostic value for maternal complications and is not an indication for delivery under ISSHP or NICE; decisions are based on maternal organ dysfunction, BP control and fetal status.

**Why not the others:**
- A – pulmonary oedema is a maternal indication for delivery.
- B – refractory severe hypertension is an indication regardless of gestation.
- D – progressive thrombocytopenia (evolving HELLP/DIC) is an indication.
- E – eclampsia requires delivery once the mother is stabilised.

**Ref:** Magee LA (ISSHP), Pregnancy Hypertens 2022; NICE NG133.

</details>

**7.** Regarding randomised trials in hypertensive disorders of pregnancy:

- A. In the Magpie trial, magnesium sulphate significantly reduced maternal mortality. — T / F
- B. In CHIPS, less-tight control (target diastolic 100 mmHg) improved perinatal outcomes compared with tight control (target 85 mmHg). — T / F
- C. In CHAP, treating mild chronic hypertension to below 140/90 mmHg increased the rate of small-for-gestational-age infants. — T / F
- D. In ASPRE, aspirin 150 mg daily from the first trimester reduced preterm pre-eclampsia. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (False) – Magpie halved eclampsia (RR 0.42); the reduction in maternal death (RR 0.55) was not significant.
- B (False) – perinatal outcomes were similar; less-tight control caused more severe maternal hypertension (40.6 vs 27.5 %).
- C (False) – CHAP reduced the composite adverse outcome (30.2 vs 37.0 %) without increasing SGA.
- D (True) – preterm pre-eclampsia 1.6 vs 4.3 % (OR 0.38) with aspirin started at 11–14 weeks.

**Ref:** Magpie Trial, Lancet 2002; Magee LA (CHIPS), NEJM 2015; Tita AT (CHAP), NEJM 2022; Rolnik DL (ASPRE), NEJM 2017.

</details>

**8.** A 27-year-old woman is brought to the emergency department on day 6 after an uncomplicated vaginal delivery with a generalised seizure, preceded by 2 days of headache. BP 158/102 mmHg; she is now drowsy with no focal deficit. Urine shows 2+ protein. What is the most appropriate management?

- A. Magnesium sulphate loading and infusion, antihypertensive treatment and brain imaging
- B. Levetiracetam and outpatient EEG; late eclampsia cannot occur beyond 48 hours
- C. Lumbar puncture before any treatment
- D. Phenytoin load and discharge when awake
- E. Observation only, as BP is below 160/110 mmHg

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Post-partum eclampsia can occur up to several weeks after delivery and is treated with MgSO₄ and BP control. A late (> 48–72 h) post-partum seizure should be imaged (CT/MRI) to exclude cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, haemorrhage and to identify PRES.

**Why not the others:**
- B – late post-partum eclampsia is well described; MgSO₄ is first-line.
- C – lumbar puncture is not the first step and is unsafe before imaging in a drowsy patient.
- D – phenytoin is inferior and discharge is unsafe.
- E – eclampsia often occurs with only moderately raised BP; the seizure mandates treatment.

**Ref:** Magee LA (ISSHP), Pregnancy Hypertens 2022; Eclampsia Trial Collaborative Group, Lancet 1995.

</details>

**9.** Regarding ICU management of severe pre-eclampsia:

- A. Maintenance fluids should be restricted to around 80 mL/h. — T / F
- B. Post-partum oliguria should be treated with repeated 500 mL fluid boluses until urine output exceeds 0.5 mL/kg/h. — T / F
- C. Magnesium sulphate potentiates non-depolarising neuromuscular blocking agents. — T / F
- D. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors are contraindicated during breastfeeding. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – T, D – F.**

- A (True) – capillary leak and low colloid osmotic pressure make pulmonary oedema a major iatrogenic risk (NICE: ~80 mL/h).
- B (False) – oliguria in pre-eclampsia usually resolves spontaneously; repeated boluses cause pulmonary oedema.
- C (True) – monitor with a nerve stimulator and reduce rocuronium doses.
- D (False) – ACE inhibitors are contraindicated antenatally, but enalapril and captopril are compatible with breastfeeding.

**Ref:** NICE NG133; Magee LA (ISSHP), Pregnancy Hypertens 2022.

</details>

**10.** A 36-year-old woman underwent caesarean section 6 hours ago for HELLP syndrome (platelets 41 × 10⁹/L). She now complains of severe right upper quadrant and right shoulder pain; HR 128/min, BP 85/50 mmHg, haemoglobin falling from 112 to 74 g/L. Abdominal ultrasound shows free fluid; the uterus is well contracted with minimal vaginal loss. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A. Uterine atony
- B. Acute fatty liver of pregnancy
- C. Pulmonary embolism
- D. Amniotic fluid embolism
- E. Ruptured subcapsular hepatic haematoma

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** Severe RUQ and shoulder-tip pain with haemorrhagic shock and haemoperitoneum in HELLP syndrome suggests hepatic haematoma rupture. Management is massive transfusion, correction of coagulopathy and urgent CT with angio-embolisation, or laparotomy with perihepatic packing.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the uterus is contracted and vaginal loss minimal.
- B – AFLP causes liver failure (hypoglycaemia, coagulopathy, jaundice), not haemoperitoneum.
- C – PE causes hypoxaemia and RV strain, not falling haemoglobin and free fluid.
- D – AFE presents with sudden cardiorespiratory collapse and DIC around delivery, not with localised pain and haemoperitoneum.

**Ref:** Magee LA (ISSHP), Pregnancy Hypertens 2022.

</details>

**11.** A 30-year-old woman at 31 weeks has BP 145/92 mmHg, mild headache and no proteinuria; liver enzymes, platelets and creatinine are normal. Her sFlt-1/PlGF ratio is 22. What does this result mean?

- A. Pre-eclampsia is confirmed and delivery should be planned within 1 week
- B. Pre-eclampsia within the next week is very unlikely (negative predictive value ~99 %)
- C. HELLP syndrome is likely within 48 hours
- D. Aspirin 150 mg should be started now to prevent pre-eclampsia
- E. The result is uninterpretable before 34 weeks

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** In PROGNOSIS, an sFlt-1/PlGF ratio ≤ 38 ruled out pre-eclampsia within 1 week with an NPV of 99.3 % in women with suspected disease at 24–37 weeks. She still needs follow-up for gestational hypertension.

**Why not the others:**
- A – a low ratio argues against pre-eclampsia; even a high ratio (> 38) has a PPV of only ~37 % for pre-eclampsia within 4 weeks.
- C – a low ratio does not predict HELLP.
- D – aspirin prophylaxis must start before 16 weeks to be effective.
- E – the ratio was validated from 24 weeks + 0 days to 36 weeks + 6 days.

**Ref:** Zeisler H (PROGNOSIS), NEJM 2016; Rolnik DL (ASPRE), NEJM 2017.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 28-year-old woman at 33 weeks with pre-eclampsia is admitted to the ICU with BP 175/115 mmHg, platelets 85 × 10⁹/L and SpO₂ 92 % on air with bilateral crackles. Outline your management.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

Key points expected:
- **Multidisciplinary team:** obstetrician, obstetric anaesthetist, neonatologist, midwife; left lateral position, fetal monitoring.
- **Pulmonary oedema:** oxygen/NIV (or HFNO), upright position, **IV nitrate infusion**, furosemide; strict fluid balance (~80 mL/h total), avoid fluid boluses; echocardiography (diastolic dysfunction, peripartum cardiomyopathy).

## W25 · D2 – Postpartum haemorrhage and amniotic fluid embolism

**1.** A 26-year-old woman delivers vaginally after an induced labour. Thirty minutes later the calibrated drape shows 320 mL of blood; HR 118/min, BP 94/58 mmHg. The uterus feels soft. According to the WHO/FIGO/ICM 2025 guideline, what is the most appropriate interpretation and action?

- A. Normal blood loss after vaginal birth – reassess in 1 hour
- B. PPH is not yet present because blood loss is below 500 mL – start oxytocin infusion only
- C. PPH is diagnosed – start the full treatment bundle immediately (uterine massage, oxytocics, tranexamic acid, IV fluids, genital tract examination, escalation)
- D. PPH is diagnosed only if blood loss reaches 1,000 mL – prepare for transfusion
- E. Tachycardia is expected after birth – give oral fluids

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**C.**

**Why C:** The 2025 consolidated guideline diagnoses PPH at ≥ 500 mL, or at ≥ 300 mL with any abnormal vital sign; this woman has 320 mL with tachycardia and a shock index > 1. The MOTIVE bundle should be applied immediately, as in E-MOTIVE.

**Why not the others:**
- A – she already meets the definition and is haemodynamically compromised.
- B – the 300 mL threshold with abnormal vital signs applies; giving only part of the bundle delays treatment.
- D – 1,000 mL defines severe PPH, not PPH.
- E – tachycardia with a soft uterus signals early hypovolaemia.

**Ref:** WHO/FIGO/ICM 2025; Gallos I, Lancet 2025; Gallos I (E-MOTIVE), NEJM 2023.

</details>

**2.** Regarding tranexamic acid in postpartum haemorrhage (WOMAN trial):

- A. The dose studied was 1 g IV over 10 minutes, with a second 1 g if bleeding continued after 30 minutes. — T / F
- B. The relative benefit was greatest when tranexamic acid was given more than 3 hours after birth. — T / F
- C. Tranexamic acid significantly increased venous thromboembolic events. — T / F
- D. Tranexamic acid reduced death due to bleeding but did not reduce hysterectomy. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – second dose if bleeding continued after 30 min or restarted within 24 h.
- B (False) – benefit was seen only within 3 h (RR 0.69); no benefit later.
- C (False) – thromboembolic events were not increased.
- D (True) – death due to bleeding 1.5 vs 1.9 % (RR 0.81); hysterectomy and all-cause mortality were unchanged.

**Ref:** WOMAN Trial Collaborators, Lancet 2017.

</details>

**3.** A 33-year-old woman with severe pre-eclampsia and brittle asthma has atonic PPH after vaginal delivery. She has received oxytocin 5 IU slow IV and an oxytocin infusion; the uterus remains boggy despite massage, and tranexamic acid has been given. Which uterotonic is most appropriate next?

- A. Ergometrine 0.5 mg IM
- B. Carboprost 250 µg IM
- C. Carboprost 250 µg IV bolus
- D. Misoprostol 800 µg sublingually
- E. Syntometrine (oxytocin plus ergometrine) IM

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Misoprostol (PGE1) has no contraindication in hypertension or asthma and is an effective additional uterotonic, while balloon tamponade and surgical options are prepared.

**Why not the others:**
- A – ergometrine causes vasoconstriction and severe hypertension; contraindicated in pre-eclampsia.
- B – carboprost (PGF2α) causes bronchospasm; contraindicated in asthma.
- C – carboprost must never be given IV, and is contraindicated here anyway.
- E – Syntometrine contains ergometrine.

**Ref:** Mavrides E (RCOG), BJOG 2017; WHO/FIGO/ICM 2025.

</details>

**4.** Regarding fibrinogen in obstetric haemorrhage:

- A. Plasma fibrinogen at term is normally 4–6 g/L. — T / F
- B. A fibrinogen below 2 g/L at the diagnosis of PPH predicts progression to severe haemorrhage. — T / F
- C. In OBS2, pre-emptive fibrinogen concentrate given when FIBTEM A5 was ≤ 15 mm reduced red-cell transfusion. — T / F
- D. Early severe hypofibrinogenaemia is typical of amniotic fluid embolism and placental abruption. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – T, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – pregnancy is hypercoagulable; fibrinogen rises markedly.
- B (True) – Charbit 2007: fibrinogen < 2 g/L had a PPV of 100 % for severe PPH.
- C (False) – OBS2 found no reduction in bleeding or transfusion; benefit is plausible only with FIBTEM A5 < 12 mm or Clauss < 2 g/L.
- D (True) – consumptive DIC with hyperfibrinolysis develops early, out of proportion to blood loss.

**Ref:** Charbit B, J Thromb Haemost 2007; Collins PW (OBS2), Br J Anaesth 2017.

</details>

**5.** A 38-year-old woman in induced labour at 40 weeks suddenly becomes agitated and dyspnoeic 10 minutes after artificial rupture of membranes. SpO₂ 78 %, BP 70/40 mmHg, temperature 36.9 °C; the fetal heart rate drops to 70/min. Twenty minutes later she bleeds from IV sites; fibrinogen is 0.8 g/L. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- A. Amniotic fluid embolism
- B. Anaphylaxis to oxytocin
- C. Massive pulmonary thromboembolism
- D. Septic shock from chorioamnionitis
- E. Eclampsia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** Sudden hypoxaemia and hypotension during labour, followed by overt DIC with very low fibrinogen, in an afebrile woman, fulfils the Clark/SMFM criteria for AFE. Induction and membrane rupture are recognised associations.

**Why not the others:**
- B – anaphylaxis to oxytocin is exceptional and does not cause early fulminant DIC; rash, bronchospasm and angio-oedema would be expected.
- C – PE causes hypoxaemia and RV failure but not early overt DIC.
- D – she is afebrile and the onset is abrupt.
- E – eclampsia presents with seizures and hypertension, not hypotension and DIC.

**Ref:** Clark SL, Am J Obstet Gynecol 2016; Pacheco LD (SMFM), Am J Obstet Gynecol 2016.

</details>

**6.** Regarding amniotic fluid embolism:

- A. Onset must occur during labour or within 30 minutes of delivery of the placenta to meet the research case definition. — T / F
- B. Fever of 38 °C or more during labour supports the diagnosis. — T / F
- C. Fetal squames in pulmonary artery blood are pathognomonic. — T / F
- D. Early echocardiography typically shows acute right ventricular dilatation and dysfunction. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – T, B – F, C – F, D – T.**

- A (True) – one of the four Clark/SMFM criteria.
- B (False) – absence of fever is a criterion, to exclude sepsis.
- C (False) – fetal squames are found in many pregnant women without AFE; there is no diagnostic test.
- D (True) – acute pulmonary vasoconstriction causes RV failure; LV failure follows.

**Ref:** Clark SL, Am J Obstet Gynecol 2016; Pacheco LD (SMFM), Am J Obstet Gynecol 2016.

</details>

**7.** A 32-year-old woman with suspected amniotic fluid embolism has return of spontaneous circulation after 6 minutes of CPR and a resuscitative caesarean delivery. She is intubated; BP 75/40 mmHg, echo shows a dilated, hypokinetic RV with a D-shaped LV, fibrinogen 0.9 g/L. All of the following are appropriate EXCEPT:

- A. Noradrenaline to maintain systemic and RV coronary perfusion pressure
- B. Rapid infusion of 3 L of crystalloid to raise central venous pressure
- C. Inhaled nitric oxide or epoprostenol
- D. Fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate guided by levels/viscoelastic testing
- E. Referral for VA-ECMO if shock remains refractory

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**B.**

**Why B:** In AFE the initial lesion is acute RV failure; large volume loading distends the RV, shifts the septum and worsens LV filling and cardiac output. Fluids should be cautious, and crystalloid excess also worsens coagulopathy.

**Why not the others:**
- A – vasopressors protect RV coronary perfusion (SMFM).
- C – pulmonary vasodilators reduce RV afterload.
- D – early, aggressive replacement of fibrinogen and factors is recommended.
- E – VA-ECMO is a recognised rescue for refractory cardiogenic shock.

**Ref:** Pacheco LD (SMFM), Am J Obstet Gynecol 2016.

</details>

**8.** A 35-year-old woman has a 3,500 mL atonic PPH after caesarean section. Despite uterotonics, tranexamic acid, an intrauterine balloon and a B-Lynch suture, bleeding continues; she remains hypotensive (BP 70/40 mmHg) on noradrenaline with ongoing transfusion. Interventional radiology is not available for 2 hours. What is the most appropriate next step?

- A. Further carboprost doses beyond 8 doses
- B. Transfer to another hospital for uterine artery embolisation
- C. Recombinant factor VIIa as definitive treatment
- D. Expectant management with continued transfusion
- E. Hysterectomy

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** After failure of pharmacological, mechanical and conservative surgical measures in an unstable woman, hysterectomy should be performed without delay ("sooner rather than later"). Interventional radiology is appropriate only in a stable or stabilising patient with immediate access.

**Why not the others:**
- A – the maximum carboprost dose is 2 mg (8 doses); more doses add toxicity, not efficacy.
- B – transfer of a haemodynamically unstable bleeding patient is unsafe.
- C – rFVIIa is a last-resort adjunct, not definitive haemostasis, and carries thrombotic risk.
- D – continued bleeding with shock requires source control.

**Ref:** Mavrides E (RCOG), BJOG 2017; WHO/FIGO/ICM 2025.

</details>

**10.** A 37-year-old woman with two previous caesarean sections has an anterior placenta praevia; ultrasound and MRI at 28 weeks suggest placenta percreta. What is the most appropriate delivery plan?

- A. Await spontaneous labour and attempt vaginal birth
- B. Caesarean section at 39 weeks in the local unit with manual removal of the placenta
- C. Caesarean section at 39 weeks with carbetocin prophylaxis only
- D. Planned caesarean hysterectomy at about 34–36 weeks in a specialist centre, leaving the placenta in situ
- E. Immediate delivery at 28 weeks

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**D.**

**Why D:** Placenta accreta spectrum after previous caesarean sections with praevia carries a very high risk of massive haemorrhage. Planned delivery at ~34–36 weeks (before labour) in a centre with a multidisciplinary team, cell salvage, blood bank and interventional radiology, with hysterectomy and the placenta left in situ, reduces blood loss.

**Why not the others:**
- A – labour with a praevia/percreta risks catastrophic bleeding.
- B – attempting to remove an invasive placenta causes torrential haemorrhage; waiting until 39 weeks risks emergency presentation.
- C – uterotonics do not prevent bleeding from the placental bed of an accreta.
- E – there is no indication for delivery at 28 weeks in a stable woman.

**Ref:** Mavrides E (RCOG), BJOG 2017.

</details>

**O1. (open)** A 30-year-old woman has a 1,800 mL atonic PPH 1 hour after vaginal delivery, HR 135/min, BP 80/45 mmHg. Describe your stepwise management.

<details><summary>Model answer</summary>

- E – bispecific T-cell engagers cause CRS, typically after step-up or first full doses; infection is treated in parallel.

**11.** Regarding drugs used in tumour lysis syndrome:

- A. Allopurinol rapidly lowers an already elevated serum uric acid. — T / F
- B. Blood samples for uric acid in patients receiving rasburicase should be transported on ice. — T / F
- C. Allopurinol requires dose reduction of azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine. — T / F
- D. Febuxostat is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency. — T / F

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A – F, B – T, C – T, D – F.**

- A (False) – allopurinol only prevents new uric acid formation; existing urate is removed by rasburicase.
- B (True) – rasburicase continues to degrade urate ex vivo, giving falsely low results.

**12.** A 71-year-old man with metastatic prostate cancer has 3 weeks of thoracic back pain, worse lying flat, and since this morning bilateral leg weakness (power 4/5) with a sensory level at T8. He can still walk with help. What is the most appropriate immediate management?

- A. Plain thoracic spine radiographs and outpatient review
- B. Analgesia and physiotherapy; MRI within 1 week
- C. Lumbar puncture
- D. Dexamethasone 96 mg IV bolus and await neurological progression before imaging
- E. Dexamethasone 16 mg, whole-spine MRI within 24 hours and urgent spinal surgical/clinical oncology review

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**E.**

**Why E:** This is suspected MSCC with neurological deficit. NICE NG234 recommends dexamethasone 16 mg as soon as possible, whole-spine MRI as soon as possible and always within 24 h, and urgent decision on surgery (Patchell: better ambulation with surgery + RT in selected patients) or radiotherapy. He is still ambulant – the best predictor of a good outcome.

**Why not the others:**
- A – plain X-rays must not be used to exclude MSCC.
- B – 1 week is the timeline for spinal metastases **without** suspected cord compression.
- C – LP is not indicated and may worsen compression.
- D – high-dose regimens are not more effective; waiting for progression loses function.

**Ref:** NICE NG234 2023; Patchell RA, Lancet 2005. → [D4](D4.md)

**14.** A 42-year-old woman with acute promyelocytic leukaemia on day 9 of ATRA and arsenic trioxide develops fever, dyspnoea, weight gain of 5 kg, bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusions and rising creatinine; WBC 18 × 10⁹/L. Blood cultures are taken. What is the most appropriate specific treatment?

- A. Dexamethasone 10 mg IV twice daily, with temporary interruption of ATRA/arsenic if severe
- B. Increase the ATRA dose
- C. Tocilizumab 8 mg/kg
- D. Furosemide alone
- E. Leukapheresis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**A.**

**Why A:** This is differentiation syndrome (fever, infiltrates, effusions, weight gain, AKI, leucocytosis) caused by ATRA/arsenic. Dexamethasone 10 mg twice daily is started at the first suspicion, with temporary interruption of the differentiating agents in severe cases, alongside empirical antibiotics.

**Why not the others:**
- B – more ATRA worsens the syndrome.
- C – tocilizumab is for CRS after T-cell therapies.
- D – diuretics may help fluid overload but do not treat the cause.
- E – leukapheresis is avoided in APL (it may worsen coagulopathy) and is not the treatment of differentiation syndrome.

**Ref:** Azoulay E, Lancet Respir Med 2019. → [D5](D5.md)
